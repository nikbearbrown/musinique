# Spotify's Hidden Curriculum: Artists Are Being Taught to Compose for Signals

_Draft for Musinique._

<!-- HERO IMAGE PROMPT: A precise editorial image about Spotify playlist curation as an institution of attention: a musician, a curator desk, visible playlist rows, human listeners, and faint platform signal lines. No logos. No fake UI text. -->

Source notes: This draft draws on `Spotify_Curator.md` and `spotify-playlist-vetting-guide.md`, especially the distinction between playlist curation as a listening service, playlist placement as an algorithmic signal, and fake playlisting as the simulation of audience behavior.

**Searle frame:** Spotify's metrics count as instruction when artists alter composition and release strategy to satisfy them.

Spotify has a curriculum.

It is not published as a curriculum. It does not look like a syllabus. It appears as dashboards, best-practice guides, promotional tools, algorithmic rewards, and the quiet fear that the next release will disappear if the first fourteen days go badly.

But it teaches.

It teaches artists that skip rate matters. It teaches that saves matter. It teaches that completion matters. It teaches that playlist adds matter. It teaches that genre coherence matters. It teaches that a song should be legible to recommendation systems before it is allowed to become legible to a public.

Some of these lessons are useful. Artists should care whether listeners skip. Artists should care whether anyone returns. Artists should care whether a song fits the context into which it is placed. Data can puncture vanity. It can reveal that the track an artist loves is not the track listeners keep.

The danger begins when the signal becomes the teacher of the song.

At that point, composition changes category. The artist is no longer asking only what the song means, what it wants, or what tradition it enters. The artist is asking how to produce platform-recognized behavior. The music becomes an answer to the dashboard.

The Searle formula is direct: Spotify's metrics count as instruction in a context where artists change their creative acts to satisfy the metrics. This is not metaphor. A hidden curriculum is what an institution teaches without naming as instruction. Spotify teaches through reward.

The curator playbook accepts this and tries to make it useful. Build a playlist. Test positions. Rotate tracks. Watch streams per listener. Promote the context. That is practical advice. It may help artists survive. But survival advice should not be mistaken for artistic philosophy.

The hidden curriculum narrows imagination when artists forget that signals are proxies. A save is a proxy for attachment. A completion is a proxy for tolerance or interest. A repeat is a proxy for desire. A playlist add is a proxy for use. The platform sees proxies. The musician makes music for persons.

When the proxy becomes the person, the work starts to flatten.

This is especially dangerous for independent musicians because they receive the curriculum without the cushion of a large catalog, label budget, or established fan base. Every release feels like an exam. The first week becomes judgment. The algorithm becomes teacher, registrar, and rumor mill.

The correction is not to reject data. The correction is to restore the hierarchy. Data can inform the artist. It cannot define the musical act. A song that fails a metric may be flawed, mismatched, badly promoted, too early for its audience, or simply not designed for that institution.

The platform teaches artists to compose for signals. The artist has to remember that signals are not listeners. They are shadows cast by listeners when the institution is watching.

## Research Addendum (2026-06-03)

**Research date:** 2026-06-03

**Topic tags:** spotify

**Research notes:**
- Spotify-related claims should distinguish editorial pitching, algorithmic recommendation, playlist exposure, and paid/opt-in tools such as Discovery Mode. Treating them as one black box weakens the argument.
- Spotify defines artificial streaming around listening intent and manipulation, including bots/scripts and paid services that guarantee streams. For playlist-vetting pieces, the central test is whether listener behavior remains durable after exposure ends.
- Discovery Mode terms are useful because Spotify explicitly says the program does not guarantee placement, streams, new listeners, or followers. That caveat supports a careful distinction between influence, exposure, and outcome.
