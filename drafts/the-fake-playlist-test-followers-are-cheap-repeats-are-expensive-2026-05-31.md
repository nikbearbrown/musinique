# The Fake Playlist Test: Followers Are Cheap, Repeats Are Expensive

_Draft for Musinique._

Source notes: This draft draws on `Spotify_Curator.md` and `spotify-playlist-vetting-guide.md`, especially the distinction between playlist curation as a listening service, playlist placement as an algorithmic signal, and fake playlisting as the simulation of audience behavior.

**Searle frame:** A follower count counts as authority only in a context where followers behave like listeners.

The fake playlist test begins with one sentence: followers are cheap, repeats are expensive.

The music promotion economy wants artists to look at the wrong number. It shows playlist followers because followers are legible. They fit in a screenshot. They make the curator look important and the artist feel close to something. A playlist with 40,000 followers looks like a room full of people.

It may be a room. It may also be scenery.

A playlist follower counts as audience only when the follower behaves like a listener. That means listening through, saving, returning, following the artist, adding the track to a personal playlist, or producing some downstream action that cannot be reduced to a single mechanical play. Without that behavior, the follower count is a decorative institution. It confers status on the playlist without proving musical uptake.

The vetting guide gives the useful red flags. A playlist with thousands of followers and a curator with no profile followers is suspicious. A generic username, no photo, no bio, and no social presence are suspicious. Sudden follower spikes are suspicious. A stream spike without saves, followers, or social movement is suspicious. One-to-one listener-to-stream patterns can be suspicious. Absurdly high stream-to-listener ratios can also be suspicious when they do not match any plausible fan behavior.

But the simplest practical distinction is this: fake systems can buy the first act more easily than the second. They can generate a play. They can generate a follower. They can generate a number that makes the dashboard twitch. It is harder to generate the shape of preference over time.

Preference has texture. Real listeners are uneven. They skip. They return. They save some tracks and ignore others. They show geography that makes sense. They come from plausible sources. They sometimes follow the artist. They behave differently around a song they love than around a song that merely passed through the room.

Bots have to simulate all of that. Some do it badly. Some do it better. But the more human the simulation has to become, the more expensive the fraud becomes. That is why repeats matter. A repeat is not proof of authenticity, but it is a more demanding imitation than a stream. A save is more demanding still. A profile follow asks the listener to connect the song to a person or project. That is exactly the movement fake playlisting tries to bypass.

The curator playbook is useful because it points toward an honest version of the same mechanism. Build a playlist that listeners actually use. Give it a mood. Keep it coherent. Rotate tracks. Watch streams per listener. Do not worship monthly listeners. A small group of people who listen 3.83 times each is more valuable than a large group that touches the song once and disappears.

That is not just a marketing lesson. It is a philosophical one. The repeat is the listener saying, through action, "again." In music, "again" is one of the most important words there is. Children learn songs through again. Fans build attachment through again. Hymns, hooks, choruses, lullabies, protest songs, and guilty pleasures all survive because somebody wants them again.

The fake playlist sells "once" at scale. The real playlist creates the conditions for "again."

So the test is not: how many followers does the playlist have? The test is: what kind of repetition does it produce? If the answer is none, the follower count is not audience. It is costume jewelry.
