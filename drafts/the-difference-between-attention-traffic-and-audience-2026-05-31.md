# The Difference Between Attention, Traffic, and Audience

_Draft for Musinique._

Source notes: This draft draws on `Spotify_Curator.md` and `spotify-playlist-vetting-guide.md`, especially the distinction between playlist curation as a listening service, playlist placement as an algorithmic signal, and fake playlisting as the simulation of audience behavior.

**Searle frame:** Traffic counts as audience only when repeated human attention forms a standing relationship to the artist or song.

Artists need three words and they need to stop using them as if they mean the same thing.

Traffic. Attention. Audience.

Traffic is movement through a system. A song was served. A play occurred. A source generated activity. Traffic can be bought, routed, botted, misplaced, or genuinely earned. It is the broadest and weakest category.

Attention is a human act. Someone listened in a way that selected the song from the background. Attention may be brief, but it has direction. It is not merely sound passing through a device. It is a person giving the song some portion of mind.

Audience is attention that can return. It is a standing relationship, even a tiny one. A saved track. A profile follow. A listener who plays the next release. A person who recognizes the name. Audience is not guaranteed by attention, but it grows from repeated attention.

Spotify's dashboard often encourages artists to collapse these categories. Monthly listeners look like audience. Streams look like attention. Playlist placement looks like discovery. Sometimes they are. Often they are not.

The playlist vetting guide is really a guide to distinguishing these acts. Fake playlists produce traffic without audience. Weak placements produce exposure without attention. Strong placements produce human signals: saves, repeats, follows, personal playlist adds, coherent geography, and downstream listening.

The Searle formula: traffic counts as audience in the music business only when repeated human attention forms a relationship to the artist or song. Without the relationship, traffic remains traffic. It may be useful. It may create a royalty event. It may test a hook. But it is not yet audience.

This distinction protects artists from false hope and false despair. A traffic spike that disappears is not proof that the music failed. It may prove only that the source was wrong. A small number of listeners with high repeat behavior may be more meaningful than a large number with no return. The artist who understands the distinction can read the dashboard without being hypnotized by it.

The curator-first model tries to convert context into attention and attention into audience. That is the honest path. Build a playlist people use. Place music inside it where it fits. Let the listener move from utility to affection. Watch whether they come back.

The fraudulent path sells traffic as if it were audience. It takes advantage of the artist's loneliness. It knows that a rising graph feels like being heard.

But being counted is not the same as being heard.

That sentence should be taped above every Spotify for Artists dashboard. Traffic is the machine moving. Attention is the person turning toward the song. Audience is the person returning after the machine has moved on.
