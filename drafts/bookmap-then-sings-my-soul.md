# Then Sings My Soul: 150 of the World's Greatest Hymn Stories
## Robert J. Morgan | bookmap

---

## Part One: Chapter-by-Chapter Summaries

---

### The Cure for Shot Nerves *(Introduction)*

Morgan opens not with argument but with autobiography: a man depleted, walking beside a brownish pond near a freeway, singing old hymns to no one. What he discovers beside that unlikely water is the book's entire thesis compressed into a single scene. Hymns are not nostalgia. They are medicine. The chapter makes its case through accumulation — Fanny Crosby, the Scottish Psalter, Watts and Wesley — before arriving at the quiet declaration that this book exists to re-acquaint us with one hundred fifty of them. Morgan is careful to say he isn't attacking contemporary worship music. He is insisting we don't trade away something irreplaceable. The chapter earns its place by establishing the stakes: these are the songs people sing when dying, when afraid, when no words of their own will come.

---

### The Lord Bless You and Keep You *(14th century BC)*

The oldest text in the collection is not a hymn first but a benediction — Numbers 6:24-26, the priestly blessing God gave Moses. Morgan traces the stunning archaeological moment when silver scrolls from the seventh century BC were unrolled in Jerusalem's Hinnom Valley, revealing the oldest surviving copy of any biblical text. These weren't liturgical objects for congregations; they were amulets worn against the body. The theology preceded the music by centuries, but when Peter Christian Lutkin finally set those ancient words to his classical melody, something sealed. The summary earns its weight by connecting the archaeological find to the intimacy of the blessing itself: words worn close to the skin eventually became words sung in unison.

---

### Be Thou My Vision *(8th century)*

Patrick was sixteen when raiders seized him and dragged him to Ireland as a slave. He was thirty when he returned — not as a freed man seeking safety, but as an evangelist carrying a Latin Bible and a dream he couldn't shake loose. Morgan renders the Macedonian-vision moment with economy: Patrick saw an Irishman in a dream, pleading with him to come. He went. What followed — two hundred churches, a hundred thousand baptisms — is one of Christian history's most fruitful missions. The hymn itself arrived centuries later, an anonymous Irish poem from the eighth century, translated by Mary Elizabeth Byrne, versified by Eleanor Hull, set to a traditional melody named for a place in Ireland where Patrick once challenged druids. A slave's obedience, refracted through seven centuries, became a prayer still sung in the morning.

---

### All Glory, Laud, and Honor *(AD 820)*

Theodolf of Orleans wrote this Palm Sunday hymn in prison. His bishop's robes exchanged for a monastery cell, accused of conspiracy he probably didn't commit, he meditated on Christ's triumphal entry and wrote seventy-eight stanzas — thirty-nine couplets — because imprisonment gives you time. The legend that King Louis heard him singing from a window and ordered his release cannot be confirmed, but the image persists because it feels true to what hymns do: they penetrate walls. Most congregations sing only the first few stanzas today; Morgan includes the verse about the little donkey that time has mercifully retired. What remains is the recognition that praise written in unjust captivity has a particular authority.

---

### Jesus, the Very Thought of Thee *(12th century)*

Bernard of Clairvaux founded 170 monasteries, advised popes, and preached the Second Crusade — that last achievement a genuine blight on his legacy, which Morgan names without flinching. But Bernard is also the "honey-tongued doctor" whose writing about Christ's mercy made Luther call him "the best monk that ever lived." Morgan quotes him at length, letting Bernard preach across eight centuries about the mechanics of grace. The hymn attributed to him — its full authorship disputed — distills his mystical devotion to Christ into a form anyone can carry. The chapter's method here becomes a template: biography as window into theology, theology as window into why the song matters.

---

### All Creatures of Our God and King *(1225)*

Francis of Assisi preached to birds. The story may be legend, but Morgan treats it as something better than fact — as a symptom of a man so fully convinced of creation's participation in praise that he bent down and addressed a flock in a valley. Written as Francis was dying, *Cantico di Frate Sole* ("Song of Brother Sun") exhorts sun, moon, wind, fire, and creatures to worship together. The English version didn't arrive until 1919, when William Draper adapted it for a children's festival in Leeds. Morgan anchors the chapter in Psalm 148, showing that Francis wasn't eccentric — he was simply taking seriously what the psalmist had already commanded. The summary illuminates how a medieval deathbed prayer became a 20th-century children's hymn without losing anything essential.

---

### The God of Abraham Praise *(1404 and 1770)*

This is the most structurally unusual story in the collection: a hymn assembled across three centuries, two religions, and at least three authors. Maimonides wrote his thirteen credal statements in the twelfth century. Daniel ben Judah versified them into the *Yigdal* in 1404. Meyer Leon sang it in a London synagogue in 1770 while Thomas Olivers — once the worst boy known in his Welsh town, later a transformed evangelist — sat in the audience and was "so moved" he asked for the music afterward. What Olivers produced was a consciously Jewish-Christian document, annotating nearly every line with a Hebrew scripture reference. Morgan's pleasure in this story is evident. The chapter demonstrates that Christian hymnody has rarely been as purely self-generated as we imagine.

---

### A Mighty Fortress Is Our God *(1529)*

Luther was a musician who borrowed secular melodies for sacred words and occasionally returned them to the taverns when congregations protested. He believed music was the second greatest treasure after Scripture, and that anyone who thought otherwise deserved "nothing but the braying of asses." This is the most architecturally magnificent of the Reformation hymns — Luther's fortress imagery drawn from Psalm 46, the same psalm he would request when facing trial. Morgan notes that at least eighty English translations exist, then reproduces two of the lesser-known ones alongside Hedge's familiar American version, letting the reader feel the difficulty of pinning Luther's volcanic German to English without losing the craft. The chapter's implicit argument: some hymns carry their theology so heavily that translation itself is a theological act.

---

### Now Thank We All Our God *(1636)*

The context for this hymn is one of the most harrowing in the collection. Martin Rinkart buried hundreds during the Thirty Years' War — sometimes fifty in a single day — the last surviving pastor in a walled city simultaneously plagued, besieged, and starving. He personally negotiated the Swedish army's withdrawal. That this man, in those circumstances, wrote a Doxology-level hymn of thanksgiving is the chapter's entire argument, stated as biography rather than theology. Morgan lets the facts do the work. Rinkart composed it for survivors who had reason for bitterness but chose praise instead. The hymn has been sung by German congregations as their equivalent of the Doxology ever since — a national act of gratitude rooted in the memory of a pastor who refused to be extinguished.

---

### The Lord's My Shepherd *(1650)*

The Scottish Psalter was six years in the making — revision committees comparing Francis Rous's metrical translations against the original Hebrew with the same rigor applied to Bible translation itself. What emerged in 1650 was a document of exquisite care, and only this single portion — Psalm 23 set to the tune *Crimond* — escaped the bounds of Scotland to become universally beloved. Morgan closes with a small, irresistible piece of local trivia: the clockmaker in Crimond accidentally installed one six-minute section on the clock face instead of five minutes, making every hour in Crimond sixty-one minutes. The residents have always had a little extra time for singing the Lord's My Shepherd.

---

### Praise God from Whom All Blessings Flow *(1674)*

Thomas Ken wrote this Doxology not for congregational worship but for schoolboys — private hymns for their rooms, to be sung upon waking, at bedtime, and at midnight if sleep wouldn't come. All three ended with the same stanza, which became the most widely sung verse in the world. Morgan traces Ken's extraordinary life: chaplain to Charles II (who wanted to lodge a mistress in Ken's house; Ken refused), imprisoned in the Tower of London under James II, buried at sunrise with the Doxology sung at his funeral. The chapter's arc is about fidelity — a man who said no to power when it counted and yes to whatever truth required, whose private hymn for boys outlasted everything else he did.

---

### Sing Praise to God Who Reigns Above *(1675)*

Philip Spener's revival in Frankfurt is the context: small home prayer groups, expository preaching, the word *Pietist* coined as an insult that became a movement. Johann Jakob Schütz was Spener's friend and attorney, and his 1675 hymn captures the joy of watching a church come back to life. Morgan's interest here is less in the hymn than in the pattern — that institutional Christianity grows cold, that revival arrives through ordinary faithfulness, and that the people who experience it feel compelled to sing. The chapter reads as a brief history of German Pietism told through one man's testimony-in-verse.

---

### Fairest Lord Jesus *(1677)*

The origin of this hymn is genuinely mysterious — a Jesuit manuscript from 1662, published in 1677, its author unknown, its English translator anonymous for the first three stanzas. Joseph Seiss wrote the fourth stanza for an American Lutheran Sunday school book in 1873. Morgan finds something fitting in the anonymity: a hymn about the beauty of Christ that directs no attention toward its authors. He quotes John Chrysostom at length on the dual nature of Christ — human enough to be hungry, divine enough to feed five thousand — making the theological point that "Fairest Lord Jesus" is not sentimentalism but incarnation doctrine set to music.

---

### Praise to the Lord, the Almighty *(1680)*

Joachim Neander converted at twenty after attending a church service specifically to mock it. He spent his remaining decade writing hymns during walking meditations near Düsseldorf. His favorite gorge was eventually named for him: Neander Valley, *Neanderthal* in old German. Two centuries after Neander walked there singing, miners found human bones in its caves — bones that William King claimed proved evolution's missing link. The chapter arrives at an image of genuine irony: a man who wrote hymns celebrating creation has his name permanently attached to a concept he would have rejected entirely. Morgan names this without moralizing, leaving the reader to sit with it.

---

### Behold the Savior of Mankind *(1700)*

The only surviving hymn from Samuel Wesley Sr. was rescued from the fire that nearly killed his five-year-old son John. Everything else Samuel wrote — manuscript poems, compositions — burned when Epworth's citizens torched his house in one of several attacks inspired by his rigid pastoral style. Morgan frames the chapter around loss and its strange reversals: the father who hoped his hymns would secure his legacy lost them all, but one — and that one became the forerunner of the six thousand hymns his son Charles would later write. A father's ash became a son's inheritance.

---

### Behold the Glories of the Lamb *(1707)*

Isaac Watts was ugly by his own era's standards — five feet tall, oversized head, tallow skin — and Elizabeth Singer, who had fallen in love with his poetry, couldn't bring herself to marry him when she saw him. But his mother had nursed him on a stone outside his imprisoned father's door, and Watts seems to have absorbed early that the gap between worth and recognition was wide. His first hymn, written at nineteen in response to his father's challenge to improve the dismal singing at church, was also — his contemporaries believed — the first English hymn designed for congregational use. This chapter establishes the stakes: Watts is the Father of English Hymnody, and his two most prolific years were spent as a barely-out-of-school young man in Southampton.

---

### Alas! And Did My Savior Bleed *(1707)*

The secondary story here is Fanny Crosby's conversion in 1851. She was thirty-one, at a revival service in New York, when the congregation sang this Watts hymn. At the third line of the fifth stanza — *Here, Lord, I give myself away* — she later said, "my very soul was flooded with celestial light." Morgan's interest is in the long arc of influence: Watts composed this hymn as a barely-adult young man in Southampton, and decades after his death it was the instrument of another generation's most prolific hymnist's conversion. The chapter is a meditation on hymns as vessels whose contents are released in times and places the author never imagined.

---

### I Sing the Mighty Power of God *(1715)*

The controversy around Watts's hymns — whether congregations should sing human compositions at all, or only the Psalms — split churches on both sides of the Atlantic. A Kentucky minister rode horseback to the General Assembly in Philadelphia to formally protest. Watts, who had the emotional volatility Morgan describes as "quick of resentment" but practiced gentleness, was by 1712 largely bedridden from a breakdown. Sir Thomas and Lady Abney invited him for a week; he stayed thirty-six years. In 1715 he published *Divine and Moral Songs for Children*, including this hymn — an adult congregation's favorite that was written for children, selling eighty thousand copies in its first year. The chapter's implicit argument: the hymns that cross from children to adults do so because they contain something true enough to need no age restriction.

---

### Jesus Shall Reign *(1719)*

Watts's paraphrase of the Psalms — his most ambitious project — gave the church its first great missions hymn. *Jesus Shall Reign* reinterprets Psalm 72 through New Testament eyes, injecting grace and eternal life where the psalmist had spoken of sacrifices and temporal blessings. Morgan connects it to Eric Liddell, the Scottish Olympic hero later martyrized in China, who sang it before boarding his train to Edinburgh for the last time. The chapter earns its length by connecting the Psalm, the paraphrase, and the missionary trajectory into a single argument about how Watts changed what Christians thought they were singing about.

---

### O God, Our Help in Ages Past *(1719)*

The BBC played this when World War II was declared. Churchill's funeral was accompanied by it. Its origins are a man sitting at a desk on a wealthy family's estate, working through Psalm 90 — the oldest psalm, attributed to Moses — one stanza at a time. Morgan reproduces nearly all the original verses, including the ones that didn't survive to modern hymnals, allowing the reader to hear the full theological scope: God as eternal dwelling, human life as grass, time as an ever-rolling stream bearing its sons away. He adds a postscript: Psalm 98, which Watts processed into "Joy to the World" in the same 1719 collection, reminding us how much of Christmas we owe to a man who was primarily writing theology.

---

### God Is the Refuge of His Saints *(1719)*

A brief chapter built around Watts's gentler treatment of Psalm 46 — the same text Luther set as a fortress. Where Luther built battlements, Watts built sanctuary. The comparison is implicit but the chapter uses it to illuminate the difference between two men, two centuries, two temperaments working from the same scripture. Watts suffered a stroke in 1739 that left him unable to write; he dictated for several more years before dying in 1748, buried in Bunhill Fields, his six hundred hymns written mostly before he was thirty.

---

### Am I a Soldier of the Cross? *(1724)*

Watts published this hymn not in a hymn collection but in a volume of sermons, as the closing poem of a message on "Holy Fortitude." Its secondary life is what makes it chapter-worthy: the Sunday night in Indianapolis, 1870, when D. L. Moody needed a song leader and a tax collector named Ira Sankey got up on a box on a street corner and sang it to factory workers walking home. Moody accosted him afterward with questions, announced he'd been looking for him for eight years, and within twenty-four hours the most famous evangelistic duo of the nineteenth century had formed. The chapter is about how a hymn written as a sermon appendix became the occasion for a partnership that would reach millions.

---

### And Can It Be? *(1738)*

Charles Wesley was premature at birth — kept alive in wool wrap until his actual due date, when he opened his eyes and cried for the first time. The chapter traces his Oxford years, his disastrous Georgia mission (he insisted on triple-immersion infant baptism; a woman fired a gun at him), his spiritual crisis, and finally the Sunday in May 1738 when he wrote in his journal: "I now found myself at peace with God." Two days later he "began a hymn upon my conversion" — probably this one, though Morgan is careful about the attribution. The fourth verse's dungeon imagery — chains falling off, heart freed, rising and following — is either description or testimony. Either way, it is one of the most electrifying stanzas in English hymnody.

---

### O for a Thousand Tongues to Sing *(1739)*

Charles wrote this hymn on the first anniversary of his conversion — eighteen stanzas, beginning with "Glory to God and praise and love." He was inspired by a Moravian who had said, "Had I a thousand tongues, I would praise Him with them all." When John Wesley compiled his hymn collection in 1780, he placed this seventh stanza first, and there it remained: the opening hymn of Methodism's foundational songbook. Morgan moves quickly here because the biographical facts are well-trodden, but he pauses on Charles's account of his changed life: he had been in bondage to his temper and his drinking, and after conversion "I almost forgot I was ever in bondage to him." The chapter is finally about what happens when a conversion actually changes something.

---

### Hark! The Herald Angels Sing *(1739)*

Charles wrote "Hark, how all the welkin rings" — *welkin* being an archaic English word for the vault of heaven — and he was genuinely annoyed when people changed his hymns. He published a small essay warning publishers to print them exactly as written or add the correct text in the margin, noting that none of them were able to "mend either the sense or the verse." In 1753, George Whitefield changed "welkin" to "Harold Angels" and the rest is liturgical history. Morgan acknowledges both the improvement and the irony: the hymnist who most forcefully demanded fidelity to his own text has the most famous version of his most famous Christmas hymn changed by a friend.

---

### Christ the Lord Is Risen Today *(1739)*

Charles preached in fields because pulpits were closed to him, and crowds in the tens of thousands came — one observer counted more than a thousand people, "some of them fashionable but most of the lower rank." Joseph Williams's eyewitness account of Charles preaching in Bristol captures his method: convicting the congregation of sin, then compelling them toward grace with "the most forcible motives, arguments, and expostulations." The hymn he wrote for Easter — originally without the Alleluias, which were added later — represents his most exuberant mode, the preacher who couldn't stop even when the form demanded rest.

---

### Jesus, Lover of My Soul *(1740)*

No one knows exactly what prompted this hymn, but Morgan reads its imagery as anticipatory autobiography — the "nearer waters rolling," the "tempest," the midnight escapes and dangerous roads that would define Charles's itinerant years. He structures the chapter around Charles's eventual domestic settling at age forty-nine, exhausted after years of horseback ministry across England and Wales. The tenderness in the hymn — "hide me, O my Savior, hide" — belongs to a man who knew what it was to have no hiding place. Morgan closes with Charles's last dictated hymn, bedridden and unable to write: "O could I catch a smile from thee, and drop into eternity?" One method of prayer, unbroken from thirty to eighty.

---

### Hallelujah Chorus *(1741)*

Handel was bankrupt, half-paralyzed, artistically exhausted, and publicly written off — "His inspiration is exhausted," wrote Frederick the Great — when the manuscript arrived from Charles Jennens. Twenty-three days after opening it, he had the Messiah. Morgan doesn't try to explain this. He lets Handel's own account stand: "Whether I was in my body or out of my body when I wrote it, I know not." He adds the story of King George II rising during the Hallelujah Chorus — no one knows why; he may have been hard of hearing and thought it was the national anthem — and notes that from that night forward audiences everywhere have stood. The chapter is about what happens when a depleted man accepts a text he can't improve.

---

### O Come, All Ye Faithful *(1743)*

John Francis Wade was a Catholic refugee in France, earning his living as a music copyist of extraordinary skill. Seven original manuscripts of this Latin Christmas carol in his handwriting survive, all bearing his signature. Morgan traces the hymn's journey: from Wade's pen to English Catholic congregations returning to Britain, to an Anglican minister named Frederick Oakley who tried twice to translate it — first producing "Ye faithful, approach ye" (which didn't catch on) and then, after converting to Catholicism himself, arriving at "O come, all ye faithful." Two brave English Catholics, Morgan concludes, living a century apart in different countries, combined their work to bid us come "joyful and triumphant."

---

### When Morning Gilds the Skies *(1744)*

An anonymous German Catholic hymn, translated into English by Edward Caswall — himself a convert from Anglicanism to Catholicism, following Newman's lead. The original had twenty-eight double stanzas; Caswall's translation plus his additions give us the version we know. Morgan reproduces a generous selection of the omitted stanzas, letting readers feel the hymn's original scope: a liturgical clock marking every hour of the day, from waking to midnight, from work to prayer to sleep. The chapter is a quiet argument for praying in all circumstances, at all hours — a thoroughness of praise that modernity has mostly abandoned.

---

### Guide Me, O Thou Great Jehovah *(1745)*

William Williams converted after hearing Howell Harris preach while standing on a gravestone. He then spent forty-three years traveling ninety-five thousand miles on horseback across Wales, drawing crowds of ten thousand or more. Once he addressed an estimated eighty thousand. He has been called "the Watts of Wales" and wrote over eight hundred hymns. This one, drawn from Exodus imagery — the desert, the bread of heaven, the Jordan crossing — was his most famous, described here through President Garfield's deathbed, where it was sung at his wife's request as he rallied briefly at a window. Morgan notes it was also sung at Princess Diana's funeral. The chapter is about hymns that attach themselves to death and dying because they were written for exactly that — the final wilderness crossing.

---

### O Happy Day That Fixed My Choice *(1755)*

Philip Doddridge lost eighteen of nineteen siblings in infancy. He was himself apparently stillborn until he cried. He grew up studying the Bible's history on delft fireplace tiles. When he turned to ministry with no means of support, he set apart a day for prayer; the postman arrived during it with a letter from a benefactor offering to fund his training. He interpreted this as a direct answer and spent the rest of his life "praying without ceasing, even while getting washed and dressed in the morning." He published nearly four hundred hymns posthumously in 1755, including this one — a hymn of consecration whose lasting popularity came from its simple certainty: something happened on this day that cannot be undone.

---

### Come, Thou Fount of Every Blessing *(1758)*

Robert Robinson attended George Whitefield's preaching at age seventeen on a dare, drunk, intending to mock. Whitefield's exclamation — "the wrath to come, the wrath to come!" — didn't release him for nearly three years. He wrote this hymn at twenty-three, for his Pentecost sermon at a Calvinist Methodist chapel in Norfolk. Morgan chooses to close not with the familiar verses but with the last lines: "Prone to wander, Lord, I feel it, prone to leave the God I love." Robinson died suddenly at fifty-four at a Birmingham preacher's home, having spent his final years in theological drift. The ending he wrote into his own hymn became, inadvertently, his autobiography.

---

### Father, Whate'er of Earthly Bliss *(1760)*

Anne Steele's fiancé drowned the day before their wedding. She lived the rest of her life with her father in a small English parish, writing hymns and devotional material out of a chronic hip injury and accumulated grief. Her father prayed that the Lord would keep her "humble" as he sent her poems to be published. Her last words were "I know that my Redeemer liveth." The chapter is spare because the life is spare — a woman who asked for "a calm and thankful heart, from every murmur free" and received it at the cost of everything she had wanted. One of the collection's quietest chapters, and one of the most affecting.

---

### There Is a Fountain *(1772)*

William Cowper lost his mother at age six and never recovered. Morgan quotes the poem Cowper wrote upon receiving her portrait decades later with the kind of tenderness that suggests he recognizes in Cowper something about the permanent wound of early maternal loss. Cowper attempted suicide multiple times before converting to Christ in a mental asylum while reading Romans 3:25. His depressions never fully lifted — Morgan is honest about this — but they gave him, as he notes, "heightened sensitivity to the heart of God and to the needs of others." The hymn with its blood-imagery was not evangelical cliché but testimony from a man who had measured the distance between drowning and deliverance.

---

### O for a Closer Walk with God *(1772)*

Cowper wrote this during a December night in 1769, before daybreak, while his friend Mary Unwin lay near death. He fell asleep after the first two lines; the third and fourth verses arrived when he woke. He described them as "whispered to my heart." The chapter works in tandem with the previous entry — the same man, a few years later, still in the same household, still dependent on the same friendship, asking the same God for restoration of what he'd lost. The hymn's second verse — "Where is the blessedness I knew, when first I saw the Lord?" — is not a theological question but a personal one, and its honesty is what has kept it in hymnals for two and a half centuries.

---

### God Moves in a Mysterious Way *(1774)*

Cowper's final hymn before his second and permanent breakdown, this chapter follows him from the partnership with John Newton that produced the *Olney Hymns*, through his secular poetry (including the anti-slavery *Pity for Poor Africans* that contributed to Wilberforce's campaign), to his 1800 death, which Morgan reports was accompanied by a moment of sudden transfiguration: "I am not shut out of heaven after all." The chapter is elegiac, and Morgan allows it to be. He quotes from Cowper's lesser-known poem *Sometimes a Light Surprises* at length — a piece of unexpected joy written by a man whose life contained very little of it — before closing on the famous hymn's title-line. God's ways remain mysterious. Cowper's life is evidence.

---

### Rock of Ages *(1776)*

Augustus Toplady was a Calvinist who despised John Wesley with a passion that bordered on obsession, calling him "the most rancorous hater of the gospel system that ever appeared on this island" and accusing him of "satanic shamelessness." He died at thirty-eight. He wrote this hymn as an article ending — a theological slap at Wesley's Armenian position on sin and salvation. Morgan closes with the irony that Toplady's famous hymn bears a startling resemblance to language Wesley himself had published thirty years earlier: "O rock of salvation, rock struck and cleft for me." The two men, he concludes, "were not as incompatible as they thought." Whether that observation comforts or exasperates the reader says something about them.

---

### All Hail the Power of Jesus' Name *(1779)*

Edward Perronet's grandparents fled Catholic France. He traveled with the Wesleys until they fell out over Methodist policy; John Wesley then excluded his hymns from Methodist collections. He went off to pastor a small independent church in Canterbury and died there, his last words a cascade of Trinitarian glory. His hymn, published anonymously in 1779, has a missionary legend attached — E. P. Scott, faced with spears pointed at his heart in India, closing his eyes and beginning to sing, finding when he opened them that every spear had been lowered. Morgan neither confirms nor disputes the account; he includes it because it says something about what the hymn has always claimed: that this name has power over hostility.

---

### Amazing Grace *(1779)*

John Newton's mother died when he was seven. He spent the following years oscillating between boarding school and the high seas, deserting the British Navy, being flogged, contemplating murder and suicide, and sinking into what he called a life "unrivaled in fiction." The storm of March 9, 1748, when he cried out to God from a ship in extremis, became the fixed point of his life — he marked the anniversary every year until he died. Morgan is careful to note that the famous title wasn't Newton's own ("Faith's Review and Expectation") and that its text references 1 Chronicles 17:16, David's stunned prayer: "Who am I, O Lord God?" He closes with a nearly forgotten verse Newton added: "The earth shall soon dissolve like snow, the sun forbear to shine, but God who called me here below shall be forever mine." The hymn earns its longevity by telling the truth about sin before it tells the truth about grace.

---

### Though Troubles Assail Us *(1779)*

Newton's lesser-known hymn based on Genesis 22:14 — the same chapter where Abraham names the mountain "The Lord Will Provide." Newton spent fifteen years at Olney, walked daily with Cowper, preached for twenty-eight more years in London's financial district, and told a friend near the end: "My memory is nearly gone, but I remember two things: that I am a great sinner, and that Christ is a great Savior." The chapter is brief but dense — a man distilling a lifetime of grace into two clauses, then walking off the stage.

---

### How Firm a Foundation *(1787)*

Nobody knows who wrote this hymn. The author's line in John Rippon's 1787 collection simply reads "K" — probably his minister of music, Robert Keene. What Morgan finds remarkable is the structure: each stanza is drawn from a specific biblical promise, explicitly catalogued in the original title, *"Exceedingly Great and Precious Promises."* The hymn is not poetry decorated with scripture; it is scripture restructured as song. The chapter is implicitly about anonymous craftsmanship — work that outlasts its maker's name.

---

### There Is a Balm in Gilead *(c. 1800)*

The first chapter devoted entirely to the Negro Spiritual begins with honesty about its difficulty: these songs cannot be precisely dated, their authors are largely unknown, and their origins were conditions of radical oppression. Morgan is careful not to domesticate the context. Slaves sang while working — sometimes as a heave-ho mechanism while hauling timber — and sometimes, risking the lash, in torch-lit groves of nocturnal worship. What emerged was a music unlike anything written by pastors in studies: it dealt with daily pain and future hope without the luxury of theological distance. Fisk University's Jubilee Singers carried these songs to the world; Thomas A. Dorsey later formalized their influence into what we call gospel music. This chapter is the collection's pivot point from European to American hymnody.

---

### Praise the Savior, Ye Who Know Him *(1806)*

Thomas Kelly wrote 765 hymns. He was Irish, wealthy, and initially ordained in the Irish Episcopal Church, until the Archbishop of Dublin objected to his evangelical preaching and forbade him the diocese. He preached anyway. His generosity during the Dublin famine of 1847 secured his popular reputation. Morgan closes with a generous selection of the hymn's verses — including the lesser-sung lines about Christ fitting his people "for conflict" — because he wants readers to encounter the full thing, not just the familiar fragment. The chapter is an argument for the density of hymnody that gets simplified away.

---

### The Star-Spangled Banner *(1814)*

Francis Scott Key was an evangelical Christian who taught Bible classes and wrote hymns like "Lord, with glowing heart I'd praise Thee." His famous night watching the bombardment of Fort McHenry is told here with the hostage-recovery mission that put him on the British ship in the first place. Morgan includes the fourth stanza — "Blessed with victory and peace, may the heaven-rescued land praise the power that hath made and preserved us a nation" — as evidence that Key understood the national anthem as a hymn. The chapter is tactful about the distance between Key's theology and contemporary American civil religion: it names what he intended without claiming we have fulfilled it.

---

### Angels from the Realms of Glory *(1816)*

James Montgomery's parents left him at six in a Moravian settlement to sail as missionaries to Barbados. They never returned. He drifted through his youth, eventually landing at a Sheffield newspaper that got him jailed twice for his editorials. He emerged from prison a celebrity, using his fame for foreign missions and the British Bible Society. On Christmas Eve 1816, he opened his Bible to Luke 2 and wrote his Christmas poem in a single day. It appeared in his newspaper that night. His parents would have been proud. The chapter is about exile redeemed — a boy abandoned to faith eventually producing work that honors the faith that abandoned him.

---

### Silent Night *(1818)*

The organ at St. Nicholas Church in Oberndorf was broken on Christmas Eve. Father Joseph Mohr had planned music; he wrote words instead, took them to organist Franz Gruber, and asked for a simple tune for guitar. That night, December 24, 1818, they performed the carol as a duet — two men solving a small liturgical crisis, not knowing they had written the most translated carol in history. Karl Mauracher came to repair the organ and took a copy away. The Strasser family, glove-makers who used their children's singing as a marketing device at fairs, found it. It reached the king and queen. Morgan's version of the story privileges the accident — every step of the carol's dissemination was unplanned, including its creation.

---

### Jesus! I My Cross Have Taken *(1824)*

Henry Francis Lyte's father abandoned the family. His mother died. He was orphaned at nine. His school superintendent saw something in him, took him in, and paid for his education through Trinity College Dublin. Lyte won prizes for his poetry repeatedly. He was ordained at twenty-one, began ministering to a congregation in the fishing village of Lower Brixham, and spent twenty-three years there while tuberculosis slowly won. He wrote this hymn at twenty-nine, before the worst of it. "Man may trouble and distress me, 'twill but drive me to Thy breast" has the quality of a resolution adopted in advance — a decision about how to interpret suffering made before the suffering arrives, and tested until it held.

---

### In the Cross of Christ I Glory *(1825)*

John Bowring reportedly wrote this hymn after hearing about a cathedral in Macau destroyed except for its front wall — atop which a metal cross remained silhouetted against the sky. Morgan cannot confirm the story's validity but tells it anyway, distinguishing between stories that are probably true and stories that are "true" in the sense of capturing something real about the subject. The hymn's tune, Rathbun, was composed by Ithamar Conkey in a fit of post-discouragement inspiration after a rainy Sunday when no choir members showed up. He named the tune for the one soprano who did: Mrs. Beriah S. Rathbun.

---

### Holy, Holy, Holy *(1826)*

Reginald Heber died at forty-three in a pool of cool water in India, having suffered a stroke after preaching in the heat. His fifty-seven hymns were found in a trunk by his widow, who finally succeeded in publishing them posthumously. He had spent sixteen years in an obscure English parish writing every one of them — including the great missionary hymn *From Greenland's Icy Mountains* and this Trinitarian hymn based on Revelation 4. The chapter is about the relationship between obscurity and quality: Heber's best work was done in a place no one was watching, and it outlasted the parishes and diocese that had claimed his most visible years.

---

### My Faith Looks Up to Thee *(1830)*

Ray Palmer was twenty-two, exhausted from working and studying simultaneously, and alone in his rented room when he wrote this poem. He wept on finishing it, and then put it away, never intending it for anyone else. Two years later Lowell Mason asked him for hymn texts, and Palmer hesitantly opened his journal. Mason copied it down, ducked into a store to transcribe it, and that evening composed the tune *Olivet*. When they met again Mason told Palmer, "You may live many years and do many good things, but I think you will be best known to posterity as the author of 'My faith looks up to Thee.'" He was right. The chapter is about what gets written when no one is watching, and what happens when someone finally does.

---

### O for a Faith That Will Not Shrink *(1831)*

William Bathurst resigned from the Anglican ministry over disagreements with the Book of Common Prayer regarding baptism and burial rites — a principled exit from institutional comfort that left him at the family estate writing hymns nobody commissioned. He wrote two hundred of them. This one, based on Luke 17:5 ("Lord, increase our faith"), was titled *The Power of Faith* in his own manuscripts. Morgan juxtaposes it with George Müller's testimony about faith grown incrementally over twenty-six years — not faith as dramatic event but as accumulated discipline. The chapter is a brief argument against the performance of certainty and for the slow work of trust.

---

### My Country, 'Tis of Thee *(1831)*

Samuel Francis Smith was a twenty-three-year-old seminary student in a sparse room with a stack of German materials to translate and half an hour before sunset. The tune to "God Save the Queen" was in the pile; the words arrived in the same half hour. He wrote it on a scrap of paper. A friend took it to Boston's Park Street Church; it was first sung by a children's choir on July 4, 1831. Smith went on to live into his late eighties, dying suddenly at the Boston train station en route to a preaching appointment. Oliver Wendell Holmes memorialized him in verse at a Harvard class reunion. Morgan's gentle irony: America's most enduring patriotic hymn was written in thirty minutes by a man who forgot about it immediately afterward.

---

### Lead, Kindly Light *(1833)*

John Henry Newman was becalmed on the Mediterranean for weeks — exhausted, homesick, sick from Sicilian fever, spiritually undone. On June 16, 1833, alone in his cabin, he wrestled through to a kind of peace and wrote this hymn. He published it the following year under the title *"Faith Heavenly Leadings."* Twelve years later he shocked England by converting to Catholicism. He eventually became a cardinal. The hymn has been sung by Anglicans, Catholics, and Protestants with equal conviction ever since — a fact that might have pleased Newman, or might not. The chapter does not resolve this. It ends with C. H. Mackintosh's observation that God never gives guidance for two steps at a time.

---

### O Worship the King *(1833)*

Robert Grant grew up in the corridors of imperial power — son of a director of the East India Company, himself a parliamentarian who sponsored Jewish civil rights legislation, eventually knighted and appointed Governor of Bombay. He wrote this hymn in the early 1830s after reading Psalm 104 and comparing the King of kings with the majesty of British royalty. The comparison was not flattering to British royalty. Grant died in India in 1838; a medical college was built in his honor. His hymn, Morgan concludes, is his most lasting memorial — a call to worship a King before whom all human sovereignty is insufficient.

---

### The Solid Rock *(1834)*

Edward Mote was walking to work one morning when the chorus arrived: "On Christ the solid rock I stand, all other ground is sinking sand." By the end of the workday he had four verses. On Sunday he visited a dying woman with his friend Brother King, who couldn't find his hymn book. Mote offered his newly written poem. The woman received it with such evident comfort that Mote had a thousand copies printed for distribution. He eventually gave up carpentry to pastor a Baptist church for twenty-one years, resigning in 1873 with the words: "The truths I have been preaching, I am now living upon, and they'll do very well to die upon." Few chapters in the collection deliver the relationship between lived theology and dying theology as cleanly as this one.

---

### Just as I Am *(1836)*

Charlotte Elliott was embittered, chronically ill, and had spent a violent dinner party railing against God. The Swiss minister who happened to be present didn't recoil. He asked if she was tired of herself — if she was holding her hate because she had nothing else. Then he told her what the cure was, and she asked, "I would come to God just as I am?" She wrote the poem years later as a fundraiser for a school for the children of poor clergy. It sold across England. Charlotte lived to eighty-two; when she died, her family found over a thousand letters from people whose lives the hymn had changed. The chapter is about what happens when someone tells the truth about conversion — not that it requires preparation, but that it requires arriving without any.

---

### Nearer, My God, to Thee *(1840)*

Sarah Flower Adams wanted to be an actress. She played Lady Macbeth to rave reviews. Frail health redirected her to literature. Morgan cannot confirm the Titanic story — that the band played this as the ship went down — but notes that a Canadian survivor reported comfort from hearing it. He focuses instead on Sarah's sister Eliza, who composed the music for her hymns; they were close. Eliza died of tuberculosis in 1846. Sarah, who had nursed her, contracted consumption too. She died in 1848 at forty-three. The hymn she wrote for a pastor's sermon about Jacob at Bethel — a stone pillow, a dream, a ladder — became associated with death at sea. The chapter doesn't explain why. It doesn't need to.

---

### I Heard the Voice of Jesus Say *(1846)*

Horatius Bonar wrote this hymn for the children of his Edinburgh Sunday school — doodling four faces in the margin of the manuscript page while he composed. His congregation's elders twice stormed out in protest when a hymn was announced; the children never protested. Bonar and his wife lost five children in rapid succession. Then a surviving daughter returned widowed with her own five young children. "God took five from me some years ago," Bonar said, "and has given me another five to bring up for him in my old age." He preached until he was nearly eighty and requested in his final days that no biography be written. "He wanted all the glory to be Christ's alone." The chapter is about a man whose theology of suffering was tested against his own life and held.

---

### Abide with Me *(1847)*

Henry Francis Lyte returns here, now dying. He entered his pulpit with difficulty on September 4, 1847, preached his last sermon, walked along the Devonshire coast in prayer, and emerged from his room an hour later with a written copy of this hymn. Some accounts say he wrote it that afternoon; others say he found an earlier draft and completed it. Morgan thinks both are probably true. He posted the final version to his wife from Avignon, then died in Nice on November 18, 1847. The fishermen of Brixham, hearing the news, asked his son-in-law to hold a memorial service. Abide with Me was first sung on that occasion. The chapter closes a two-chapter arc on Lyte: the man who wrote about taking up the cross in his twenties died asking to be held through the dark.

---

### It Came Upon the Midnight Clear *(1849)*

Edmund Sears wrote two Christmas carols fifteen years apart that are mirror images of each other — same meter, same theme, can be sung to the same tune. The more famous, this one, contains no mention of Christ. Sears was Unitarian; his focus is entirely on the angelic plea for peace on earth. Morgan observes the date: 1849, as the United States moved toward civil war. He reproduces the stanza most hymnals omit: "Yet with the woes of sin and strife the world hath suffered long; beneath the angel-strain have rolled two thousand years of wrong." The chapter is honest about what the hymn is and isn't — not a nativity hymn, but a peace plea from a man watching his nation arm itself.

---

### In Heavenly Love Abiding *(1850)*

Anna Waring was a lifelong student of Hebrew who read the Psalms daily in their original language and devoted herself to prison ministry. Her poem "Safety in God" was published in a small 1850 volume alongside eighteen other hymns. Morgan includes one of her other less-known hymns at length — "Father, I know that all my life is portioned out for me" — as evidence of a writer whose quiet consistency produced work of comparable quality. The chapter is brief because the life was quiet; Morgan lets the poetry speak.

---

### Crown Him with Many Crowns *(1851)*

Two men wrote this hymn. Matthew Bridges, a convert to Catholicism, wrote six stanzas. Godfrey Thring, a staunch Anglican, found one of Bridges's stanzas theologically suspect and wrote six new ones. Hymnals ever since have mixed and matched the twelve resulting stanzas. Morgan lists the first lines of all twelve in sequence — a structural choice that illuminates both the hymn's richness and the oddity of its divided authorship. The two men never collaborated; their stanzas are interleaved in most hymnals without attribution. The chapter is a minor study in how communal hymns become more than their individual authors intended.

---

### Good King Wenceslas *(1854)*

Wenceslas was murdered by his pagan brother in 929, reportedly as he walked to church, having ruled Bohemia for only a few years — years in which he reduced death sentences, built churches, and carried firewood to orphans on his own shoulders. John Mason Neal was an Anglican minister who believed church architecture and music had been grotesquely modernized, established a committee to restore dilapidated church buildings, and campaigned against the ugly stoves Victorians had installed to heat medieval sanctuaries. He translated ancient Greek, Latin, and Syrian hymns, giving the church "Good Christian Men, Rejoice" and "O Come, O Come, Emmanuel." This poem was original — his tribute to a king whose concern for the poor he recognized as a form of theology.

---

### Still, Still with Thee *(1855)*

Harriet Beecher Stowe raised seven children, managed a household, taught at a college, wrote thirty books, and survived the drowning of one son, the alcoholism and disappearance of another, and the morphine addiction of a daughter. *Uncle Tom's Cabin* sold ten thousand copies its first week. She had dedicated herself to Christ at fourteen after a sermon by her father Lyman Beecher. She woke every morning at four-thirty to read and pray before the household stirred. This hymn is the testimony of that practice — meditation on Psalm 139:17-18 while watching the sunrise and listening to birds. The chapter's implicit claim is that the woman who helped end American slavery was sustained by the same early-morning discipline that produced this quiet hymn.

---

### What a Friend We Have in Jesus *(1855)*

Joseph Scriven's first fiancée drowned the night before their wedding. He emigrated from Ireland to Canada, resolved on something. Ten years later, when his mother faced a crisis, he sent her this poem. A neighbor found it beside his sickbed during his final illness and asked if he had written it. "The Lord and I did it between us," Scriven said. His second fiancée died of tuberculosis before they could marry. He spent his remaining years giving away his clothes and cutting firewood for widows. He drowned in a creek in 1896 at sixty-six — his death as ambiguous and lonely as his life. His grave is positioned so that his feet point toward those of Eliza Catherine Roche, that "at the resurrection they might arise facing one another." Morgan does not editorialize.

---

### More Love to Thee, O Christ *(1856)*

Elizabeth Prentiss wrote this poem in 1856, following the deaths of two of her children, then left it unfinished. Found it thirteen years later in a stack of old papers. Her husband insisted she finish it. She scribbled an ending in pencil. He printed copies. One landed on William Howard Doane's desk in Cincinnati. The hymn was published. Morgan quotes Elizabeth's private journal: "Out in the woods and on my bed and out driving, when I am happy and busy, and when I am sad and idle, the whisper keeps going up from more love, more love, more love." The chapter is about the hymns that are prayed before they are sung — internal petitions that become public property by accident.

---

### Stand Up, Stand Up for Jesus *(1858)*

Dudley Tyng's arm was ripped from its socket by a corn thrasher. He died four days later. His last words to his father: "Stand up for Jesus, father, and tell my brethren of the ministry to stand up for Jesus." The week before, five thousand men had gathered to hear him preach; a thousand were converted. George Duffield heard the dying words at the funeral, preached from Ephesians 6 the following Sunday, and read the poem he had written in response. The hymn editor of a publication heard it, found music, published it, and it became one of America's most popular hymns. The chapter is about the strange arithmetic of martyrdom: Dudley's dying wish, transmitted through a preacher's poem, reached more people than Dudley ever did.

---

### Eternal Father, Strong to Save *(1860)*

The Navy Hymn. Franklin Roosevelt's favorite hymn. The hymn played as John F. Kennedy's casket was carried up the Capitol steps. Morgan situates it within the nineteenth century's tradition of "Mariners' hymns" — entire sections of hymnals devoted to prayers for those at sea — noting that twenty-four such hymns appeared in one 1832 collection alone. The tune's name, *Melita*, is the island where Paul was shipwrecked in Acts 27. The hymn's author, William Whiting, is otherwise nearly unknown. The chapter is about how hymns attach themselves to national grief and remain, long after the specific grief passes, as containers for the next.

---

### Jesus Loves Me *(1860)*

Anna and Susan Warner lost their family's wealth in the panic of 1837 and moved to a Revolutionary War-era house on an island in the Hudson across from West Point. They wrote to support themselves — Anna's *Robinson Crusoe's Farmyard*, Susan's *The Wide Wide World*, and eventually a joint novel called *Say and Seal*, in which a dying boy is comforted by his Sunday school teacher singing "Jesus loves me, this I know." William Bradbury read the novel, set the words to music, added the chorus, and the hymn entered every Sunday school on earth. Susan and Anna conducted Bible classes for West Point cadets for forty years. They are the only civilians buried in the military cemetery there. The chapter is about who gets to comfort the dying — and who remembers it afterward.

---

### I Gave My Life for Thee *(1860)*

Francis Havergal was seventeen and tired when she sat down in the Düsseldorf art museum opposite Domenico Fetti's *Ecce Homo* — a portrait of the thorn-crowned Christ with the inscription beneath: "I have done this for you. What have you done for me?" The words of a hymn arrived. She wrote them, then threw them in the fire — discouraging herself. The paper fell out of the grate. Her father found it, wrote a tune, and published it. The chapter circles back to Zinzendorf's encounter with the same painting two centuries earlier — the painting that launched the Moravian missions movement — and asks, quietly, whether the sight of Christ in a museum can be the beginning of a lifetime.

---

### Battle Hymn of the Republic *(1861)*

Julia Ward Howe heard soldiers singing "John Brown's Body" at a Virginia Union Army camp and went to bed knowing she needed to write better words. She woke at 4 AM with them arriving in lines and rushed to transcribe them before they faded — "I learned to do by often scratching down verses in the darkened room when my little children were sleeping." The Atlantic Monthly published the poem in February 1862 and paid her five dollars. The chapter connects the hymn to its context: the darkest days of the Civil War, and a woman who understood that moral purpose required language equal to the moment. After September 11, 2001, the nation sang it again in the National Cathedral.

---

### He Leadeth Me *(1862)*

Joseph Gilmore was twenty-eight and newly graduated when he agreed to fill the pulpit at First Baptist Church of Philadelphia. He meant to preach on Psalm 23 — had done so three or four times before — but this time got no further than the words "He leadeth me." He stayed in the pastor's parlor after the service talking with friends, penciling the hymn on a blank page of his sermon notes, handed it to his wife and forgot about it. She sent it to a Boston religious paper. In 1865 he went to preach as a candidate at Second Baptist Church in Rochester, picked up a hymnal, and opened it to his own hymn. He had not known Bradbury had set it to music. The chapter is about the hymns we forget writing and the discovery that they have been singing without us.

---

### Revive Us Again *(1863)*

W. P. McKay's conversion is one of the collection's most structurally complete stories: his mother gave him a Bible when he left home, he sold it for a small amount, and years later — a physician now, alienated from faith — a dying patient who had bought a secondhand Bible was brought into his hospital. When the patient died, McKay found the Bible was his mother's original gift, his own name still written inside in her handwriting. He tells the story himself, and Morgan reproduces the account nearly in full. No editorial is necessary. The chapter is about the objects that outlast the people who gave them and find their way back.

---

### For the Beauty of the Earth *(1864)*

Folliot Sandford Pierpoint was twenty-nine when he walked out on a spring day, saw the Avon River and the green hills and the ocean in the distance, and wrote this poem. He intended it for Anglican communion services. Americans adopted it as a Thanksgiving hymn. Almost nothing else is known about him. He resigned from his teaching position and seems to have wandered — writing hymns, teaching occasionally, publishing poetry — until his death in 1917. Morgan pairs the hymn's pure thankfulness with Viktor Frankl's observation that concentration camp prisoners dreamed of warm baths and bread — the things we take for granted — and Emerson's remark that if the constellations appeared only once in a thousand years, we would be astonished. The chapter is about the spiritual discipline of noticing.

---

### My Jesus, I Love Thee *(1864)*

William Ralph Featherstone was sixteen when he wrote this as a poem probably celebrating his conversion. He sent it to an aunt in California; somehow it was published in a British hymnal in 1864 as an anonymous text. He died in Montreal at twenty-six. Almost nothing else is known about him. Morgan uses the chapter to push back against the assumption that wisdom requires age — quoting Isaac Watts composing at nineteen, John Milton at fifteen, and the teenage writers of several other hymns in the collection. "God can change the world through anyone, regardless of age, who will say, My Jesus, I love Thee."

---

### Shall We Gather at the River? *(1864)*

Robert Lowry was lying on a sofa in his Brooklyn apartment on a sweltering July afternoon, too depleted to move, when he began musing about the crystal river in Revelation 22. He thought about why hymnists had written so much about "the river of death" and so little about the "pure water of life." He wrote the hymn as question and answer — "Shall we gather?" / "Yes, we'll gather" — and notes that the music came with the words. Often sung at baptisms, Lowry knew it was a song about heaven. The chapter is about the hymns that arrive unbidden to exhausted people.

---

### Day by Day *(1865)*

Carolina Sandell Berg watched her father fall overboard and drown while they were traveling together by ship. She was twenty-six. She returned home alone and began processing grief through poetry — her teens poems published that year, including "Children of the Heavenly Father." Seven years later she wrote this hymn about the daily strength the Lord provides. Morgan structures the chapter as accumulation: biblical promise after biblical promise — Exodus 15:2, Deuteronomy 33:25, Nehemiah 8:10, Psalm 46:1, Isaiah 40:31, 2 Corinthians 12:9, Philippians 4:13 — as though the hymn is insufficient to contain what needs to be said, and the scriptures must amplify it.

---

### Jesus Paid It All *(1865)*

Elvira Hall's mind was wandering during a long pastoral prayer at Monument Street Methodist Church in Baltimore. She was in the choir loft, doodling on the flyleaf of her hymnal, when words began arriving. She scribbled them on the front cover. A coal merchant named John Grape found the poem, took the church organ home for safekeeping during renovations, and composed the music at his house. The hymn's straightforward theology — "sin had left a crimson stain; He washed it white as snow" — carries none of the intellectual complexity of the stories behind most hymns in this collection. Morgan uses it to tell a simple story about a woman who wasn't paying attention and then suddenly was.

---

### Now the Day Is Over *(1865)*

Sabine Baring-Gould wrote more books than any other author in the British Museum Library. He published a new novel annually. He collected folk songs across southwest England. He raised fifteen children. He restored his family estate. He wrote *Lives of the Saints*, *Curious Myths of the Middle Ages*, and *The Book of Were-Wolves: Being an Account of a Terrible Superstition*. He lived to ninety. His secret: "I simply stick to a task when I begin it." This vesper hymn, written for a single evening service in 1865, is the quietest thing about him. Morgan notes the irony that the most prodigiously productive man in the collection wrote the collection's most restful hymn.

---

### Onward, Christian Soldiers *(1865)*

Baring-Gould reappears here: the same eccentric preacher, different occasion. He needed a marching song for village children walking to a Sunday school rally, searched his hymnals, found nothing suitable, and wrote this one in a single night. "Written in great haste, and I am afraid some of the rhymes are faulty," he said. "Certainly nothing has surprised me more than its popularity." Morgan notes that several hymns in the collection were originally written for children — "I Sing the Mighty Power of God," "I Heard the Voice of Jesus Say" — and that the ones that crossed over to adults did so because something in them was truer than age.

---

### Rejoice, Ye Pure in Heart *(1865)*

Edward Plumptre needed a long processional for the Peterborough Cathedral choir festival. He had four hundred people who needed time to walk down the aisles. He wrote eleven stanzas, most of which have been lost from common use. Morgan reproduces several of the omitted verses, arguing implicitly that the hymn's joy is cumulative — that what we usually sing is a fraction of the original abundance. The chapter is brief but makes its point: liturgical practicality has shortened many hymns past the point where their full meaning can be felt.

---

### Safe in the Arms of Jesus *(1868)*

Fanny Crosby gave birth to a child whose sex she never disclosed in public. The infant died. She never spoke of it except to say, in her oral biography, that "God gave us a tender babe, and soon the angels came down and took our infant up to God." A relative who lived with her for six years could never get her to speak of it. On April 30, 1868, musician Howard Doane knocked on her door, hummed a tune, and said he had forty minutes before his train. She clapped her hands: "That says, 'Safe in the arms of Jesus.'" She went to the other room, knelt, prayed, composed the poem in thirty minutes, and dictated it before he left. Fanny considered it, among all her thousands of hymns, "in a class by itself." The chapter understands why without saying so.

---

### O Little Town of Bethlehem *(1868)*

Phillips Brooks was six feet six and three hundred pounds. His sermons came in lightning bursts because he felt he had more to say than time allowed. On Christmas Eve 1865, he attended a five-hour service at the Church of the Nativity in Bethlehem, moved by something he couldn't fully articulate. Three years later, preparing for his children's Christmas program, he wrote this hymn from that memory and handed it to his organist, Lewis Redner, saying he'd name the tune after him if it was good. Redner struggled for weeks, awoke the night before the performance with the music ringing in his mind, jotted it down, went back to sleep. Brooks kept his promise: the tune is "St. Louis," a slightly adjusted spelling of Redner's first name.

---

### The King of Love My Shepherd Is *(1868)*

Henry Williams Baker spent his career compiling *Hymns Ancient and Modern* — the standard Anglican hymn book, selling over 150 million copies — and his critics called it "Hymns, Asked for and Mutilated" for his relentless editing of other people's work. He wrote many hymns himself, including this rendition of Psalm 23, which his friend John Ellerton reported was on his lips as he died: "Perverse and foolish oft I strayed, but yet in love He sought me, and on His shoulder gently laid, and home rejoicing brought me." Morgan closes with Baker's morning hymn, a lesser-known piece of considerable beauty, as evidence that the man who shaped Anglican hymnody could also write without institutional ambition.

---

### The Ninety and Nine *(1868)*

Ira Sankey found Elizabeth Clephane's poem in a penny newspaper on a train between Glasgow and Edinburgh. He cut it out and saved it. The next day, at a Moody meeting in Edinburgh, Moody turned to Sankey at the end of a sermon on the Good Shepherd: "Have you a solo appropriate for this subject?" Sankey placed the newspaper clipping on the organ, had never seen music written for it, prayed briefly, struck A-flat, and improvised both tune and words simultaneously, note by note. Moody came to him afterward: "Sankey, where did you get that hymn?" The chapter is about improvisation as prayer — the occasion that demands something you haven't prepared.

---

### Sweet By and By *(1868)*

Sandford Bennett was filling prescriptions in his Wisconsin apothecary when his friend Joseph Webster walked in, visibly depressed. "It's no matter, it will be all right by and by," Webster said. The hymn idea arrived instantly. Bennett wrote as fast as he could while two customers stood by the stove talking. "Here is your prescription, Joe," he said, handing over the sheet. Webster read the words, picked up his violin, and played a melody. A third customer entered and declared, "I never heard that song before, but it is immortal." The hymn was finished in under thirty minutes. The chapter is one of the collection's lightest — a story about grief transformed into joy in a pharmacy.

---

### Rescue the Perishing *(1869)*

Fanny Crosby was speaking to blue-collar workers in Cincinnati when she felt an overwhelming certainty that "some mother's boy" in the room "must be rescued that night, or not at all." She mentioned it from the platform. Afterward, a young man of about eighteen came to her and asked, "Did you mean me?" Thirty-five years later, at a YMCA meeting in Lynn, Massachusetts, a man approached her after she told the story. "I was that boy," he said. "That evening you spoke, I sought and found peace." He turned and left, unable to say more. The chapter is about the difference between preaching to crowds and seeing a single person.

---

### Jesus Loves Even Me *(c. 1870)*

Philip Bliss was born in a Pennsylvania log cabin where his father led daily prayers and music was the primary entertainment. At ten he sold vegetables door-to-door and heard a piano for the first time at an elegant house. The woman playing told him to get out with his "big bare feet." He never forgot the sound. He left home at eleven to work in lumber camps, taught himself music by correspondence and mounted his horse "Old Fanny" to travel, teaching singing schools. In 1870 he was meditating on Romans 5:5 — "the love of God has been poured out in our hearts by the Holy Spirit" — and wrote this hymn for his Sunday school children. The chapter is about the music that finds those it was never meant to exclude.

---

### I Need Thee Every Hour *(1872)*

Annie Hawks was thirty-seven, a housewife with three children in Brooklyn, doing ordinary household tasks on a bright June morning in 1872. She later wrote: "Suddenly, I became so filled with the sense of nearness to the Master that, wondering how one could live without Him, either in joy or pain, these words — 'I need Thee every hour' — were ushered into my mind." She handed them to Dr. Robert Lowry, who wrote the tune and chorus at the organ in his parsinage living room that afternoon. Sixteen years later, after her husband's death, she wrote that she finally understood her own hymn. The chapter is about the hymns we write before we understand them and the grief that teaches us what they mean.

---

### Lord, Speak to Me *(1872)*

Frances Ridley Havergal's father devoted his life to improving the music of the Anglican Church and wrote over a hundred hymns. She began reading the Bible at four and writing poems at seven. Her mother's deathbed words — "nothing but the precious blood of Christ can make you clean and lovely in God's sight" — stayed with her. She wrote this hymn on April 28, 1872, as "A Worker's Prayer" — a petition not for personal blessing but for the ability to be an instrument. Morgan closes with a biographical detail that illuminates the prayer's stakes: Havergal was writing for laypeople who helped in the church but felt inadequate to the work, which is to say she was writing for nearly everyone.

---

### Blessed Assurance *(1873)*

Phoebe Knapp lived in a Brooklyn mansion, wore diamond tiaras, and entertained lavishly. Fanny Crosby, who lived in near-poverty in Manhattan's slums and worked in rescue missions, was a frequent houseguest. One day in 1873, Phoebe played a new tune. Fanny immediately clapped her hands: "Why, that says, 'Blessed assurance.'" She composed the words. The chapter includes the scene of Fanny being led onto the platform at a Moody Sankie meeting in New York, the congregation mid-verse of her own hymn, Moody rising to interrupt the singing: "Praise the Lord — here comes the authoress." Fanny took her seat in thunderous applause, "humbly thanking God for making her a blessing to so many."

---

### It Is Well with My Soul *(1873)*

Horatio Spafford lost his real estate fortune in the Chicago Fire, buried his four-year-old son from Scarlet Fever, sent his wife and four daughters across the Atlantic on the *Ville du Havre*, and received a cable when they arrived in Wales: "Saved, alone." The ship had sunk in two hours. Two hundred and twenty-six died, including all four girls. The captain of the ship carrying Spafford to join his wife called him aside in mid-Atlantic: "I believe we are now passing over the place where the *Ville du Havre* went down." Spafford went to his cabin and wrote: "When sorrows like sea-billows roll — whatever my lot, Thou hast taught me to say, it is well." Philip Bliss wrote the music; within three years he was dead with his wife in a train wreck in Ohio. The chapter does not suggest that faith makes suffering comprehensible. It suggests that it makes testimony possible.

---

### Another Year Is Dawning *(1874)*

Frances Havergal wrote this as a New Year's prayer, printed it on greeting cards, and sent it to friends. A few days later, she received a letter informing her that her American publisher had gone bankrupt in the market crash of 1873, ending her entire American literary future. She wrote to a friend: "I had built a good deal on my American prospects. Now I will be done. It's not a sigh, but only a song." The chapter is about the relationship between what we pray and what we are asked to mean it.

---

### Bringing in the Sheaves *(1874)*

Knowles Shaw wrote this gospel song and died four years later when his train derailed in Texas. His last conversation — witnessed by Elder Kirk Baxter, who survived — was about what made his evangelistic meetings effective: preaching Christ, keeping Jesus before the people, never becoming discouraged. As the train went off the track, Baxter watched Shaw save the life of a fellow passenger before being pinned by the wreckage. In nineteen years of preaching, his diary recorded 11,400 conversions. He entered heaven, Morgan concludes, "rejoicing, bringing in the sheaves."

---

### Take My Life and Let It Be *(1874)*

Frances Havergal wrote this hymn in a single sleepless night after ten people in a house were all converted or surrendered to Christ during her visit — "the last night of her visit" too excited to rest. She later used each stanza as personal inventory, eventually donating nearly fifty pieces of jewelry to the Church Missionary Society, retaining only a memorial brooch and a locket containing a portrait of a deceased niece. "I don't think I need to tell you I never packed a box with such pleasure," she wrote. The chapter is about the difference between singing "Take my silver and my gold" and meaning it.

---

### I Will Sing of My Redeemer *(1874)*

Philip Bliss was returning to Chicago from Pennsylvania with his wife Lucy when the Ashtabula bridge collapsed and the train fell seventy-five feet into a ravine. Bliss escaped through a window. The fire started. Lucy was still inside. "If I cannot save her, I will perish with her," he said, and crawled back into the burning car. Both died. He was thirty-eight. His trunk arrived in Chicago safely. In it were the words of his last hymn: "I will sing of my Redeemer and His wondrous love to me; on the cruel cross He suffered, from the curse to set me free." The chapter does not soften the circumstance. It names the hymn and lets it stand.

---

### All the Way My Savior Leads Me *(1875)*

Fanny Crosby's blindness was caused, she believed, by a man who was not a licensed physician applying hot poultices to her inflamed eyes when she was six weeks old. She forgave him, publicly and often, saying he had "unwittingly done me the greatest favor in the world." She couldn't see, but her phenomenal memory allowed her to carry a mental Bible — Pentateuch, Gospels, Proverbs, vast portions of other books. This hymn was prompted by a specific incident: she had no rent money, prayed, a stranger appeared at her door with exactly the amount needed, and disappeared. "That night she wrote the words to 'All the Way My Savior Leads Me.'" The chapter is about the gap between general theological claim and particular evidence.

---

### O the Deep, Deep Love of Jesus! *(1875)*

Samuel Trevor Francis was crossing Hungerford Bridge in London one winter night when he looked at the dark Thames water and heard "the temptation whispered to me, making an end of all this misery." He pulled back, and in that moment heard a voice asking, "You do believe in the Lord Jesus Christ?" He replied that he did. He went on to become a London merchant whose real passion was open-air preaching for the Plymouth Brethren. He traveled the world, preached for seventy-three years, and died at ninety-two. The heavy, rolling melody *Ebenezer* — nicknamed "Ton-y-Botel" (Tune in a Bottle) from the legend that it washed ashore in Wales — was composed by Thomas Williams and first appeared in an 1890 Welsh hymnal. The chapter's central image — God's love as ocean, the self submerged and surrounded — makes sense as testimony from a man who had stood on a bridge and chosen not to.

---

### Jesus, I Am Resting, Resting *(1876)*

Jean Sophia Pigot's poem became J. Hudson Taylor's favorite hymn. He would take breaks from the crushing weight of directing the China Inland Mission and sit at a small reed organ, singing it. Morgan tells the story of the September 1869 letter from fellow missionary John McCarthy that arrived while Taylor was near breakdown — the letter about abiding in Christ, not striving, looking at Him rather than trying harder. "As I read, I saw it all. I looked to Jesus, and when I saw — oh how the joy flowed." The chapter is about the difference between spiritual exertion and spiritual rest — and the discovery that one of them actually works.

---

### Like a River Glorious *(1876)*

Frances Havergal caught a severe cold while vacationing in Wales in 1876 and was told she might die. Her response was "if I am really going, it is good to be true." This is the third Havergal chapter, and Morgan uses it to trace the arc of her life toward its end: the same woman who had been unable to surrender her jewelry, unable to give up her secular concerts, unable to trust the Lord with her American publishing prospects, was by 1876 genuinely at peace with the prospect of dying at forty-two. She died three years later, her final ten minutes described by her sister as "an almost visible meeting with her King" — the closest thing in the collection to a deathbed vision.

---

### O Master, Let Me Walk with Thee *(1879)*

Washington Gladden was the father of the Social Gospel — a congregational pastor who championed labor rights, attacked political corruption, and crusaded against industrial injustice. He was also, Morgan notes without flinching, the man who advanced the liberal biblical criticism that undermined conservative theology and embraced evolution. He wrote this hymn during a period of heavy criticism, sitting alone in his church. The second stanza — addressed to "the taunting Pharisee," which Gladden's critics read as self-pity — was cut by the hymnal editor who first published it. What remained became a prayer that Christians of "all stripes" have adopted. The chapter is honest about the contradiction: a man whose theology many consider deeply flawed wrote a prayer most Christians still mean.

---

### God Be with You *(1880)*

Jeremiah Rankin wrote this as a benediction after discovering that "Goodbye" meant "God be with you." He was pastor of Washington's First Congregational Church and president of Howard University, the great African American college. Morgan tells the story of Darlene Deibler, a missionary liberated from a Japanese prison camp in 1945 after years of captivity, her husband and mentor both dead, sailing away from the island with nothing. As the boat pulled from shore, she heard Indonesian voices rising from the distance — the people who had come to faith through her mission, singing "God be with you till we meet again." Morgan lets the scene do its work without commentary.

---

### O Love That Wilt Not Let Me Go *(1882)*

George Matheson went blind. His fiancée left him because she wouldn't marry a blind man. He never married. He became one of Scotland's most celebrated preachers. On June 6, 1882 — his sister's wedding day, which sent his mind back to his own abandoned engagement — he sat alone and thought about a love that is never conditional, never withdrawn. He wrote the hymn in what he later described as the quickest composition of his life, without any process of thinking. "I had the impression of having it dictated to me by some inward voice rather than of working it out myself." The chapter is about sorrow repurposed — the wound that becomes the wound's healing.

---

### Take Time to Be Holy *(1882)*

William Longstaff was treasurer of Bethesda Free Chapel in Sunderland and heard a missionary quote, "Take time to be holy." He wrote the poem. George Stebbins composed the music. The hymn's trajectory from an English port city through a publication in New York to global circulation follows the patterns of Moody-Sankey evangelism that distributed dozens of hymns in this collection. Stebbins outlived nearly everyone in the chapter, dying at nearly one hundred in 1945 — visited in his old age in the Catskills by a young George Beverly Shea, to whom the old man listened while Shea sang loudly into his ear.

---

### 'Tis So Sweet to Trust in Jesus *(1882)*

Louisa Stead's husband drowned, possibly saving a boy from the water, possibly saving their four-year-old daughter Lillie. The accounts differ. The certainty is that a seaside picnic ended in tragedy and Louisa subsequently went to South Africa as a missionary with her daughter, writing this hymn in those early years of loss. She served fifteen years, married Robert Woodhouse, returned to America for health, then went back to Africa in 1901. She retired in 1911 and died in 1917. Her daughter Lillie married a missionary and continued the work in what is now Zimbabwe. The chapter is a story about inheritance: a faith tested by drowning, transmitted to a child, carried across a generation.

---

### How Great Thou Art *(1885)*

Carl Boberg wrote a Swedish poem in 1885 that he thought was forgotten. An English missionary named Stuart Hine found it years later in Russia and expanded it — the first verse composed during a Carpathian thunderstorm, the second near the Romanian border listening to birds, the third while witnessing mountain-dwellers coming to Christ. The final verse was written back in Britain. J. Edwin Orr heard it sung by Naga tribespeople in Assam. Tim Spencer copyrighted it. George Beverly Shea received a leaflet during the 1954 Billy Graham Crusade in Harringay Arena. In the New York Crusade of 1957, it was sung ninety-nine times, with the choir joining on the refrain. The chapter is a map of a hymn's journey from Swedish meadow to global chorus.

---

### Standing on the Promises *(1886)*

Russell Kelso Carter was an athlete, educator, rancher, preacher, and physician. His heart had been "chronically diseased for seven years, resisting the remedies of physicians." He tells his own conversion story at length — the summer of 1879, kneeling in his mother's room in Baltimore, making a consecration that "covered everything." He rose, went to Boston for prayer and anointing, returned "walking by faith and not by feeling," and resumed his college work. He was healed, he says, "by the power of God alone." Morgan reproduces Carter's account directly, letting the testimony stand without editorial apparatus. The hymn follows naturally: a man who staked everything on promises has a particular authority when he sings about them.

---

### When They Ring the Golden Bells *(1887)*

Daniel Dion de Marbelle was born in France, sailed the Arctic on whaling ships, fought in the Mexican War, played music in the Civil War, joined an opera troupe, became one of the first clowns hired by James A. Bailey's Barnum & Bailey Circus, organized his own circus, assisted Buffalo Bill, and spent his last years in poverty in an abandoned schoolroom, singing in the choir at a Methodist church in Elgin, Illinois. The chapter is a portrait of a life in motion — the clown who had time for everyone except God, until God took everything and slowed him down enough to talk. Morgan closes with de Marbelle's own testimony from the Elgin choir. The hymn, written during his last years, is about the home beyond the river. He earned the longing.

---

### Leaning on the Everlasting Arms *(1887)*

Anthony Showalter was teaching a singing school in Alabama when two letters arrived from former pupils, both announcing the death of their wives. He opened the Bible, found Deuteronomy 33:27, and wrote a chorus: "Leaning, leaning, safe and secure from all alarms." He sent it to Elisha Hoffman saying he couldn't write verses. Hoffman wrote three stanzas, sent them back, and Showalter set the whole thing to music. Morgan closes with Spurgeon: "There are seasons when we sink quite low, dear child of God. Even when you are at your lowest, underneath are the everlasting arms." And Matthew Henry: "Even when they are pressed above measure, everlasting arms with which believers have been wonderfully sustained and kept cheerful in the worst of times." Two theologians and two bereaved young men, producing a hymn by correspondence.

---

### Trust and Obey *(1887)*

A young man at a Moody meeting in Brockton, Massachusetts rose during testimony to say, "I am not quite sure, but I am going to trust and I am going to obey." Daniel Towner jotted the sentence down and sent it with a note to Reverend J. H. Sammis, who wrote a poem. Towner composed the music, grew discouraged, crumpled the manuscript, and threw it in the wastebasket. His wife, straining his office the next morning, found it and left it on the organ: "I feel the melody you have written is just what is needed." She was right. In 1893 Towner became head of music at Moody Bible Institute. He wrote the melodies of "At Calvary," "My Anchor Holds," and "Grace Greater Than All Our Sins." He died leading singing in a revival in Missouri at age seventy.

---

### Sunshine in My Soul *(1887)*

Eliza Edmunds Hewitt was struck by an unruly student who slammed his slate across her back, injuring her spine. She was placed in a heavy cast for six months. When the cast was removed, her doctor told her to walk in Fairmount Park on a warm spring day. She came home and wrote this hymn immediately. Her injuries prevented her return to teaching; she devoted the rest of her life to Bible study and hymn writing, producing "My Faith Has Found a Resting Place," "Will There Be Any Stars in My Crown," and "When We All Get to Heaven." The chapter is about the hymns that arrive in recovery — the body's first steps after confinement meeting the soul's articulation of release.

---

### Yesterday, Today, Forever *(1890)*

A.W. Tozer's critique of A.B. Simpson's hymns is reproduced almost in full: Simpson's compositions are not great hymns by any measurable standard. They lack literary excellence, compactness, universality, and dignified music. "On none of these counts could Mr. Simpson's compositions qualify." And then: "After saying all this, I would yet confess that hardly a day goes by that I do not kneel and sing in a shaky baritone comfortably off-key, the songs of Simpson." Tozer's conclusion is the chapter's: Simpson "put into a few of his songs more of awful longing, of tender love, of radiant trust, of hope and worship and triumph, than can be found in all the popular gospel songs of the last hundred years put together." The chapter is about what great hymnody is actually for.

---

### My Faith Has Found a Resting Place *(1891)*

Lidie Hearne (writing as "Lidie H. Edmunds," a pen name for Alicia Edmunds Hewitt) wrote this hymn; William J. Kirkpatrick composed the music. Kirkpatrick's biography is what drives the chapter: born in Pennsylvania in 1838, his father his first music teacher, editor of his first hymn book at twenty-one, and composer of melodies for "Coming Home," "He Hideth My Soul," "Jesus Saves," and dozens more. He died working on a hymn at his desk. His wife found the study light on, called to him twice, received no answer, and found him slumped over his final composition. Its last words: "Just as thou wilt, Lord, this is my cry; just as thou wilt, to live or die."

---

### America the Beautiful *(1893)*

Katharine Lee Bates was tired from teaching summer school in Colorado when her group hired a wagon to Pike's Peak, then mules for the final stretch. She was exhausted when she reached the summit. "When I saw the view I felt great joy," she wrote, "all the wonder of America seemed displayed there." The opening lines of the hymn arrived while standing on a 14,000-foot peak looking at a continent. She wrote the four stanzas in a notebook, put it away, and didn't copy it out until two summers later. She revised it again nine years after that. The melody *Materna* was reportedly composed by Samuel Ward while crossing New York Harbor after a day at Coney Island, the notes scribbled on his shirt cuff. The chapter is about the hymns that require the right elevation.

---

### When the Roll Is Called Up Yonder *(1893)*

James Black was calling roll for a youth meeting. One name didn't answer: Bessie, the daughter of an alcoholic. Crushed by her absence, he said, "O God, when my own name is called up yonder, may I be there to respond." Opening the gate at his house afterward, the thought became a hymn. Words and music arrived together. Morgan tells the secondary story: Japanese concentration camp, 1942, a boy named Brian Thompson grabs an electrified wire. He is electrocuted. At roll call that night, when his name is called, there is no answer. The schoolchildren sing the hymn at his funeral. "How important it was for us to sing — and know — 'When the roll is called up yonder, I'll be there.'"

---

### I Must Tell Jesus *(1894)*

Elisha Hoffman was making pastoral calls in Lebanon, Pennsylvania, when a destitute woman took his hand, crying, "What shall I do? Oh, what shall I do?" He told her she should take her sorrows to Jesus. "You must tell Jesus." Her face lit up: "Yes! I must tell Jesus." He heard the words as he walked home. He drew out his pen. Hoffman lived to ninety, writing over two thousand hymns — including "Are You Washed in the Blood," "Down at the Cross," and "Leaning on the Everlasting Arms." The chapter is about the pastoral encounter that does not stay contained to the one who had it.

---

### At Calvary *(1895)*

William Newell was a prodigal reclaimed by Moody Bible Institute — his broken father writing to R. A. Torrey, who reluctantly agreed to admit the boy, who then surprised everyone. In 1895, William began composing his testimony in verse. The idea rolled in the back of his mind for weeks. One day the lines arrived; he ducked into an empty classroom with an envelope. He handed the verses to Daniel Towner in the hallway: they could use a good melody. By the time he finished his lecture, the tune was ready. Towner told him it might be the best song either of them would ever write. "Mercy there was great, and grace was free; pardon there was multiplied to me." The chapter is about the hymns that require a life's worth of material to write.

---

### Jesus Loves the Little Children *(c. 1895)*

"Tramp, Tramp, Tramp, the Boys Are Marching" was a Union Civil War ballad by George Frederick Root, written under the pen name "Wurzel" because Root was embarrassed to be writing simple martial music. After the war the melody remained popular but the words were dated. A minister named Clare Herbert Woolston wrote new verses and a chorus. The civil war prisoner became the children of the world. "Red and yellow, black and white, they are precious in His sight" replaced the soldier waiting for liberation. The chapter is a minor case study in how music survives its original occasion and waits for better words.

---

### I Surrender All *(1896)*

Judson Wheeler Van DeVenter spent five years struggling with the decision to leave teaching and enter full-time music evangelism. Friends urged him; he resisted. Finally, on his knees, he said, "Lord, if you want me to give my full time to Thy work, I'll do it. I surrender all to Thee." He wrote the hymn years later while staying in the Sebring home in East Palestine, Ohio, recalling that moment. He eventually taught hymnody at Florida Bible Institute in Tampa. In the 1930s, a wide-eyed student sat listening to the old evangelist. That student was Billy Graham, who later wrote that Van DeVenter was "a kind, deeply spiritual gentleman" and one of the evangelists who most influenced his early preaching. The chapter closes a circle across generations.

---

### Under His Wings *(1896)*

William Ockler Cushing spent twenty years as a Christian pastor before the death of his wife and a severe voice problem drove him out of ministry. "In near despair he asked God to give him something to do for the kingdom." The answer was hymn writing. He produced over three hundred hymns — including "Ring the Bells of Heaven," "When He Cometh," and "Down in the Valley." This one weaves together Ruth and Boaz, three Davidic Psalms, and Jesus's lament over Jerusalem into a single image: the warmth of the feather curtain of God. The chapter is about the second acts that begin when the first act ends in apparent failure.

---

### Count Your Blessings *(1897)*

Johnson Oatman Jr. averaged two hundred hymns a year for thirty years, producing five thousand total, including "Higher Ground," "No, Not One," and "The Last Mile of the Way." His father had "the best singing voice in the East." Oatman Jr. became a Methodist minister but never entered full-time pastoral ministry, enjoying the business world and preaching without cost. He wrote this hymn for a young people's songbook. Morgan juxtaposes it with Martin Luther's observation that "the greater God's gifts and works, the less they are regarded," and Emerson's observation about constellations. The chapter is about the intentionality gratitude requires — not the gratitude that arrives unbidden, but the kind that must be practiced.

---

### Face to Face *(1898)*

Grant Colfax Tullar was named for the president and vice president serving when he was born in 1869. A jar of nearly empty jelly — "So this is all for me, is it?" — prompted a hymn about "all for me the Savior suffered." He composed words and music on the spot at the parsonage piano. The next morning a packet of poems arrived from Mrs. Frank Breck in Portland, Oregon, including "Face to face with Christ my Savior." The words fit his melody exactly. He discarded his own poem and published hers. Mrs. Breck was a homemaker with five children who wrote hymns "over an amending basket with a baby on my arm, and sometimes when sweeping or washing dishes," recording them in a notebook while resting in her rocking chair. The chapter is about what gets written in the margins of domestic life.

---

### When We All Get to Heaven *(1898)*

Eliza Edmunds Hewitt returns for her final chapter — the same woman who was injured by a student's slate, who wrote "Sunshine in My Soul" during convalescence, who produced "My Faith Has Found a Resting Place" under a pseudonym. This time the chapter is organized around John 14: "Let not your heart be troubled. In my Father's house are many mansions." Morgan includes her nearly forgotten hymn "The Everlasting Hymn," in which she imagines heavenly worship building toward "Holy, holy, holy." The chapter is an argument for the breadth of one overlooked life — a Sunday school teacher whose contributions to American hymnody span several chapters and multiple pseudonyms.

---

### There Is Power in the Blood *(1899)*

Lewis Jones wrote both words and music during a camp meeting in Maryland. He graduated from Moody Bible Institute and worked for the YMCA. Morgan tells the story of missionary Dick Hillis in a Chinese village, a demon-possessed man, an extended prayer in the name of Jesus, and the moment the dog in the doorway began shrieking in circles and dropped dead. "Now continued Kong... Suddenly the old dog in the doorway vaulted into the air, screeching, yelping, whirling in circles, snapping wildly at his tail. Then dropped dead." The chapter asks nothing of the reader except to sit with the account. Some hymns get tested in ways their authors never imagined.

---

### O, That Will Be Glory *(1900)*

Charles H. Gabriel was one of the most prolific gospel song writers of his era. His mother told him she would rather he write a song that helped somebody than become president of the United States. He wrote this hymn in 1900, inspired by his friend Ed Card, director of the Sunshine Rescue Mission in St. Louis, who punctuated his sermons with the word "glory" and ended his prayers with "and that will be glory for me." Music director Charles Alexander saw the song and said to himself, "Ed Mann has wasted a page — I do not believe that song will be sung much." Several months later he heard it at a convention and couldn't stop thinking about it. He made it the theme song of his Australian campaigns. It became one of the most popular gospel songs of its era. The chapter is about the songs that sneak past your initial judgment.

---

### Does Jesus Care? *(1901)*

Frank Graff was known as the "Sunshine Minister" of the Philadelphia area. Then a series of heartbreaks dismantled his cheerfulness. He collapsed into depression. In the unfamiliar valley, he began singing Scriven's "What a Friend We Have in Jesus" and found 1 Peter 5:7 taking hold: "Casting all your care upon Him, for He cares for you." Out of that experience he wrote a hymn built around commonly asked questions: "Does Jesus care when my heart is pained, too deeply for mirth or song?" The answer — "Oh yes, He cares, I know He cares" — carries the weight of a man who was not naturally inclined toward doubt and found himself there anyway.

---

### This Is My Father's World *(1901)*

Maltbie Babcock told his secretary, "I'm going out to see my Father's world," whenever he went hiking from his Lockport, New York church. He wrote sixteen stanzas, each beginning with those words. He eventually went to the Holy Land as a gift from his New York congregation — and died of fever in Naples on the way home, at forty-two. His wife published his writings posthumously in 1901. The chapter uses Ralph Waldo Emerson and the particular quality of spring light to establish what Babcock was responding to, then closes with his own poem of exhortation: "Be strong. We are not here to play, to dream, to drift." A man who died before he reached Jerusalem left behind a hymn about belonging to the world's Creator.

---

### Near to the Heart of God *(1903)*

The two daughters of Howard and Lucy McAfee died of diphtheria within twenty-four hours of each other. His brother Cleveland, chaplain at Park College, "sat long and late, thinking of what could be said in word and song." He wrote the hymn. At Saturday night choir rehearsal, the choir learned it. Then they walked to Howard McAfee's home and stood under the sky outside the darkened quarantine house and sang it. The next morning it was sung at communion. Morgan's restraint in this chapter is the chapter. The hymn was written for the people who needed it most, by someone inside the grief, the night before they were asked to stand outside a house where nothing could be done.

---

### God Will Take Care of You *(1904)*

Civilla Martin was sick and unable to travel with her husband Walter to his preaching appointment. Their young child said, "Oh, daddy, you don't have to stay home — God will take care of us." Walter went. When he returned, Civilla had written the words. He composed the music at the little organ. It was first published in the songbook he was compiling for the Practical Bible Training School. Morgan introduces J.C. Penney — who nearly lost his mind in the Depression, was hospitalized, heard this hymn through the wall of the hospital chapel, walked in, and experienced what he called "the most dramatic and glorious 20 minutes of my life." He was never plagued with worry again. The chapter is about what gets heard through walls.

---

### His Eye Is on the Sparrow *(1905)*

Civilla Martin returns for a second chapter. She and her husband were visiting a couple named Doolittle in Elmira, New York — both bedridden, both chronic invalids, both inexplicably cheerful. When Walter asked their secret, Mrs. Doolittle replied simply, "His eye is on the sparrow, and I know He watches me." Civilla went home and wrote the poem, mailing it the next day to Charles Gabriel. The chapter closes with Jesus himself — a woodworker who apparently watched birds closely enough to preach about them: not one falls to the ground apart from your Father's will, He said. The chapter is about the observation that enables the sermon that enables the hymn.

---

### Have Thine Own Way, Lord *(1907)*

Adelaide Pollard was in her forties, had failed to raise support for a missionary call to Africa, and attended a prayer meeting where an elderly woman prayed, "It doesn't matter what you bring into our lives, Lord. Just have your own way with us." That night she read Jeremiah 18 — the potter and the clay — and wrote the hymn before she slept. She eventually did reach Africa, was driven out by World War I, spent years in Scotland and America writing and speaking. At seventy-two she collapsed at Penn Station in New York, waiting for a train to a speaking engagement. The chapter is about the prayer that prepares us for what we cannot know is coming.

---

### Joyful, Joyful, We Adore Thee *(1907)*

Henry Van Dyke was ambassador to the Netherlands, lieutenant commander in the Navy chaplain's corps, president of the National Institute of Arts and Letters, a Princeton professor, the author of *The Other Wise Man*, and moderator of the Presbyterian General Assembly. He handed the college president a piece of paper at breakfast during his preaching visit to Williams College in 1907: "Here is a hymn for you. Your mountains were my inspiration. It must be sung to the music of Beethoven's Hymn of Joy." The chapter is the collection's most formally distinguished biography — a man whose credentials could fill a page choosing, finally, to write a hymn. The hymn must be sung to music it did not create, fitted to a joy larger than itself.

---

### He Keeps Me Singing *(1910)*

Luther Bridgers's wife and three sons died in a house fire in Kentucky while he was away preaching. He nearly didn't survive the grief. Several months later, working through depression with the help of Job's "songs in the night," he wrote both words and music for this hymn. The fourth verse includes the line "sometimes He leads through waters deep" — and if you know the story, you know what water he means. He married again in 1914, served as a Methodist evangelist for eighteen years, and retired to Georgia in 1945. He died in Atlanta in 1948. The chapter is brief because the hymn says what needs to be said.

---

### Our Great Savior *(1910)*

J. Wilbur Chapman's Sunday school teacher Mrs. C. C. Binkley leaned around the other boys during a testimony meeting, looked directly at the hesitant fourteen-year-old, and said, "Would it not be best for you to rise?" When he still hesitated, "she put her hand under my elbow and lifted me up just a little bit." He stood. He later became one of the era's most famous evangelists, circling the globe with Charles Alexander. In 1910 he wrote this hymn celebrating the multiple roles Christ plays in a believer's life — friend for sinners, strength in weakness, help in sorrow, guide and keeper. It's set to the Welsh tune *Hyfrydol* — "good cheer" — composed in 1830 by a choir director who named none of his tunes after himself.

---

### In the Garden *(1912)*

C. Austin Miles was a pharmacist who became a hymnal editor. His hobby was photography; he used his dark room for Bible study. In March 1912, waiting for film to develop, he opened John 20 and then — he later said — began to see it: Mary at the tomb, John and Peter coming and going, Mary turning, Jesus standing. "I knew it was He. She knelt before Him, with arms outspread and looking into His face, cried, 'Rabboni.'" He woke from the vision with his Bible open, his muscles tense. He wrote the words immediately. He wrote the music that evening. The chapter is about the devotional imagination as a mode of composition — the reader who walks inside the text and doesn't come back the same.

---

### Brighten the Corner Where You Are *(1913)*

Ina Duley Ogden received an invitation to the Chautauqua Circuit — the nationwide network of speakers and performers that Theodore Roosevelt called "the most American thing in America." She was packing to go when her father was seriously injured in an automobile accident. She canceled her tour. She made up her mind to be a blessing wherever she was — to her father, to those around him. She wrote the poem. Homer Rodeheaver, song director of Billy Sunday's campaigns, made it the opening of every Billy Sunday meeting for twenty-two years. The chapter closes with a quiet reversal: instead of speaking to thousands, Ina Duley Ogden has ministered to millions. The smaller calling contained the larger one all along.

---

### The Old Rugged Cross *(1913)*

George Bennard was not a natural songwriter. He was a preacher who carried the words of this hymn for months — through meetings across the Midwest, polishing them, singing them, watching audiences respond. "I began to understand the meaning of the cross," he said. "The Christ of the Cross became more than a symbol. It was like seeing John 3:16 leave the printed page, take form, and act out the meaning of redemption." George Beverly Shea remembered him as an old man at a Bible conference in Indiana — slight, short, with long white hair, not a trained voice but "great feeling and expression." When he sang his own hymn, people were moved. The chapter is about what it costs to sing something you've spent a lifetime understanding.

---

### The Love of God *(1917)*

Frederick Lehman was moving thirty tons of lemons and oranges a day at a Pasadena packing factory in 1917, his finances having failed. He arrived one morning with a hymn forming, sat on an empty lemon crate during breaks, and penciled words with a stubby pencil. He had a melody and two stanzas but needed a third. He remembered lines from a sermon: "Could we with ink the ocean fill, and were the skies of parchment made..." He traced them — through legend to an asylum wall to their actual origin: an eleventh-century Jewish poem by Meyer Ben Isaac Nehorai. A failed Christian businessman, a German packing factory, and an eleven-century Jewish poet produced the third stanza of a hymn about the inexhaustible love of God.

---

### Wonderful Grace of Jesus *(1918)*

Haldor Lillenas bought a five-dollar organ. He wrote this hymn on it. He then sold the hymn for five dollars — just enough to pay for the organ. He went on to organize Lillenas Publishing Company, which was eventually purchased by the Nazarene Publishing House and became one of the world's largest church music publishers, owning more than twenty thousand song copyrights. The hymn that paid for the organ now anchors the catalog that owns the rights to twenty thousand more. The chapter is about the invisible economics of sacred music — and the songwriter who couldn't have predicted any of it.

---

### I'd Rather Have Jesus *(1922)*

George Beverly Shea was twenty-one, working for Mutual Insurance Company, and had just sung on a national radio talent show — losing the contest to a yodler but receiving fifteen dollars and a taste of fame. On a Sunday shortly afterward, he sat down at his mother's organ to practice for church. His eyes fell on a clipping she had left for him: a poem by Mrs. Ray F. Miller. As he read, a melody composed itself. He played it for his mother, who wrapped her arms around him. He sang it in church that morning for the first time. It became his signature song for seventy years. The chapter is about the clipping left on a keyboard — the thing placed where someone will find it who needs to find it.

---

### Turn Your Eyes upon Jesus *(1922)*

Helen Howarth Lemmel was born in England to a Wesleyan minister, studied vocal music in Germany, married a wealthy European who left her when she went blind. She was fifty-five when she heard the statement that became the hymn: "So then turn your eyes upon Him, look full into His face, and you will find that the things of earth will acquire a strange new dimness." She later recalled: "I stood still, and singing in my soul and spirit was the chorus, with not one conscious moment of putting word to word to make rhyme." She lived in near-destitution in Seattle, composing hymns on a small plastic keyboard by her bed, calling Doug Goins's father at all hours to transcribe them before she forgot. She said once, "God is going to bless me with a great heavenly keyboard — I can hardly wait." She died thirteen days before her ninety-eighth birthday.

---

### Great Is Thy Faithfulness *(1923)*

Thomas Chisholm was born in a log cabin in Kentucky, taught school at sixteen despite the poverty of his own education, and spent decades alternating between illness, evangelism, journalism, and insurance. Through all of it he discovered what Lamentations 3 called "new every morning." He wrote this hymn while serving in Vineland, New Jersey, sending it to musician William Runyon, who prayed earnestly over the composition. It was published in 1923 in Runyon's private pamphlets and moved slowly. Then Will Houghton at Moody Bible Institute began calling for it in chapel: "Well, I think we shall have to sing 'Great Is Thy Faithfulness.'" It became the institute's unofficial theme. George Beverly Shea and the Billy Graham Crusade choirs carried it to the world. Thomas lived in a Methodist home for the aged in Ocean Park, New Jersey, walking by the ocean, dropping in at the diner, fellowshiping at conferences. He died there in 1960, having written 1200 poems and hymns. The chapter is about the long, unglamorous faithfulness that enables a hymn about faithfulness.

---

### Little Is Much When God Is in It *(c. 1924)*

Fred Suffield's pounding on the door during a blizzard led to a stranded passenger's gratitude, which led to correspondence, which led to courtship, which led to marriage. He and Kitty Suffield became evangelists. They invited George Beverly Shea — a teenager — to spend a month in Westport, Ontario, holding meetings. His voice cracked on the high notes. He sat down, mortified, vowing never to sing again. Kitty told him to sing in a lower key. He did, and never stopped. In June 2000, at a Billy Graham Nashville mission reception, Bev Shea rose at ninety-two and sang the Suffields' hymn with a voice still carrying: "Little is much when God is in it." The chapter is about the small correction that redirects a life.

---

### Precious Lord, Take My Hand *(1932)*

Thomas Andrew Dorsey — not the bandleader — was the son of a Georgia revivalist preacher. He played barrel house piano in a Chicago speakeasy under the name "Georgia Tom." He converted, began writing gospel songs, and for three years sent out five hundred copies of "If You See My Savior" without receiving a single order. He kept writing. In August 1932, a telegram found him in St. Louis: "Your wife just died." He reached home to learn his baby son had also died. "I began to feel that God had done me an injustice," he said. "I didn't want to serve Him anymore." The following Saturday, alone in a friend's music room, he felt a sudden calm. His fingers moved over the keys. "Precious Lord, take my hand. Lead me on, help me stand. I am tired, I am weak, I am worn." The chapter is about the prayer composed at the bottom of everything.

---

### He Lives *(1932)*

A Jewish man said to Alfred Henry Ackley, "Why should I worship a dead Jew?" That same morning, Ackley heard a liberal preacher on the radio say that Christ's bodily resurrection was irrelevant, that his body could be dust in a Palestinian tomb, and that "the main thing is, His truth goes marching on." Ackley wanted to throw the radio across the room. He preached with unusual vigor twice that day. His wife, exhausted by his agitation, told him: "It's time you did that which you can do best. Why don't you write a song about it?" He opened his Bible to Mark's resurrection account. "A thrill went through him." Twenty minutes later he was at the piano. The chapter is about the sermon that doesn't fit into a sermon and becomes a song instead.

---

### Beyond the Sunset *(1936)*

A group of musicians on Rainbow Point at Winona Lake watched a magnificent sunset. One of them, a blind man named Horace Burr, exclaimed, "My, that sure is a wonderful sunset. I would have missed a lot if you folks hadn't been here to describe it." When someone commented on his cheer about not seeing it, he said: "I can see. I see through other people's eyes. And I think I often see more. I see beyond the sunset." The conversation turned to heaven. Virgil and Blanche Brock returned to their lodgings, sat at an old piano, and began putting together words and music. "To us it seemed as if a bright light of truth had streamed into our hearts," Virgil said. They dedicated the hymn to Horace Burr, who saw farther than anyone there.

---

### Search Me, O God *(1936)*

J. Edwin Orr had studied revivals more comprehensively than anyone alive. A student at Columbia International University asked him: "Besides praying for revival, what can I do to help bring it about?" "Without a moment's pause, he glanced in my direction and gave me an answer I've never forgotten: You can let it begin with you." Orr had written the hymn years before during a revival in Ngaruawahia, New Zealand, hearing aboriginal girls sing a Polynesian farewell melody and fitting the words of Psalm 139 to it. He jotted them on an envelope in the post office. Morgan closes with Jeremiah 17:9: "the heart is deceitful above all things." The chapter is a warning about the prayer it describes. Dangerous to offer, Orr suggests. Dangerous in the best possible way.

---

### It Is No Secret *(1949)*

Stuart Hamblin was a hard-drinking cowboy film star with a popular radio program. Billy Graham came to Los Angeles for a three-week crusade and they took a liking to each other. On the last night, at 4:30 AM, Hamblin called the hotel weeping. Graham woke his wife and friends to pray while Stuart and Susie drove over. That night Stuart gave his heart to Christ. The next morning he told his radio audience. The newspapers picked it up. The resulting publicity launched Graham's half-century of mass evangelism. Shortly after, Hamblin met John Wayne on a street. "What's this I hear about you, Stuart?" "Well, do — it's no secret what God can do." "Sounds like a song," said Wayne. Hamblin went home and wrote it. The chapter is about the conversation that becomes the lyric.

---

### His Name Is Wonderful *(1959)*

Audrey Mieir was playing the organ at a small church in Duarte, California on a Christmas Sunday. The choir loft had been converted to a manger scene. Pine boughs filled the air with fragrance. The pastor rose and said, "His name shall be called, wonderful." Audrey later wrote: "I tell you the truth, that's all it took. I wrote the words and music in the flyleaf of my Bible." She taught it that evening to the youth group. A friend told her the song needed a bridge. She ordered a hamburger at lunch, opened her Bible, listed names of Jesus on a napkin, and went back to the office piano: "He's the great Shepherd, the Rock of all Ages, Almighty God is He." The chapter is about the hymns written in the margins of church bulletins, on napkins, in Bible flyleaves — everywhere except at a desk, with time.

---

### The All-Sufficient King *(2002)*

Robert Morgan himself, the book's author, is the subject of the collection's final chapter. He had long wanted to preach on the Tabernacle and finally found the structure for it. At a conference at the University of Edinburgh, unable to sleep, he clicked on the desk lamp and visualized himself walking the Tabernacle complex — through the gate representing Christ, past the bronze laver, into the holy place — and arriving at the most holy place, the ark of the covenant, the earthly footstool of God's throne. "I was overwhelmed with the awesome privilege of coming boldly right to the throne of the Almighty." The words came quickly. He closes with an invitation: you can be a hymn-writer. As you study the Bible, compose your devotions in verse. "The world's best hymns, after all, have yet to be written."

## Bridge: Between the Stories and the Essay

What emerges from these one hundred fifty chapters is not simply a history of Christian music but a history of crisis. Almost every hymn in this collection was written in response to something: bereavement, imprisonment, shipwreck, blindness, depression, failure, fire. The question the book keeps raising — what sustains people when everything fails — never quite reaches a systematic answer. What it reaches instead is testimony: particular voices, in particular circumstances, finding words that outlasted them. What follows is less a conventional review than an attempt to sit with that pattern, to ask what it means that the songs we trust most were born in the dark.

## Part Two: The Literary Review Essay

---

# The Ark of the Ordinary: Robert J. Morgan's *Then Sings My Soul* and the Theology of Catastrophe

**Songs from the Ground Floor**

There is a peculiar comfort in discovering that "Amazing Grace" was written by a man who had been flogged, had contemplated murder, and was convinced he was beyond redemption. We want our hymns pure — ascending from spiritual height to spiritual height, crystallized in contemplative peace. What Robert J. Morgan demonstrates across one hundred fifty stories is that we have been wrong about this from the start. The hymns we trust most were not written on mountaintops. They were written in prisons, beside deathbeds, in lemon-crate breaks during factory shifts, on the backs of envelopes in post offices during unexpected revivals, in the margin of a Bible at a small Christmas service in a suburb of Los Angeles. They were written by people for whom singing was not a luxury but a lifeline — the last available act of the will when all other acts had been stripped away.

Morgan's project sounds, on the surface, devotional and retrospective: one hundred fifty stories about where familiar hymns came from. What the book actually is, read entire, is a sustained argument about the relationship between suffering and song. Not the argument that suffering ennobles (it often doesn't), not the argument that faith eliminates pain (it plainly doesn't), but the quieter and harder argument that some forms of truth can only be spoken by people who have been forced to the edge of what they can endure.

**The Recurring Pattern**

The first thing you notice, moving through these chapters, is how often the biographical facts resist the hymns they preceded. Carolina Sandell Berg watched her father fall overboard and drown. She was twenty-six. Within the year she had written "Children of the Heavenly Father" — "safely in His bosom gather" — and seven years later she wrote "Day by Day," about the daily strength God provides for struggling children. The gap between what she experienced and what she sang is not hypocrisy. It is the gap that Morgan's entire book is trying to illuminate.

Horatio Spafford lost his son to Scarlet Fever. He lost his fortune in the Chicago Fire. He sent his wife and four daughters across the Atlantic; all four daughters drowned when the *Ville du Havre* sank in two hours. His cable from his wife: "Saved, alone." Crossing to meet her, the captain called him to the spot where the ship had gone down. He went to his cabin and wrote: "When sorrows like sea-billows roll — whatever my lot, Thou hast taught me to say, it is well." Morgan does not speculate about Spafford's psychology. He records the event, records the hymn, and leaves the reader with what cannot be explained: that a man sailing over his daughters' grave wrote the sentence "it is well."

This is the book's structural method and its theological argument at once. Morgan does not explain these people. He describes them. The description is the argument. Here is what happened to this person. Here is what they wrote afterward. Draw your own conclusions about whether faith is adequate to tragedy — but draw them having looked at the actual evidence.

**The Economics of Hardship**

One pattern Morgan does not make explicit but the reader cannot miss is the economic dimension of this history. Isaac Watts grew up in a dissenting household with an imprisoned father. Philip Bliss was born in a log cabin, left home at eleven to work in lumber camps. Thomas Dorsey wrote his most famous hymn twenty-four hours after burying his wife and son. Charles Gabriel composed "O That Will Be Glory" while working a factory job with failing finances. Frederick Lehman wrote "The Love of God" sitting on a lemon crate during breaks from moving thirty tons of citrus daily.

The prosperity gospel — the theology that equates faith with material blessing — has no account for these lives. What they represent is nearly its inverse: the people who wrote the most durable hymns were, disproportionately, people for whom material comfort was not available, and who therefore had no earthly anchor to compete with the spiritual one. This is not a romantic claim. Poverty is not ennobling, and Morgan never suggests otherwise. It is a descriptive claim: the theology that holds under pressure is the theology hammered out by people who had no alternative.

William Cowper attempted suicide multiple times before converting in a mental asylum. He never fully recovered from his depression. His depressions gave him, Morgan observes, "heightened sensitivity to the heart of God and to the needs of others." This is the most careful sentence in the book. It does not say that suffering is good. It says that some kinds of sensitivity require it.

**What the Stories Leave Out**

Morgan is a pastor writing for a popular Christian audience, and the book reflects those constraints. The political context of the slave spirituals gets acknowledged — "their hellish voyage, aboard slave ships, was only the beginning of their sorrows" — but then quickly moves toward the music itself and away from the systems that produced the conditions from which the music emerged. The church's role in sustaining or challenging slavery is absent. The racism that structured the lives of Thomas Dorsey and the Fisk Jubilee Singers is named but not interrogated.

This is not Morgan's project, and criticizing him for it is partly unfair. But it matters to note what the book cannot do: it cannot ask why the people most responsible for shaping American Christian music were disproportionately those with the least access to the institutions of American Christianity. That question sits underneath these pages like groundwater. *Then Sings My Soul* does not drill for it. A different book would need to.

Similarly, the theological liberalism of Washington Gladden — whose hymn "O Master, Let Me Walk with Thee" is included — gets named honestly but not examined seriously. Morgan notes that Gladden "advanced the liberal-leaning biblical criticism that undercut conservative theology" and "embraced evolution." He is honest that many conservative Christians opposed Gladden's views. He does not ask whether any of those views were correct, or what it means that the prayer Gladden wrote in response to theological criticism has been adopted wholesale by the very conservatives who rejected his theology. That irony is in the book. The interrogation of it is not.

**The Hymn as Witness**

What Morgan understands intuitively, even where he doesn't articulate it systematically, is that the hymn functions as a form of testimony. Not testimony in the thin sense of "sharing what God has done for me," but testimony in the legal and moral sense: the statement of a witness who was present, who saw what happened, who is accountable to the truth of the account. When Spafford writes "it is well," he is testifying — not to abstract theological doctrine but to something he experienced, at a specific location in the Atlantic Ocean, in the middle of a specific grief. The claim can be credited or doubted, as all testimony can. But it cannot be dismissed as hypothetical.

This is why these hymns have staying power that most worship music doesn't. They are not generic. They are addressed by specific people from specific circumstances to a God they had specific reasons to distrust. The generality of their language — "when sorrows like sea-billows roll" — is earned by the particularity of what preceded it. Spafford's sea-billows are real sea-billows. That specificity is what makes the abstraction load-bearing.

Morgan's great instinct as a curator is to insist on the biographical context before the theological claim. He knows that the claim is only intelligible from within the story. "Amazing Grace" means something different when you know about the flogging, the desertion, the March storm, the slow halting spiritual growth. It doesn't mean something easier. It means something more staked.

**The Question the Book Raises**

Near the end of the collection, Morgan inserts himself. He has been studying the Old Testament Tabernacle and wanting to preach on it for years. At a conference in Edinburgh, unable to sleep, he visualizes the Tabernacle complex and walks it in his imagination — through the gate, past the bronze laver, into the holy place and the most holy place. The hymn comes quickly. He publishes it as the collection's final entry and closes with an invitation: you can write hymns too. The world's best hymns have yet to be written.

It is a generous close, and it is also the book's most revealing moment. One hundred forty-nine times, Morgan has told stories about people who wrote hymns because they had no choice — because the grief was too large, the grace too specific, the moment too urgent to remain silent. Now he suggests that we might try the same, when we study the Bible, if the devotion moves us.

The gap between those two things is the question the book finally raises without quite asking. Can hymns be chosen, or must they arrive? Can we decide to write something that will hold under the pressure of someone else's catastrophe, or does that require the catastrophe itself? Morgan's answer, implicit in a hundred fifty accounts, seems to be: you write what you have. Cowper had depression. Spafford had a grief that defies proportion. Fanny Crosby had blindness and thirty minutes. George Bennard had months of preaching, carrying unfinished words through the Midwest, waiting for clarity. The hymns that hold are the ones that cost something to write.

This is not a counsel of despair. It is a counsel of honesty. If you have something to testify to, testify. If the thing is real, the words will come. If the words are adequate to the thing, someone else will find themselves reaching for them in a dark night you cannot yet imagine.

That is the theology of this book. It is not systematic. It is accumulative, biographical, irreducibly particular. And it is, on the evidence, correct.

## Research Addendum (2026-06-03)

**Research date:** 2026-06-03

**Topic tags:** songwriting, cognition

**Research notes:**
- For songwriting drafts, make the craft layer explicit: lyric, melody, harmony, rhythm, phrasing, rhyme placement, section contrast, and title placement do different jobs in the listener memory system.
- A good revision move is to identify the song seed. Does the draft begin from a lyric image, melodic contour, rhythmic feel, harmonic movement, story premise, or production texture?
- When the piece is about children, teaching, or folk material, emphasize repeatability and participatory memory: repetition, call-and-response, rhyme, and predictable variation are features, not simplifications.
- Music-cognition pieces should avoid reducing music to brain trivia. The interesting point is how perception, expectation, memory, movement, emotion, and social context make sound meaningful.
- For music-as-medicine or music-and-memory claims, keep the clinical language cautious: distinguish anecdotal therapeutic power from controlled evidence, and name the mechanism being proposed.
- The listener is not a passive receiver. Anticipation, repetition, surprise, entrainment, and memory help explain why a simple musical event can feel larger than its acoustic facts.
