# The Curator Is the New Small Radio Station

_Draft for Musinique._

Source notes: This draft draws on `Spotify_Curator.md` and `spotify-playlist-vetting-guide.md`, especially the distinction between playlist curation as a listening service, playlist placement as an algorithmic signal, and fake playlisting as the simulation of audience behavior.

**Searle frame:** A curator counts as a broadcaster when listeners rely on that curator to organize attention.

The small radio station used to do something modest and powerful. It made music local to a listener's day.

It did not merely transmit songs. It created a context. A host, a time slot, a region, a mood, a set of recurring expectations. You did not only hear a track. You heard it as part of a relationship with a guide.

The independent Spotify curator is now trying to occupy some of that space. Not at the scale of old radio. Not with the same legal structure. Not with the same cultural authority. But the act is related. The curator organizes attention. The listener delegates a small part of taste. The playlist says: come here for this kind of feeling, this kind of hour, this kind of use.

The curator playbook gets this right when it says the playlist is the product. The artist who becomes a curator stops saying "please listen to my song" and starts saying "I have built a listening context you can use." That shift is not cosmetic. It changes the social act. The song is no longer presented as a plea. It appears inside a frame of service.

This is what small radio understood. Listeners tolerate discovery when it arrives inside trust. They will try an unfamiliar song because the station has carried them before. They will give the first verse more patience because the surrounding context has made a promise. The curator's job is to earn that patience.

Spotify's institutional language makes this easy to misunderstand. It calls playlists "content" or "surfaces" or "sources." The better word is probably program. A playlist programs time. It decides what follows what. It teaches the listener what counts as belonging. It creates a mood world and then asks a song to live there.

A good curator therefore has obligations. The playlist has to serve the listener first. It cannot be only a disguised shelf for the curator's own music. It cannot accept every paid submission. It cannot be a random warehouse of desperate artists. The curator's authority comes from refusal. A playlist that accepts everything says nothing.

This is where the ethical line around payment matters. Paying a curator to listen and consider a track is payment for labor. Paying for placement is payment for institutional deception. The first can preserve judgment. The second buys the appearance of judgment after judgment has been removed.

The formula is simple: a curator counts as a broadcaster in a context where listeners trust that curator to organize musical attention. Break the trust and the curator becomes something else: inventory manager, traffic broker, or fraud channel.

The artist-curator model can be honorable. Build a real playlist. Define the listener. Serve the mood. Include other artists generously. Place your own music where it belongs, not everywhere it can be shoved. Watch the data, but do not let the data replace taste.

The new small radio station does not need a tower. It needs a promise. The promise is not "I can get you streams." The promise is "I know what belongs here."
