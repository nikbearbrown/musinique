### CHAPTER 8: Hugh Prestwood
**Core Claims:**
- Writing melodies a cappella (no chord support) produces fresher melodic thinking
- The "spark and fire" metaphor: protect early ideas from premature exposure until they can sustain external pressure
- Positive thinking requires daily practice, like piano — it does not arrive spontaneously
- Instinct must override brain when they conflict; gut feeling is the final authority

**Evidence:**
- 12 songs per year, 1 song at a time, methodical
- "Ghost In This House" from *Grapes of Wrath*; "The Song Remembers When" from Ann Sexton poem
- MTM Records deal through Crystal Gayle accident; Michael Johnson connection as real career launch

**Logical Gaps:**
- "Anytime my brain and gut feeling disagree, I go with gut feeling" — this is stated as universal principle but cannot be tested without knowing the base rate of gut decisions that failed, which are absent from the narrative.
- The "I was so impressionable, had I gotten negative feedback I probably wouldn't have written anymore" admission is the book's most significant single data point about the fragility of early creative development — but it is not developed analytically. The implication (creative development is highly path-dependent on early feedback quality) goes unexamined.
- Prestwood's "world's biggest stove" metaphor — front burner is the song, two million back burners are everything else — is charming but encodes a priority structure without justifying why that structure is optimal.

**Strongest Contribution:** "Don't fill in any blank with anything negative" — meaning: until evidence exists to the contrary, assume the positive outcome. This is the most practically applicable piece of advice in the book for managing the psychological demands of a speculative career.

- For songwriting drafts, make the craft layer explicit: lyric, melody, harmony, rhythm, phrasing, rhyme placement, section contrast, and title placement do different jobs in the listener memory system.
