# The Playlist Follower Count Is the Wrong Number

_Draft for Musinique._

<!-- HERO IMAGE PROMPT: A precise editorial image about Spotify playlist curation as an institution of attention: a musician, a curator desk, visible playlist rows, human listeners, and faint platform signal lines. No logos. No fake UI text. -->

Source notes: This draft draws on `Spotify_Curator.md` and `spotify-playlist-vetting-guide.md`, especially the distinction between playlist curation as a listening service, playlist placement as an algorithmic signal, and fake playlisting as the simulation of audience behavior.

**Searle frame:** A playlist follower count counts as useful evidence only when connected to listener activity and artist outcomes.

The playlist follower count is the wrong number.

It is not irrelevant. It is just insufficient in exactly the way that harms artists. It tells you how many accounts once clicked follow. It does not tell you how many people listen. It does not tell you how often the playlist is opened. It does not tell you whether followers are real. It does not tell you whether the playlist produces saves, repeats, or artist follows.

It is a prestige number masquerading as a diagnostic number.

This matters because artists are buying decisions with scarce money. A playlist with 50,000 followers looks safer than a playlist with 800. But if the large playlist is inactive, botted, badly matched, or full of passive listeners, it may damage the track's signal. If the small playlist is precise, loved, and used by people who save what they hear, it may be the better placement.

The curator playbook offers a better metric: streams per listener. It treats depth as more important than width. That is the right instinct. Ten thousand listeners who each touch a song once are not the same audience as 400 listeners who come back. The first can inflate a dashboard. The second can begin a fan base.

For playlist vetting, the better numbers are all relational. Playlist followers relative to curator followers. Streams relative to listeners. Saves relative to streams. Artist follows relative to placement. Geography relative to known audience. Track fit relative to playlist mood. Follower growth relative to visible promotion. No single number settles the matter. Coherence settles it.

The Searle formula: follower count counts as authority only in a context where followers behave as an audience. Outside that context, the number is decoration.

This is why fake playlists love follower counts. They are easy to display and hard for a desperate artist to interrogate. The number creates a social fact: this playlist looks important. Once it looks important, the artist fills in the rest of the story. Surely followers mean listeners. Surely listeners mean streams. Surely streams mean fans.

Each "surely" is a place where money leaks out of the artist's pocket.

The better due diligence is dull and necessary. Look at the curator's profile. Look at the playlist's track history. Look at update patterns. Search for the curator outside Spotify. Ask other artists what happened after placement. If you test a playlist, test small. Watch saves and repeats, not just plays. Watch whether the listeners make sense.

The follower count asks, "How large is the doorway?"

The artist needs to ask, "Who actually walks through, and what do they do after entering?"

Until that question is answered, the playlist follower count is not evidence. It is bait with typography.

## Research Addendum (2026-06-03)

**Research date:** 2026-06-03

**Topic tags:** spotify

**Research notes:**
- Spotify-related claims should distinguish editorial pitching, algorithmic recommendation, playlist exposure, and paid/opt-in tools such as Discovery Mode. Treating them as one black box weakens the argument.
- Spotify defines artificial streaming around listening intent and manipulation, including bots/scripts and paid services that guarantee streams. For playlist-vetting pieces, the central test is whether listener behavior remains durable after exposure ends.
- Discovery Mode terms are useful because Spotify explicitly says the program does not guarantee placement, streams, new listeners, or followers. That caveat supports a careful distinction between influence, exposure, and outcome.
