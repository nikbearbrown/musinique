# The Critic Is Dead Long Live The

_Draft rewrite for Musinique Substack._

Sometime in the last decade, without a press release or a formal ceremony, the music critic lost their primary job.

The job was gatekeeping: deciding, on behalf of an audience with limited time and money and access, what was worth hearing. For most of the twentieth century this was a genuine service. Records cost money. Radio play was scarce. You couldn’t sample everything, so you needed someone who could — someone who had heard it, thought about it, placed it in a tradition, and come back with a verdict. That was the critic. That was the transaction.

The transaction is over. Spotify serves four hundred million users music selected from a catalog of one hundred million tracks, calibrated to each listener’s history and preferences, updated in real time, and delivered without friction. The algorithm has heard everything. It has thought — in its way — about all of it. And it has come back with a recommendation engineered to the specific contours of your taste, your mood, your time of day. The gatekeeper has been replaced by a matching system of extraordinary precision and zero judgment.

What remains, then, of the critic? A recent comprehensive survey of contemporary music journalism asks this question obliquely, documents the shift from “gatekeeping” toward “contextual navigation,” acknowledges that the critic must now act as “a contextual navigator” rather than a tastemaker, and then — at the exact moment the argument becomes interesting — retreats into a discussion of SEO strategy and keyword density. The paper knows something has changed. It cannot bring itself to say what has been lost in the change, or what, if anything, should replace it.

This essay is an attempt to say that plainly.

---

## The Handoff Nobody Noticed

The paper is correct that music journalism has undergone an “ontological shift.” What it describes less clearly is how complete the handoff has been and how quickly it happened.

In the mid-twentieth century, publications like Rolling Stone and NME were genuine tastemakers in the functional sense: they had the access, the column inches, and the institutional authority to determine which artists reached a national audience and which did not. A cover story was not merely a reflection of cultural significance — it was a cause of it. The critic’s judgment and the audience’s discovery were linked in a direct chain of causation. The critic heard it first, wrote about it, and the reader, trusting the critic’s ear, sought it out.

The blogosphere cracked this model in the early 2000s by democratizing the voice. Anyone could write a Pitchfork-style review; the discourse became distributed. But the fundamental transaction remained: you still needed someone to tell you what to listen to, and that someone was still a human being with a sensibility, a history, and a position.

The streaming algorithm dissolved the transaction entirely, and it did so not by defeating the critic in argument but by making the argument irrelevant. Why read a review of an album you might enjoy when the platform has already queued the album, assessed your probable response, and lined up six more like it? The algorithm doesn’t tell you what’s good. It tells you what you’ll like. Those are different claims, and for most listeners, the second claim is more immediately useful than the first.

The paper registers this shift. It notes that “the modern reader no longer seeks a simple binary judgment of ‘good’ or ‘bad’ to inform a purchase.” What it doesn’t follow through on is the implication: if the reader doesn’t need a judgment, what exactly do they need from a critic? The paper proposes “contextual navigation” as the answer, but this is a description of a function, not a defense of it. The algorithm navigates context too — it knows genre histories, acoustic lineages, the collaborative filtering patterns of a hundred million listeners. If contextual navigation is all the critic offers, the critic is already obsolete.

---

## What the Algorithm Knows

To understand what the critic is for, you have to be precise about what the algorithm actually does.

The algorithm is a prediction machine. It analyzes the acoustic features of a track — its tempo, its valence, its energy level — and cross-references them with the behavioral data of listeners whose taste profiles resemble yours. When Discover Weekly places a song in your feed, it is making a probabilistic claim: given your history, there is a high likelihood you will engage with this. The evidence for this claim is your past behavior. The system is predicting your future behavior by extrapolating from your past.

This is genuinely impressive, and it is genuinely limited. The limitation is not technical but categorical. The algorithm predicts; it does not understand. It can tell you that listeners who saved a particular track also tended to save a second track, but it cannot tell you why that pairing matters, what it reveals about the culture that produced both tracks, what it means that you responded to both. It identifies the pattern. It cannot read the pattern.

The paper’s survey of music journalism history offers a useful counterpoint here. It mentions Robert Christgau — the critic who wrote over thirteen thousand capsule reviews across a fifty-year career — and his description of criticism as a somatic experience: knowing a record when it “goes through your head.” The phrase is precise. A record goes through your head not when it plays back accurately but when it has reorganized something — when it has put a question in your mind that wasn’t there before, or answered a question you didn’t know you were asking. This is not a function the algorithm serves. The algorithm serves your existing mind. The great critic disturbs it.

---

## What Only a Witness Can Say

Consider what criticism looks like when it is doing the thing only a human being can do.

Jessica Hopper, whose career the paper briefly cites, wrote about music as a moral and political landscape — not as a matter of opinion about sound but as a question of who gets to make the sound, who gets to have their sound heard, and what it costs them. Her criticism of the indie rock world’s relationship to women was not a review; it was an indictment. It named something the music itself sometimes tried to conceal, placed it in a history of concealment, and asked the reader — directly, uncomfortably — what they intended to do with the information. The algorithm cannot do this. It has no capacity for indictment. It has no stake in what the music conceals.

This is the function that remains after the algorithm has taken everything else: testimony. The critic as witness, not matchmaker. The critic who was in the room when the culture made a particular choice and can report, with precision and moral clarity, what that choice cost and what it revealed.

Testimony is not the same as recommendation. A recommendation tells you what you will probably enjoy. Testimony tells you what happened, and what it meant, and what your enjoyment of it might say about you. These are different acts, and only one of them requires a human being with a position. You cannot automate having been there. You cannot automate the responsibility of having seen something and being compelled to say what you saw.

The paper’s survey nods toward this distinction when it describes the critic’s voice as “not merely a recognizable writing style but a consistent method of cultural analysis.” But it retreats almost immediately into craft advice — open-ended interview questions, keyword strategy, the mechanics of building an audience. The practical overwhelms the philosophical at precisely the moment the philosophical is doing something interesting. What is a consistent method of cultural analysis in service of? What is the critic, equipped with this method, actually trying to do?

The paper never answers. The answer is: the critic is trying to tell the truth about what the music is doing in the world — who it serves, who it ignores, what it celebrates, what it effaces. This is harder than matching a listener to a track they’ll enjoy. It requires the critic to take a position that can be wrong, to be accountable to that position, to update it in public when the evidence demands. The algorithm takes no position. It assumes no accountability. It cannot be wrong in any meaningful sense because it makes no claims — only predictions.

---

## The Critic’s Actual Job

Here is what the modern critic is for, stated plainly: to say the things about music that music itself cannot say, and that the algorithm cannot be designed to say, because saying them requires having something at stake.

The paper’s treatment of ethics — conflicts of interest, disclosure protocols, the prohibition on “hit-pieces” — gestures toward this without arriving at it. The reason conflicts of interest corrupt criticism is not bureaucratic. It is that criticism depends on the critic having no interest in the outcome other than the truth of what they’re reporting. The moment a critic is financially or socially beholden to the outcome of their review, they stop being a witness and become a participant. The review stops being testimony and becomes promotion. The reader, who came for testimony, receives something that looks like criticism and functions like advertising.

This problem has deepened in the streaming era. The paper mentions the “newsfluencer” — the independent creator who challenges institutional journalism — without noting that the influencer model is structurally incompatible with the independence criticism requires. An influencer’s livelihood depends on maintaining the goodwill of publicists, labels, and streaming platforms. A critic whose livelihood depends on the same goodwill is a critic who will eventually soften the indictment, bury the reckoning, find something generous to say about work that deserves none. The algorithm doesn’t have this problem because the algorithm doesn’t care about the music. The critic should.

What the contemporary music press needs — and largely lacks — is not better SEO or more diverse sourcing databases, though both are real goods. It needs critics willing to write as if the music matters morally, not just aesthetically. Critics willing to name what a streaming platform’s ghost artist economy does to the musicians it displaces. Critics willing to say that a particular record, beloved by millions, is doing something troubling to the people who listen to it. Critics willing to be unfair to an individual work in service of being fair to a larger truth.

The algorithm will always be better than the critic at predicting what you’ll like next. That race is over, and the algorithm won. The race that remains — the one the critic can still run — is the race toward meaning. Not what you’ll enjoy, but what it signifies that you enjoy it. Not what the music sounds like, but what the music is doing in the world, and to whom, and at what cost.

---

Robert Christgau described knowing a record when it goes through your head. The algorithm knows a record when your behavior confirms its prediction. These are different kinds of knowing, and the difference matters more now than it ever did.

When music was scarce, the critic’s job was to be the person who had heard everything so you didn’t have to. That job is gone. Music is not scarce. Your attention is.

The critic’s job now is to help you understand what you are spending your attention on — and what it means that you are spending it there. To be the person who was listening when something happened, who understood what happened, and who is willing to say so in public, with their name attached, accountable to the claim.

That is not the algorithm’s function. The algorithm serves your existing preferences. The critic is supposed to disturb them.

One of those is more comfortable. The other is more honest.

The honest one is still necessary. Whether anyone still wants it is a separate question — and answering it honestly might be the most important act of criticism left.
