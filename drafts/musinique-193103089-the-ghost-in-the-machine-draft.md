# The Ghost In The Machine

_Draft rewrite for Musinique Substack._

His name is Johan Röhr, and he has fifteen billion streams.

You have not heard of him. This is by design. Röhr is not a person in any meaningful sense — he is a composer who produces music under 656 separate aliases, all of them inventions, all of them populated into Spotify’s own mood-based playlists under names chosen to suggest the existence of artists who do not exist. Fifteen billion streams. More than Billie Eilish’s entire catalog. More than Post Malone, Eminem, or Ed Sheeran have accumulated in careers spanning decades of actual human work. All of it delivered by a single man operating behind a wall of invented identities, filling the platform’s functional playlists with content designed to sound like music without making any of the claims music makes — about experience, about meaning, about a life lived and transformed into sound.

Spotify knew. Spotify populated the playlists themselves.

This is not a story about fraud the platform failed to catch. It is a story about fraud the platform designed, implemented, and profited from — and then, when the external fraudsters arrived with their bot farms and their RICO conspiracies and their 37 billion manufactured Drake streams, positioned itself as the aggrieved party. The audacity of this posture deserves examination. You cannot credibly fight a fire you started. And Spotify, before any bot operator gamed its recommendation engine, before any crypto casino funded a streaming farm, built the original ghost economy — methodically, institutionally, with full knowledge of what it was doing and who it was doing it to.

---

## Fifteen Billion Streams for Nobody

To understand what the Johan Röhr arrangement means, you have to understand what a stream is worth in the current architecture and who pays when a ghost takes one.

Spotify distributes royalties through a pro-rata model: all subscription and advertising revenue enters a common pool, and each artist receives a share proportional to their fraction of the platform’s total streams. The mathematics are simple and the consequence is zero-sum. Every stream that goes to a ghost artist — an invented persona, a synthetic composer, a name attached to no human biography — is a stream that does not go to a real one. The pool does not grow because Röhr’s 656 aliases accumulated fifteen billion streams. It stays fixed. What changes is the internal distribution. The ghosts eat, and the living receive less.

Spotify’s reported financial motive for the ghost artist program was structural. The platform’s royalty obligations to the major labels — Universal, Sony, Warner — are negotiated as a percentage of total streaming activity. By populating its own playlists with music it controlled directly, Spotify reduced the market share of the major labels’ catalogs, which in turn reduced its payout obligations. The ghost artists were not a curatorial choice. They were a financial instrument. Spotify manufactured the fakeness because the fakeness saved money.

The mood playlists where Röhr’s aliases appeared — Deep Focus, Peaceful Piano, Ambient Chill — are among Spotify’s most-streamed properties. They are the playlists people put on while working, sleeping, cooking, grieving. The listener who queued Peaceful Piano at two in the morning, reaching for something that felt like human company, received instead a product engineered to occupy the emotional real estate of human music without making any of its commitments. The composer existed. The artist did not. The distinction matters, and Spotify chose not to make it.

---

## How the Ghost Economy Works

A ghost artist is an efficient machine. It requires no publicity budget, no tour support, no advance against future royalties. It generates no disputes over creative control, no demands for accounting transparency, no lawsuits when the numbers don’t add up. It will never be photographed leaving a hotel. It will never say anything in an interview that requires a statement from the communications team. It streams continuously, accumulates royalties on Spotify’s preferred terms, and returns to the platform a share of the money the platform would otherwise owe to human artists with leverage.

The mechanics of the Röhr operation are not unusual — they are the industrialized version of a practice Spotify allowed to develop and then declined to name publicly. The composer creates tracks under invented artist names, submits them through the standard distribution pipeline, and receives placement in algorithmically prominent playlists. The scale distinguishes Röhr — 656 aliases, fifteen billion streams — but the structure is available to anyone who understands that Spotify’s curation of its own playlists is not governed by the same standards it applies to external content. The platform curates toward its own financial interests. The ghost artist is what that looks like in practice.

What makes this more than a contractual eccentricity is the normalization it produced. When Spotify built its ghost economy, it established the operating assumption that the identity of the person making the music does not matter to the platform — that a stream is a stream, that the name attached to it is administrative rather than substantive, that the human being behind the sound is a variable the system is indifferent to. This assumption did not stay inside Spotify’s editorial decisions. It radiated outward into the behavior of everyone who observed that Spotify was willing to fill playlists with invented names and face no consequence for it.

The external fraudsters — the bot farms, the streaming manipulation services, the Virginia RICO defendants allegedly routing casino funds through cryptocurrency to manufacture Drake’s popularity — learned their lesson from somewhere. They learned that Spotify’s curation could be gamed. They learned that invented streams accumulate like real ones. They learned that the platform’s incentive to audit was limited by its incentive to report growth. They did not invent this knowledge. Spotify demonstrated it.

---

## The Platform That Built the Haunting

Here is the question Spotify cannot answer without indicting itself: what exactly is the difference between a ghost artist it created and a ghost artist someone else created?

The platform’s official position on streaming fraud is that it uses “best-in-class” detection systems, that it removed one billion fake streams in 2024, and that it is committed to protecting the integrity of the royalty pool. These statements are issued with apparent sincerity. They are also issued by an organization that populated its own playlists with invented composers for financial reasons, declined to disclose this practice to the artists whose royalties it was thereby diluting, and continued the arrangement until investigative reporting made it impossible to maintain without public acknowledgment.

The one billion streams removed in 2024 represents, by the platform’s own admission, a fraction of the inauthentic traffic on the service. Independent research suggests that bot-driven activity may account for a substantial portion of all streaming volume. And yet the platform that declares war on fake streams is the same platform that, when the fakeness served its interests, built the infrastructure for it — the permissive distributor relationships, the playlist placement mechanisms, the royalty accounting structures that make synthetic streams financially indistinguishable from human ones.

This is not a contradiction Spotify can resolve through better fraud detection. The detection systems are aimed outward, at the bot operators and the streaming farms and the casino money flowing into amplification campaigns. They are not aimed inward, at the company’s own decade-long practice of ghosting its playlists with composed content under invented names. The RBX class action and the Virginia RICO suit allege that Spotify exercised willful blindness toward external fraud. The Johan Röhr arrangement suggests the willful blindness was always selective — the platform could see perfectly well when it was the one benefiting from the fakeness.

What Spotify built, underneath the language of discovery and artist support and democratizing music, was a two-tiered system: one tier for the artists it owes money to, whose streams it has an interest in auditing and containing; and another tier for the content it controls, whose streams it has an interest in maximizing regardless of whether a human being ever chose to listen. The ghost artist lives in the second tier. He has always lived there. Fifteen billion streams later, he is still there, and the platform is still collecting its share.

---

## What the Ghost Costs the Living

The royalty dilution is real and quantifiable. Fifteen billion streams diverted from the human pool means fifteen billion streams worth of revenue that did not reach the artists whose work actually constitutes the cultural value the platform sells. The precise dollar amount requires access to Spotify’s internal accounting — the “Shadow Pool,” as forensic researchers have termed it — but estimates of the total annual revenue diverted to ghost artists and bot farms exceed one billion dollars. That figure is not distributed evenly. It falls hardest on the independent artists at the margins of the pro-rata distribution, the ones for whom the difference between the stream count they have and the stream count they would have in a clean system is the difference between a viable career and an abandoned one.

But the cost is not only financial. The ghost artist program corrupted something harder to audit: the meaning of presence on the platform. When a listener finds an artist in a Spotify playlist, they are making an inference — this exists because someone at Spotify, or some aggregation of listener choices, determined it was worth hearing. The inference is wrong when the artist was placed there because Spotify controls the artist and benefits from the placement. The listener’s trust is not a line item in the royalty calculation. It does not appear in the SEC filings. It is, nonetheless, the thing the platform is spending when it fills Peaceful Piano with a ghost.

This is what institutions do when they normalize fakeness: they degrade the epistemic environment for everyone inside it. The listener who cannot trust the playlist. The artist who cannot trust the stream count. The investor who cannot trust the MAU figure. The journalist who cannot trust the Spotify Wrapped ranking. Each of these is a consequence of a platform that decided, at the foundational level of its product design, that authenticity was a variable it could optimize away when the economics demanded it.

---

Spotify is not the first platform to make this decision. It will not be the last. The history of social networks is a history of platforms that discovered that fake engagement is cheaper than real engagement, that manufactured metrics are more controllable than human behavior, and that the moment of reckoning — when the gap between the reported number and the real number becomes undeniable — can be deferred long enough to build a business in the interval.

MySpace learned this. Twitter learned this. The lesson comes with a stock price.

What is distinctive about Spotify’s version is the human cost it carries. Social networks manufacture fake followers and fake engagement. Spotify manufactured fake artists — fake creative presences, fake cultural voices, fake entries in the catalog of human music-making — and placed them in the playlists where real human beings go when they need something that sounds like company. It filled the space where a real artist’s work might have lived with a product optimized for the platform’s financial convenience, and called this curation.

Johan Röhr has fifteen billion streams and 656 names and no face that can be photographed leaving a hotel. The artists whose royalties his streams displaced have faces, histories, and decades of work that the platform has declined to make room for in its own playlists.

The ghost is not a bug in the machine. The ghost was always part of the design.

And the machine is still running.

**Research notes:**
- Spotify-related claims should distinguish editorial pitching, algorithmic recommendation, playlist exposure, and paid/opt-in tools such as Discovery Mode. Treating them as one black box weakens the argument.
- Spotify defines artificial streaming around listening intent and manipulation, including bots/scripts and paid services that guarantee streams. For playlist-vetting pieces, the central test is whether listener behavior remains durable after exposure ends.
- Discovery Mode terms are useful because Spotify explicitly says the program does not guarantee placement, streams, new listeners, or followers. That caveat supports a careful distinction between influence, exposure, and outcome.
