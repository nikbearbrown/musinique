# Stop Pitching Songs. Build a Listening Context.

_Draft for Musinique._

Source notes: This draft draws on `Spotify_Curator.md` and `spotify-playlist-vetting-guide.md`, especially the distinction between playlist curation as a listening service, playlist placement as an algorithmic signal, and fake playlisting as the simulation of audience behavior.

**Searle frame:** A song pitch counts as meaningful when it tells the curator what listening context the song serves.

Most song pitching begins in the wrong place.

The artist says the track is new. The artist says the track is emotional. The artist says the track is their best work. The artist says they would be honored to be considered. None of this is false. Almost none of it helps.

A curator does not need your need. A curator needs fit.

The playlist system is built around contexts. Studying. Running. Crying in the kitchen. Driving with the windows down. Sunday morning. Dark electronic focus. Gentle acoustic sleep. The listener does not open a playlist because a stranger worked hard. The listener opens it because it promises to solve an attentional problem.

So the artist has to learn a different act. Do not pitch the song as an object. Pitch the listening situation in which the song becomes useful.

This does not mean reducing music to mood wallpaper. It means understanding how discovery actually happens on platforms. A song enters a playlist by counting as the right kind of event in the playlist's context. If the playlist is "quiet happy Sunday morning," your song must be quiet, happy, morning-shaped, and Sunday-plausible. If it is too aggressive, too lyrically dense, too slow, or too polished in the wrong direction, it may be good music and still fail the act.

The curator playbook calls this the anatomy of a mood. Define the mood. Define the listener. Define the context. Then judge every song against the promise. That is also how artists should pitch. The useful pitch says: this track fits your list because it has the tempo, energy, lyrical posture, and emotional function your listeners come for.

That is a different sentence from "please support independent music."

The Searle frame is direct: a pitch counts as relevant in the context of playlist curation when it identifies the role the song can play inside the playlist's promise. Without that, the pitch is just autobiography. The curator may be sympathetic. The listener will not be present for the sympathy.

This is also why artists should build their own playlists. Not as a growth hack, though it can help. As ear training. Curation forces the artist to hear function. What does this song do? Where does its energy turn? What has to come before it? What becomes impossible after it? Which songs make it sound better? Which songs expose its weakness?

That knowledge improves pitching because it replaces self-description with fit-description. The artist stops saying, "This is my song." The artist starts saying, "This is where the song works."

There is dignity in that. Music has always had contexts. Hymns, lullabies, work songs, dance records, protest chants, wedding songs, funeral songs, radio singles, album closers. Platform playlists did not invent contextual listening. They industrialized it.

The task is not to pretend the context does not exist. The task is to choose contexts that do not deform the music.

Stop pitching songs into the void. Build the room in which the song can be heard.
