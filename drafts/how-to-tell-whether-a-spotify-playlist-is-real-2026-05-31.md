# How to Tell Whether a Spotify Playlist Is Real

_Draft for Musinique._

Source notes: This draft draws on `Spotify_Curator.md` and `spotify-playlist-vetting-guide.md`, especially the distinction between playlist curation as a listening service, playlist placement as an algorithmic signal, and fake playlisting as the simulation of audience behavior.

**Searle frame:** A Spotify playlist counts as real when its curator, content, growth, and listener behavior cohere.

A fake playlist can exist. That is part of the confusion.

It can have a URL. It can have cover art. It can have followers. It can contain songs. It can send streams. It can even appear in your Spotify for Artists dashboard. The question is not whether the playlist is there. The question is what kind of institution it is.

A real playlist has coherence across four layers: curator, content, growth, and listener behavior.

Start with the curator. Does the profile look like a person or organization with taste? There should be a name, an image, a bio or at least a recognizable identity, other public activity, and ideally some presence outside Spotify. A curator does not need to be famous. But a curator with a 20,000-follower playlist and almost no profile followers, no photo, no bio, no links, and a machine-like username is asking you to accept authority without biography.

Then inspect the content. A real playlist usually has a promise. Mood, genre, use case, scene, tempo, era, instrument, task. The promise can be broad, but it cannot be meaningless. "Sad acoustic songs for late-night studying" is a promise. "Best hits 2026 viral top music" is often bait. Look at the songs. Do they belong together? Is there a plausible relationship among energy, valence, genre, popularity, and sequence? Are there enough tracks to make the list useful? Is it updated in a way that feels maintained rather than abandoned or churned?

Next look at growth. Real playlists tend to grow unevenly but intelligibly. Marketing campaigns can produce bumps. A curator with an active social account can drive new followers. A feature can create a rise. But unexplained vertical spikes deserve suspicion. So do sharp rises followed by collapse. If you have Chartmetric or similar historical data, use it. If not, take screenshots over time. Crude observation is still better than blind trust.

Finally, look at listener behavior after placement. This is the only layer that really matters for the artist. Did the playlist generate saves? Did listeners repeat the song? Did they follow the artist? Did the geography make sense? Did the source mix make sense? Did the song get downstream algorithmic activity, or only a temporary pulse?

The common mistake is to isolate one number. Followers alone prove little. Streams alone prove little. Even saves alone need context. What matters is coherence. A real playlist leaves a pattern that resembles human use. A fake playlist leaves a pattern that resembles metric delivery.

The Searle formula helps: a playlist counts as real in the context of music discovery when its institutional signals connect to human listening practices. The playlist is not real because Spotify hosts it. It is real because a curator maintains it, listeners use it, songs fit it, and placements produce behavior that makes musical sense.

That is why "real" does not mean perfect. A legitimate playlist can be small. It can have only a few hundred followers. It can be run by one obsessive person with excellent taste and no marketing budget. A fake-looking playlist can have beautiful cover art and a large number beside its name. The real one may be the smaller one.

Before submitting, ask five questions.

Who is the curator? What is the playlist for? How did it grow? Do the tracks cohere? What happened to artists after they were added?

If you cannot answer any of those, you are not buying promotion. You are buying uncertainty with a Spotify logo on it.

## Research Addendum (2026-06-03)

**Research date:** 2026-06-03

**Topic tags:** spotify

**Research notes:**
- Spotify-related claims should distinguish editorial pitching, algorithmic recommendation, playlist exposure, and paid/opt-in tools such as Discovery Mode. Treating them as one black box weakens the argument.
- Spotify defines artificial streaming around listening intent and manipulation, including bots/scripts and paid services that guarantee streams. For playlist-vetting pieces, the central test is whether listener behavior remains durable after exposure ends.
- Discovery Mode terms are useful because Spotify explicitly says the program does not guarantee placement, streams, new listeners, or followers. That caveat supports a careful distinction between influence, exposure, and outcome.
