# BOOKMAP: How to Write a Song on the Guitar
**Sofasession Ebook (Anonymous, undated) | Free tutorial ebook**

---

## PART 1: SECTION-BY-SECTION LOGICAL MAPPING

---

### CHAPTER 1: Requirements
**Core Claim:** The reader needs three things to proceed: a guitar, knowledge of what a chord is, and the ability to read tabs.

**Supporting Evidence:** None beyond assertion. No rationale for why these three prerequisites and not others.

**Logical Method:** Gate-setting — establish minimum competency before proceeding.

**Logical Gaps:**
- "You can read tabs" is listed as a requirement, but the ebook never actually presents tabs in a way that tests this competency. Tabs appear only briefly in chord variation examples. The requirement is stated but not operationalized.
- No estimate of expected prior skill level. "You know what a chord is" covers a range from someone who learned the word yesterday to someone with three years of practice. The ebook makes no distinction between these.

**Methodological Soundness:** Adequate as a minimal gate. The prerequisites don't over-promise difficulty or under-promise required investment.

---

### CHAPTER 2: Inspiration
**Core Claim:** The best way to get inspired before writing is to listen to music similar to what you want to create. Build a playlist, immerse yourself in it, and let it narrow the possibility space before touching the guitar.

**Supporting Evidence:**
- Author's personal experience: writing a song about Austria inspired by Michel Polnareff's "Lettre à France"
- Author constructed a Spotify playlist of Austrian artists and listened during commutes

**Logical Method:** Anecdotal demonstration — one concrete example from the author's own practice.

**Logical Gaps:**
- "Best way to get inspired is by listening to music" is stated as a general principle but supported only by one personal example. No acknowledgment that some writers find listening to similar music constraining rather than liberating (the "I don't want to accidentally copy someone" problem).
- The Polnareff example is specific and useful, but it demonstrates inspiration by proximity (my song is about X because I heard a song about X) without examining whether this is a reliable or repeatable mechanism.
- The playlist strategy conflates mood preparation with structural inspiration. Listening to Austrian folk music may set a tone; it does not resolve *how* to write the song.

**Methodological Soundness:** Weak as a universal prescription, adequate as a worked personal example. The author appropriately notes: "It's very likely that you are already inspired." This honest admission partially undermines the chapter's necessity.

---

### CHAPTER 3: Pillars of Music
**Core Claim:** Every good song rests on three pillars: harmony, rhythm, and melody. The author's process addresses them in that order. The guitar is uniquely suited to this process because it can express all three.

**Supporting Evidence:** None empirical. The "three pillars" framework is asserted without citation.

**Logical Method:** Structural framing — establishes the architecture for subsequent chapters.

**Logical Gaps:**
- "Good song is based on" these three pillars is stated without defining what "good" means. The claim is presented as self-evident.
- The 30% / 70% split (harmony+rhythm vs. vocals) appears here as a foreshadow and is stated outright in Chapter 7: "My uneducated guess is: The power of a song lays 30% in harmony and rhythm and 70% in vocals." The author explicitly labels this a guess. Credit for intellectual honesty; deduct credit for presenting a guess as a structural principle early in the framework.
- The sequence (harmony → rhythm → melody) is the author's preferred order, not a logically necessary one. Many songwriters begin with a melodic fragment or lyrical phrase. The ebook presents this sequence as "the process" without defending why other entry points are inferior.

**Methodological Soundness:** Adequate as a practical scaffold. Inadequate as a theoretical claim about songwriting structure.

---

### CHAPTER 4: Harmony
**Core Claim:** Begin by choosing a key with many open chords, then construct a chord progression by experimenting with chords within that key. Trust your ear over theory.

**Supporting Evidence:**
- Provides a list of open chords and a ranked list of keys by number of open chords available
- States that C major / A minor is the most popular key in music (no citation)
- Author chose D major for personal variety reasons, not because it topped the ranked list
- Used I–IV–V–I (D–G–A–D), described as "the classic three chord Rock'n'Roll progression"

**Logical Method:** Prescriptive process with embedded worked example.

**Logical Gaps:**
- "Trust your ear" is sound advice but is not explained. The reader who cannot yet distinguish a good progression from a bad one cannot execute this instruction. The ebook assumes ear training that it doesn't provide or acknowledge needing.
- The "most popular key" claim (C major / A minor) is asserted without source. It is plausibly true but functions as an appeal to authority without an authority.
- The author chose D major against his own ranked-list recommendation (it places third, not first) for idiosyncratic reasons. This is honest, but it also undermines the ranked-list framework just introduced.
- The progression D–G–A–D is described as simple and comfortable. It is. But the ebook does not explain the underlying logic (I–IV–V chord function) in a way that lets the reader generalize to other keys. "Experiment with chords in your key" provides method but not mechanism.

**Methodological Soundness:** The approach is pedagogically valid for a beginner audience. The claim "don't overcomplicate — less is more" is well-supported by popular music history and appropriately emphasized.

---

### CHAPTER 5: Rhythm
**Core Claim:** Rhythm means speed (BPM) and time signature. Start with 4/4 if uncertain; experiment with alternatives. Then determine chord change timing, then strumming or picking pattern.

**Supporting Evidence:**
- Author chose 3/4 time (Waltz) for deliberate thematic reasons: the song is about Austria, and Waltz is associated with Vienna
- Author found 3/4 too static and evolved toward 6/8 through experimentation
- References Nothing Else Matters (Metallica) and Bob Dylan as illustrations of picking vs. strumming approaches

**Logical Method:** Sequential process with worked example demonstrating real-time iteration.

**Logical Gaps:**
- The evolution from 3/4 to 6/8 is described as the result of dissatisfaction with how "static" 3/4 sounded — but 6/8 and 3/4 are mathematically related (6/8 groups as two sets of three eighth notes; 3/4 groups as three quarter notes). The author's ear led to a correct theoretical relationship, but this connection is not explained. The reader gets "I changed it" without understanding what changed or why it worked.
- "Experiment with different techniques and patterns" is repeated advice throughout the ebook. It is correct. It is also incomplete: experimentation without a filter criterion is not a method.
- The warning that finding a strumming pattern "usually takes me 2-3 days with some breaks" is one of the most honest and practically useful statements in the ebook. It calibrates reader expectations accurately.

**Methodological Soundness:** Adequate. The advice is sound and the worked example is illuminating precisely because it shows failure and revision, not just a clean path to a result.

---

### CHAPTER 6: Chord Variations
**Core Claim:** Modifying open chord shapes by removing a finger or mixing notes from adjacent chords adds texture and interest without requiring music theory knowledge.

**Supporting Evidence:**
- Concrete tab example: D chord with high e string open (Dsus2 or similar)
- Reference to Oasis – Wonderwall as an example of a static two-note figure held across chord changes

**Logical Method:** Demonstration through tab notation + named example.

**Logical Gaps:**
- The technique described (removing a finger to expose an open string) is a valid and widely used method. But describing it as creating "tension and excitement" without explaining *why* (suspended intervals, added tones) leaves the reader unable to predict which modifications will work. They must try everything and hope.
- The Wonderwall reference is accurate but describes a different technique (a fixed melody note over changing harmony, not chord modification by finger removal). Using it as a parallel example is imprecise.

**Methodological Soundness:** The technique is real and the examples work. The conceptual explanation is thin but may be appropriate for the stated audience (no music theory background required).

---

### CHAPTER 7: Melody
**Core Claim:** Melody and vocals are the most important part of a song — approximately 70% of its power by the author's estimate. Melody should be simple, include rests, and be developed by humming over the chord progression. Lyrics follow from the melodic framework.

**Supporting Evidence:**
- Author's personal estimate: 70% vocal, 30% harmony + rhythm ("my uneducated guess")
- Process: hum fragments, record immediately on mobile phone, loop to fix the line, repeat until complete
- Emphasizes rests as structural necessity: "A melody needs rests in order to have room to breathe"

**Logical Method:** Prescriptive sequence with explicit personal qualification of the key quantitative claim.

**Logical Gaps:**
- The 70/30 claim is the ebook's most significant structural assertion and its most honestly labeled conjecture. The author says "uneducated guess." This is commendable. But the claim functions as justification for spending proportionally more time on melody — and that prescription is not derived from the guess; it is merely adjacent to it.
- "Hum some notes over it" is the entire methodology for melodic invention. This is accurate — humming is how most non-theory guitarists develop melody — but it provides no guidance for a reader whose humming produces nothing useful. There is no scaffold for melodic failure.
- "Once you have a melody (or a fragment)" assumes you got one. The path for the reader who didn't is not addressed.
- The claim that knowing melody first determines "how many words and syllables you will need" is presented as a liberating constraint. This is a defensible framing, but it is also the point at which lyric writing becomes genuinely difficult — syllable count, stress alignment, vowel placement — and the ebook does not enter this territory at all.

**Methodological Soundness:** Honest about the primacy of melody; inadequate as a guide for developing one. The recording instruction is the chapter's most practically actionable advice.

---

### CHAPTER 8: More on Harmony (App-Based Approach)
**Core Claim:** The app "Chord!" provides scale harmonization — all chords that work within a key — without requiring music theory knowledge. It is a valid alternative or supplement to the ear-based approach in Chapter 4.

**Supporting Evidence:**
- Author states personal use and satisfaction with the app
- Describes the feature: shows available chords in key, plays them back, shows multiple voicings

**Logical Method:** Tool recommendation with endorsement.

**Logical Gaps:**
- This chapter also contains a pivot to the completion of the author's own song, which he finished through online collaboration on Sofasession with "a great keyboarder from Germany." This outcome is presented as a natural endpoint of the process, but online collaboration was not introduced as a step in the framework. It appears here as a conclusion to the worked example without being positioned as a generalizable method.
- The Chord! app recommendation is accompanied by an explicit pitch to buy the premium version if you find it useful. This is transparent but introduces a commercial interest into what is otherwise a neutral tutorial.

**Methodological Soundness:** The app recommendation is valid. The chapter's mixing of tutorial content with completion narrative creates an awkward structural shift.

## BRIDGE: Synthesizing the Logical Architecture

**The ebook's argumentative spine:** *Songwriting is learnable without theory. Here is a sequential process: harmony → rhythm → melody. Work iteratively. Trust your ear. Here is how I did it.*

**Two tensions run through every chapter:**

*Tension 1: Prescription vs. Permission.* The ebook constantly toggles between telling the reader what to do ("pick a key from the top of this ranked list") and telling them there are no rules ("experiment," "trust your ear," "less is more"). These are not incompatible positions, but the ebook never articulates where the prescription ends and the permission begins. The reader can reasonably ask: is the harmony → rhythm → melody sequence a constraint or a suggestion?

*Tension 2: Process vs. Craft.* The ebook successfully transmits a *process* for writing songs. It does not — and largely does not attempt to — transmit *craft*. The hardest questions (How do I know if my melody is good? How do I match syllable stress to melodic rhythm? How do I know when to end a verse?) are acknowledged as difficult and then not answered. This is honest but limiting.

**The ebook's most proven claims:**
- Open chords in common keys lower the cognitive overhead of chord construction
- Simple progressions (three chords) have produced commercially and artistically successful songs
- Recording fragments immediately prevents loss
- Including rests in a melody is structurally necessary, not optional
- Finding a rhythm pattern takes days, not minutes

**The ebook's most significant unproven claims:**
- Harmony → rhythm → melody is the correct sequence (versus other entry points)
- Vocals account for approximately 70% of a song's power (author's own label: "uneducated guess")
- "Listening to similar music" is the best method for inspiration (supported by one personal example)

**The ebook's most significant acknowledged gaps:**
- No guidance for melodic failure — what to do when humming produces nothing
- No engagement with lyric writing beyond "fit your syllables to the melody"
- No treatment of song structure (verse/chorus/bridge architecture)

## PART 2: LITERARY REVIEW ESSAY

---

# The 70% Problem

Here is the structural problem at the center of this ebook: the author tells you that melody accounts for approximately 70% of a song's power, labels this an "uneducated guess," and then provides roughly two pages of guidance on melody out of a total text that runs to perhaps twenty. The math does not follow the belief. If you genuinely think melody is where the work lives, melody is where the teaching should live. It doesn't.

This is not a dishonest ebook. It is, in several places, a disarmingly honest one. The author admits his 70/30 ratio is a guess. He tells you that finding a strumming pattern takes him two to three days. He describes abandoning a time signature mid-process because it sounded static. These are the confessions of someone who actually writes songs, not someone selling the fantasy that songs write themselves once you have the right framework. The works experience and the willingness to show iteration, not just outcome, are the ebook's genuine assets.

But honesty about limitations does not resolve them. The ebook teaches a process — harmony → rhythm → melody — and delivers on the first two pillars with reasonable specificity. Choose a key with open chords. Build a I–IV–V progression. Pick a time signature. Experiment with strumming patterns. These are actionable instructions. A reader can execute them. On the third pillar, the most important one by the author's own accounting, the ebook essentially says: hum over the chords until something comes. Record it. Fix it. Repeat.

That is not wrong. It is, in fact, how melody writing works at the informal level. But "hum until something comes" is advice that functions only for readers who are already able to generate melodic ideas — which is to say, it functions as a reminder for people who already know how to do this, not as instruction for people who don't.

---

The ebook's most intellectually interesting move is also its most structurally awkward. In Chapter 5, the author chooses 3/4 time for thematic reasons — the song is about Austria, Waltz is Viennese, therefore 3/4 — and then discovers through experimentation that he prefers 6/8. The mathematical relationship between these signatures (6/8 groups as two bars of three eighth notes; 3/4 groups as three quarter notes; the distinction lies in whether the primary accent falls on beats one and four or on beats one, two, and three) is not explained. The reader learns that the author changed his mind and that experimentation produced the right answer. They do not learn what the right answer is or why it was right.

This is not a failure of good faith. The author stated at the outset that he has no music theory background. The ebook delivers exactly what it promises: a process derived from personal practice, not formal training. But the 3/4 → 6/8 evolution reveals precisely where the absence of theory creates a ceiling. The author's ear found the right answer; he cannot explain the mechanism that made it right. For the reader who follows the same process and arrives at the wrong answer, there is no diagnostic tool available.

---

The ranked-list of keys by number of available open chords is the ebook's most pedagogically underused resource. C major and A minor top the list; they share the same key signature and together represent the most populated key in popular music. D major ranks third. The author chose D major for idiosyncratic reasons (he had written too many songs in A minor and E minor). This is fine — variety in one's output is a legitimate creative goal — but choosing D while presenting C as the recommendation without explicitly defending the departure undermines the list's pedagogical function. The list suggests a hierarchy. The author ignores the hierarchy. The reader learns: the hierarchy exists, and you can ignore it.

This is either the ebook's most honest pedagogical moment (the rules are guidelines; trust your instincts) or its most logically inconsistent (I gave you a ranked list and immediately demonstrated that I don't use it). The ebook cannot decide which, and so doesn't resolve the tension.

---

The Wonderwall reference in Chapter 6 is imprecise in a way that reveals the ebook's theoretical limits. Oasis's Wonderwall sustains a static two-string figure (high e and B strings, open or near-open) across four chord changes. This is a specific compositional technique — a pedal point in the upper register — not an example of chord modification by finger removal, which is what the chapter is describing. The technique the ebook discusses (removing a finger from a D chord to open the high e string) creates a suspended chord voicing; the technique in Wonderwall creates a melodic ostinato against harmonic movement. These are related ideas but not the same idea. The author's ear recognized a family resemblance between the techniques, but the analogy does not hold under examination.

This is a minor error in an ebook that does not claim theoretical rigor. It matters only as an index of the theoretical ceiling: when the author reaches for an explanatory framework, the best available tool is naming a famous song, not describing a mechanism.

---

The ebook ends with the author completing his Austria song in online collaboration with a German keyboardist on Sofasession — the platform the ebook exists to promote. This is not concealed; the ebook opens by disclosing it is a promotional tutorial for the platform. But the completion method — find a collaborator online — is introduced at the end as the natural conclusion of the process rather than as a step within it. The implication is that the ebook's process gets you to a draft, and Sofasession gets you to a finished song. This may be true. It is also the commercial logic of the document, and it should be read as such.

---

Where does this leave the reader who came for instruction?

The ebook successfully teaches three things: that simple chord progressions work; that rhythmic patterns require patient iteration; and that melody is the site of the hardest work. The third teaching is delivered as a confession, not as instruction. The gap between "melody matters most" and "here is how to develop melody" remains open.

For a guitarist who already hears melodies and needs permission to trust them, this ebook provides useful scaffolding and accurate expectations. For a guitarist who does not yet hear melodies and needs instruction in generating them, the ebook ends precisely where the work begins.

That is not a flaw in the ebook's character. It is a flaw in its scope. The author wrote what he knows. What he knows is process. What remains untaught is craft.

The 70% problem is not that the author got the ratio wrong. It is that acknowledging what matters most is not the same as teaching it.

## Research Addendum (2026-06-03)

**Research date:** 2026-06-03

**Topic tags:** theory

**Research notes:**
- Music-theory claims should keep the elements distinct: pitch organizes high/low relations, rhythm organizes duration and pulse, melody orders pitches through time, harmony stacks or relates pitches, and timbre identifies sound color.
- For guitar/theory pieces, translate notation into action. Readers need to know what their fingers, ears, and attention should do differently after the concept is introduced.
- The strongest theory writing alternates between technical naming and audible consequence: what does the concept let the musician hear, predict, vary, or repair?
