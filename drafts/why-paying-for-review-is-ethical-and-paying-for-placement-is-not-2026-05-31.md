# Why Paying for Review Is Ethical and Paying for Placement Is Not

_Draft for Musinique._

Source notes: This draft draws on `Spotify_Curator.md` and `spotify-playlist-vetting-guide.md`, especially the distinction between playlist curation as a listening service, playlist placement as an algorithmic signal, and fake playlisting as the simulation of audience behavior.

**Searle frame:** Payment counts as ethical review when it compensates attention without determining the outcome.

The line is not complicated. People make it complicated because there is money on both sides of it.

Paying for review can be ethical. Paying for placement is not.

A curator's time has value. Listening carefully is labor. Maintaining a playlist is labor. Writing a response is labor. Sorting through submissions is labor. A system that asks curators to do all of that for free will either exploit them or fill the market with people who make money in worse ways.

So paying a curator to consider a song can be legitimate. The payment compensates the act of attention. It does not buy the playlist slot. The curator can listen, decide the song does not fit, and reject it. If the payment remains the same either way, judgment can still exist.

Paying for placement changes the act. Now the money does not compensate attention. It purchases institutional recognition. The curator is no longer saying, "This belongs here." The curator is saying, "This was paid into here." If the listener is not told that distinction, the playlist has become deceptive.

This is why the old word payola keeps returning. Radio payola was not merely a payment problem. It was a disclosure and judgment problem. Songs were presented as selected when they were bought. The listener received a program that looked like taste but partly functioned as advertising.

Spotify playlisting repeats the structure in a new technical environment. The playlist slot counts as recommendation. Recommendation implies judgment. If the judgment was purchased, the act has changed. It may still produce streams. It may still be legal under some arrangement. But it is not the same act.

The Searle formula clarifies it: a paid review counts as ethical promotion in a context where payment buys attention and the curator retains the right to say no. A paid placement counts as covert advertising when payment buys the favorable curatorial act itself.

Artists should care about this distinction for selfish reasons as well as moral ones. Paid placement often attracts the lowest-quality listeners, if they are listeners at all. It can create weak engagement, strange geography, no saves, no profile follows, and bad downstream signals. Even when it is not fraudulent, it may be algorithmically useless. The platform sees that people encountered the song and did not behave as fans.

Ethical review is slower. It is less flattering. It produces rejection. But rejection is information. A real curator who says "this does not fit my playlist because the vocal is too forward and the tempo drags against the list's energy" has given the artist more value than a fake curator who sells 5,000 streams.

The practical rule is simple. Pay for ears, not outcomes. Pay for critique, not placement. Pay for access to judgment, not the suspension of judgment.

And if the service guarantees streams, run.

Music does not need another market for pretending to be liked. It needs better institutions for being heard.
