# 51 Percent

_Draft rewrite for Musinique Substack._

Sometime in 2025, the internet crossed a threshold that most people who use it daily have not yet processed: automated traffic surpassed human traffic. More than half of all web activity is now generated by bots — programs executing instructions, scraping data, manipulating metrics, simulating the behavior of people who are not there. The open web, the thing we built to connect human minds across distances that would otherwise make connection impossible, now carries more machine traffic than human.

The number is specific: 51 percent automated, 49 percent human. Approximately 37 percent of all web traffic is classified as “bad bots” — programs designed for malicious purposes, including the streaming manipulation that has made the music industry’s royalty system function as a wealth transfer mechanism from working artists to fraud networks. The remaining 14 percent is “good bots” — search indexers, uptime monitors, legitimate crawlers that keep the infrastructure legible.

This is not a metaphor. This is traffic analysis.

Deerhoof knows this, even if they did not cite the percentage when they left Spotify. King Gizzard & the Lizard Wizard knows this. Every independent artist who has spent the last several years watching their streaming numbers accumulate while their income stagnates, watching their music get placed in algorithmic playlists built to retain passive listeners for a platform that has no financial obligation to the person who made the music — they know this, in the way that people know things that haven’t yet been put into language. They know that they are transmitting into a space where the majority of what receives the signal is not a human being.

They are not retreating when they leave. They are finding terrain where someone is actually there.

## The Exit

The document that tracks the artist exodus describes it in the language of grievance, which is accurate but incomplete. The grievances are real: Spotify’s average payout runs between $0.003 and $0.005 per stream, against Apple Music’s $0.007 and Tidal’s $0.013. The class-action lawsuit RBX v. Spotify alleges that the platform tolerates billions of fraudulent streams on major-label catalogs while its automated systems pull tracks from independent artists who experience legitimate success — a radio play, a genuine viral moment — because the spike looks, to the algorithm, like manipulation. Discovery Mode, the tool Spotify sells to artists as a path to algorithmic visibility, is the subject of a separate suit alleging it is a modern form of payola: listeners paying $11.99 a month for a personalized experience that is, in practice, partly a pay-to-play system where recommendations are shaped by which labels have accepted a royalty discount in exchange for preferential placement.

These are legitimate grievances. They are also the symptoms of something deeper than a broken payment model.

The artists who are leaving are not primarily responding to the economics, though the economics are unjust. They are responding to what the economics reveal about the relationship the platform has always had with their music: it was never their music to the platform. It was content. It was inventory. It was the substance that filled the recommendation engine, the thing that kept users inside the application long enough to see an advertisement or renew a subscription. The artist was always, in the platform’s actual architecture, a supplier — and suppliers who become costly or inconvenient can be replaced.

The replacement has arrived. Spotify removed more than 75 million spam tracks in a single twelve-month period. This number is presented in the industry documentation as evidence of the platform’s commitment to quality. Read it again. Seventy-five million tracks, removed because they were generated at industrial scale to exploit royalty mechanics, existed on the platform long enough to accumulate streams, and had to be identified and deleted after the fact. The system that was supposed to connect human artists with human listeners was, in the same moment, a distribution infrastructure for machine-generated content produced specifically to extract money from the pool that was supposed to pay the humans.

Deerhoof left. King Gizzard left. Others are following. They are moving behind Substack paywalls, into private Discord servers, toward Bandcamp storefronts where the split runs 85 to 90 percent in the artist’s favor and the person buying the record is, by the transaction’s nature, a human being who made a specific choice. They are building mailing lists. They are scheduling direct-to-fan livestreams. They are, in the language that gets used to describe this migration, retreating behind “digital walls.”

That framing is wrong. Walls keep things out. What these artists are building keeps something in: the possibility of a human being on the other end of the signal.

## The Entropy Spiral

There is a phenomenon in machine learning research called model collapse. The mechanism is this: AI systems trained on AI-generated data progressively degrade. The outputs become statistically distorted, the errors compound, the hallucinations multiply, because the training set contains not the irregular, surprising, contradictory texture of human-generated material but the smooth, averaged, optimized output of machines that were themselves trained on human material — and then on the machines’ outputs — and then on the outputs of outputs. The signal attenuates with each generation. The copies accumulate the artifacts of copying.

By April 2025, 74.2 percent of newly published web pages contained AI-generated content, leaving roughly a quarter of new digital material as purely human-written. Experts working in this space project that by 2030, 99 percent of online content will be AI-generated. The music industry’s version of this phenomenon has been named, with a precision that is itself a kind of horror: the Slop Era. The platforms are inundated with synthetic audio engineered not to be heard but to be streamed — to accumulate the 30-second threshold that triggers a payable play, to avoid the skip rates that would flag it for removal, to exist in sufficient volume that its fraction of the royalty pool, multiplied across hundreds of thousands of tracks and millions of automated plays, becomes real money flowing to no human purpose.

The entropy spiral closes on itself. The algorithm trained to identify human engagement cannot distinguish between human engagement and a sophisticated simulation of human engagement, because the simulation was built by studying the algorithm’s definition of human engagement and reproducing it exactly. The bots have read the documentation. They know what a save looks like, what a playlist addition signals, what the ratio of replays to unique listeners needs to be to avoid triggering a quality audit. They are producing those signals. The platform is receiving them and treating them as evidence of human interest.

Meanwhile, the humans — the ones who wrote the music, the ones who chose to listen — are becoming a minority in the space that was supposedly built for them. Not metaphorically. By traffic analysis.

What the Dead Internet Theory described as a fringe speculation about the inauthenticity of online experience has become a documented phenomenon with a percentage attached to it. The internet is not dead. But the majority of what moves through it is not alive in the way that matters for art: it does not feel, it does not choose, it is not changed by what it encounters.

An artist releasing music into that majority is doing something closer to filing a document than performing an act of communication.

## The Terrain That Remains

The artists who are leaving are finding, in the spaces they move toward, something that has become scarce enough to be remarkable: verified human attention.

This is what a Substack subscription represents, in 2026, for the artist who builds one. Not primarily revenue, though the revenue is more equitable than Spotify’s fractions of a cent. Not primarily audience size, though the audience is real in a way that a stream count may not be. What a subscription represents is a person who made a specific, friction-bearing choice — entered payment information, committed to a recurring charge, actively opted in — to receive what the artist makes directly, without an algorithm deciding whether to surface it or a bot farm deciding whether to consume it.

The friction is the point. The open web optimized for frictionlessness: the one-click stream, the autoplay continuation, the recommendation that arrives without being requested. Frictionlessness was supposed to be the gift the platform gave to listeners and artists alike, removing the barriers between music and the people who wanted to hear it. What it actually removed was the signal embedded in the act of choosing. When everything is available at zero marginal cost with zero effort, the act of listening carries no information about what the listening means to the listener.

A person who finds an artist on Bandcamp, navigates to their page, selects an album, and pays fifteen dollars for a download has told the artist something that a million algorithmic streams cannot tell them: that this music was worth the friction. That a specific human being, with competing claims on their attention and their money, decided that this was where some of both should go.

The walled gardens the industry documentation mourns as a failure of the open web are not failures. They are the places where human attention is still the actual currency, because the barriers to entry — however modest — exclude the bots that have no reason to pay fifteen dollars for a download.

Tidal at $0.013 per stream. Qobuz with its human-curated editorial focus. SoundCloud’s direct-fan model. These are not utopias and they are not solutions to the structural problems the streaming economy has created. They are spaces where the percentage of human traffic is, by design or by economic logic, higher than 49 percent. Where the signal is noisier because the signal comes from people, and people are irregular.

Irregularity is what authenticity feels like when it is in the room.

## What the Exodus Knows

I want to be careful not to romanticize this. The artists who left Spotify are not monks. They are people responding rationally to an economic and cultural environment that has made irrational promises and failed to keep them. The promise was: distribute your music through us, and we will connect you with the people who want to hear it. The failure is: we built an infrastructure that cannot reliably distinguish those people from bots, that enforces its rules selectively based on catalog size and label relationship, that takes 30 percent of the royalties generated by the promotional tool it sold you to use.

Rational people leave situations like that. Calling it an exodus implies more intentionality than most of the departures probably contained. Some of them left in anger. Some left in exhaustion. Some looked at the numbers and made a business decision.

But the direction they moved in — toward human-verified spaces, toward friction-bearing transactions, toward audiences that chose them — contains an argument that is worth extracting from the noise of the grievances, because the argument is about something more durable than Spotify’s payout rate.

It is about what music is for.

The streaming model, in its purest expression, treats music as ambient infrastructure — the thing that fills the space between a user’s decision to open an application and their decision to close it. This is not a secret. The platform’s documentation says it directly: the goal is session extension, churn reduction, subscription renewal. Music is the means. Retention is the end. The artist is a supplier in a supply chain whose customer is the listener’s attention, not the listener.

What the artists leaving are insisting on — not in manifestos, but in the choices they are making about where to put their work — is a different model. Music as an act of communication between a specific person who made something and a specific person who chose to receive it. Not content. Not inventory. Not a metric in an engagement analysis.

The 51 percent threshold means that the open web is no longer a reliable place to attempt that kind of communication. The majority of what receives the signal is not equipped to be changed by it.

The artists who know this are finding the 49 percent.

That is not a retreat. That is precision.

## Research Addendum (2026-06-03)

**Research date:** 2026-06-03

**Topic tags:** spotify, cognition

**Research notes:**
- Spotify-related claims should distinguish editorial pitching, algorithmic recommendation, playlist exposure, and paid/opt-in tools such as Discovery Mode. Treating them as one black box weakens the argument.
- Spotify defines artificial streaming around listening intent and manipulation, including bots/scripts and paid services that guarantee streams. For playlist-vetting pieces, the central test is whether listener behavior remains durable after exposure ends.
- Discovery Mode terms are useful because Spotify explicitly says the program does not guarantee placement, streams, new listeners, or followers. That caveat supports a careful distinction between influence, exposure, and outcome.
- Music-cognition pieces should avoid reducing music to brain trivia. The interesting point is how perception, expectation, memory, movement, emotion, and social context make sound meaningful.
- For music-as-medicine or music-and-memory claims, keep the clinical language cautious: distinguish anecdotal therapeutic power from controlled evidence, and name the mechanism being proposed.
- The listener is not a passive receiver. Anticipation, repetition, surprise, entrainment, and memory help explain why a simple musical event can feel larger than its acoustic facts.
