# Why Big Playlists Can Damage Small Artists

_Draft for Musinique._

Source notes: This draft draws on `Spotify_Curator.md` and `spotify-playlist-vetting-guide.md`, especially the distinction between playlist curation as a listening service, playlist placement as an algorithmic signal, and fake playlisting as the simulation of audience behavior.

**Searle frame:** A large playlist counts as opportunity only when its listeners are plausible listeners for the song.

The big playlist looks like rescue.

This is why it is dangerous.

An independent artist sees a playlist with 80,000 followers and imagines a room finally large enough for the song. The curator accepts. The dashboard jumps. Monthly listeners rise. Screenshots become possible. For a few days, the artist feels the relief of being able to point to a number.

Then nothing happens.

No saves. No profile follows. No repeat listening. No messages. No mailing list growth. No Bandcamp purchases. No algorithmic lift that survives the placement. The song entered a large room and left no social trace.

That is not neutral. On Spotify, weak engagement is information. If the track is routed to listeners who do not want it, the platform may learn that the track does not produce the signals associated with relevance. A bad placement can therefore be worse than no placement. It exposes the song to the wrong institutional test.

The Searle formula: a large playlist counts as opportunity only in a context where its listeners are plausible listeners for the song. Size alone does not create opportunity. Fit creates opportunity. Size amplifies whatever fit is already there. If the fit is bad, size amplifies indifference.

This is why small playlists can outperform large ones. A small, coherent list with real listeners can produce saves and repeats. A large, incoherent list can produce fly-by traffic. The first teaches the platform that the song means something to someone. The second teaches the platform that the song was heard and abandoned.

The fake playlist economy makes the problem worse because it sells scale stripped of context. It gives artists the one number they crave and withholds the only question that matters: who are these people, and why would they care?

A real curator knows the room. A fake curator sells the headcount.

Before chasing a big playlist, inspect the average track. Is your song similar in energy, mood, genre, and popularity? Are unknown artists actually performing there, or merely appearing there? Do placed artists gain followers? Does the list have a coherent use case? Does the curator have an identity? Would you listen to this playlist if your own song were not on it?

That last question is brutal and useful.

The fantasy is that any exposure is good exposure. Platform music has made that false. Exposure is a test. Bad exposure is a failed test administered by the wrong examiner.

The right playlist is not the biggest one. It is the one where a stranger can hear your song and think, without being instructed, "this belongs here." That recognition is the beginning of discovery.

## Research Addendum (2026-06-03)

**Research date:** 2026-06-03

**Topic tags:** spotify

**Research notes:**
- Spotify-related claims should distinguish editorial pitching, algorithmic recommendation, playlist exposure, and paid/opt-in tools such as Discovery Mode. Treating them as one black box weakens the argument.
- Spotify defines artificial streaming around listening intent and manipulation, including bots/scripts and paid services that guarantee streams. For playlist-vetting pieces, the central test is whether listener behavior remains durable after exposure ends.
- Discovery Mode terms are useful because Spotify explicitly says the program does not guarantee placement, streams, new listeners, or followers. That caveat supports a careful distinction between influence, exposure, and outcome.
