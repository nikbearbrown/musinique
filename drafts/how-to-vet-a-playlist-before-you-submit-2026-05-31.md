# How to Vet a Playlist Before You Submit

_Draft for Musinique._

Source notes: This draft draws on `Spotify_Curator.md` and `spotify-playlist-vetting-guide.md`, especially the distinction between playlist curation as a listening service, playlist placement as an algorithmic signal, and fake playlisting as the simulation of audience behavior.

**Searle frame:** A playlist counts as a good submission target when its identity, content, growth, and outcomes match the artist's track.

Before you submit to a playlist, slow down.

The money you lose on a bad placement is not only the fee. You can also lose signal quality. You can teach Spotify that the wrong listeners heard your song and did not care. You can produce a spike that looks exciting for two days and leaves no audience behind.

Vetting is not paranoia. It is basic hygiene.

Start with identity. Who runs the playlist? Look for a curator name, profile image, bio, social links, and other public playlists. The curator does not need to be a brand. A real person with modest taste is enough. But if the playlist has thousands of followers and the curator has no visible identity, treat that as a red flag.

Then examine fit. Do not submit because the playlist is large. Submit because the track belongs there. Compare mood, energy, genre, vocal style, tempo, and listener context. If your song would interrupt the playlist's promise, it is a bad target even if the curator is real.

Next examine content quality. Does the playlist have enough tracks to function as a listening experience? Is it coherent? Is it updated? Does it mix established and emerging artists in a plausible way? Or is it a warehouse of unknown submissions with no internal logic?

Then examine growth if you can. Use Chartmetric or any historical tool available. If you cannot, observe manually over time. Sudden follower spikes without visible explanation are suspicious. So are stagnant playlists with inflated follower numbers.

After placement, examine outcomes. This is the step artists skip because they want the placement to have worked. Look at saves, repeats, profile follows, listener geography, source mix, and whether any algorithmic activity follows. Streams alone are not enough. If a playlist sends plays and nothing else, it may have sent traffic rather than listeners.

The vetting guide suggests a useful standard: legitimate promotion connects artists with curators and compensates review time. It does not guarantee placement or streams. The moment someone guarantees outcomes, the act has likely changed from curation to manipulation.

The Searle formula: a playlist counts as a good submission target in a context where the playlist's human institution and the song's musical function match. This is not a matter of size. It is a matter of fit plus trust.

Ask these questions before paying:

Who is the curator? What is the playlist's promise? Do the tracks cohere? How did the followers arrive? What kind of artists are placed? What happened to them afterward? Is the payment for review or placement? Can the curator say no?

If the service cannot tolerate those questions, it has already answered them.

## Research Addendum (2026-06-03)

**Research date:** 2026-06-03

**Topic tags:** spotify

**Research notes:**
- Spotify-related claims should distinguish editorial pitching, algorithmic recommendation, playlist exposure, and paid/opt-in tools such as Discovery Mode. Treating them as one black box weakens the argument.
- Spotify defines artificial streaming around listening intent and manipulation, including bots/scripts and paid services that guarantee streams. For playlist-vetting pieces, the central test is whether listener behavior remains durable after exposure ends.
- Discovery Mode terms are useful because Spotify explicitly says the program does not guarantee placement, streams, new listeners, or followers. That caveat supports a careful distinction between influence, exposure, and outcome.
