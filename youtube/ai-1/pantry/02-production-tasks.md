# Production Tasks: What to Delegate and What to Protect

*Speed is useful only when it does not move the wrong decision out of your hands.*

**One-line capability:** Readers build a personal delegation map — the specific tasks in their practice to assign to AI and the tasks to protect as irreducibly human.

## Learning Outcomes

By the end of this chapter, you will be able to:

- **Apply** the three-column delegation map to your own design workflow.
- **Analyze** which tasks are bounded production tasks and which are judgment-bearing decisions.
- **Apply** an additive/subtractive pass to an AI-assisted deliverable.
- **Evaluate** whether a claimed productivity gain actually protects or weakens your client relationship.

## Opening Case

A freelance brand designer has a familiar problem: the client wants a campaign extension in five days. The old version of the job would have taken three weeks. Moodboards, image directions, social adaptations, deck cleanup, headline alternates, export specs. Too much work, too little time.

She uses AI heavily.

AI expands the moodboard. AI drafts image prompts. AI suggests copy alternates. AI cleans up the presentation structure. AI helps resize approved assets.

But she does not let AI choose the type system. She does not upload the confidential strategy deck. She does not let AI interpret the client's vague comment that the work should feel "less startup." She does not let AI write the final client rationale.

The project lands in five days. The client does not say, "This feels automated." The client says, "This is sharper than the last round."

What changed was not that AI did the design. What changed was the delegation map.

This case is illustrative unless replaced with an author-sourced project. Its job is to show the shape of the workflow: AI used aggressively, but not indiscriminately.

## Core Content Blocks

### 1. The Tier 1 task list

The safest first uses of AI are bounded tasks. A bounded task has a clear input, a clear output, and a low risk that the tool will silently make a strategic decision for you.

For a graphic or brand designer, Tier 1 tasks often include:

| Task | Why it is usually delegable | Human check required |
|---|---|---|
| Moodboard expansion | The tool can generate adjacent visual references quickly. | Reject cliches and category confusion. |
| First-pass copy alternates | The tool can produce many options. | Check voice, accuracy, and client fit. |
| Presentation cleanup | The tool can structure and condense. | Preserve the strategic argument. |
| Asset resizing | The task is mechanical once the system is approved. | Check hierarchy, cropping, and legibility. |
| Competitive scan summary | The tool can cluster visible patterns. | Interpret what the patterns mean. |

Generative AI productivity research supports this task-level view. Noy and Zhang found faster completion and quality improvements in constrained writing tasks (Noy and Zhang, 2023). Brynjolfsson, Li, and Raymond found productivity gains in a customer-support setting (Brynjolfsson, Li, and Raymond, 2023). Those studies do not prove a universal design multiplier. They show that bounded work is where acceleration is most plausible.

**Practical rule:** Start with tasks whose failure you can quickly inspect.

### 2. The delegation map

Use three columns:

| Delegate | Explore then decide | Protect |
|---|---|---|
| AI can do most of the task after you set the frame. | AI can generate options, but you make the decision. | AI may assist around the edge, but the decision stays human. |

The middle column is the most important. Many designers treat AI as either forbidden or automatic. Most real work is neither. You can use AI to explore a direction and still protect the final decision.

For brand identity work:

| Delegate | Explore then decide | Protect |
|---|---|---|
| File naming, deck cleanup, image placeholders, rough copy alternates, resizing approved assets | Moodboards, rough logo directions, campaign themes, competitive pattern scans, first-pass naming territories | Final mark, typography, client rationale, disclosure judgment, contract promises, confidential strategy, final approval |

**Practical rule:** If the task changes what the work means, it is not a simple delegate task.

### 3. Additive vs. subtractive work

AI systems are good at adding. More options. More texture. More adjectives. More visual motifs. More plausible directions.

Senior design is often subtractive. Fewer elements. Clearer hierarchy. Sharper concept. More restraint.

This is not a mystical claim about machines. It is a workflow observation. Generative tools are built to produce candidates. They do not naturally know when the best move is to remove the impressive part.

Design research helps here. Dorst and Cross describe creative design as co-evolution between problem and solution spaces: the designer's understanding of the problem changes while solutions are explored (Dorst and Cross, 2001). If you accept the first impressive output, you stop that co-evolution too early.

**Practical rule:** Every generative pass needs a subtractive pass.

### 4. Platform selection by task

Specific tools change. The selection criteria last longer.

Before choosing a platform, ask:

1. Does this task involve confidential client material?
2. Will the output appear in a final deliverable?
3. Does the client need to own or register the output?
4. Does the tool provide usable commercial terms for this context?
5. Can I inspect and revise the result?

Adobe Firefly may be a lower-risk choice for some commercial image tasks because Adobe markets Firefly around licensed and public-domain training data and offers indemnification options for some enterprise customers (Adobe Firefly Enterprise terms, current-state source) [contested — see pantry flag]. Midjourney may be useful for exploration but its terms, public generation norms, and output authorship questions require care (Midjourney Terms of Service, current-state source). Stable Diffusion workflows vary widely depending on model, license, and deployment context.

This is current-state guidance. Verify tool terms before publication and before client use.

**Practical rule:** Pick tools by task risk, not by habit.

### 5. The 2x speed benchmark

"AI made me faster" is not enough. Faster at what?

Use a simple benchmark:

| Task | Old time | AI-assisted time | Human decision protected? | Quality changed? |
|---|---:|---:|---|---|
| Moodboard expansion | 4 hours | 45 minutes | Yes | More range, more cleanup |
| Deck cleanup | 3 hours | 1 hour | Mostly | Better structure |
| Logo direction | 12 hours | 4 hours | Only if final selection is human | Risk of generic output |

The benchmark is not a promise. It is an audit. If speed comes from skipping the protected decision, it is not a gain. It is deferred failure.

**Practical rule:** Count time saved only when quality and accountability survive.

## Worked Example

**Situation:** A local architecture firm asks for a brand refresh for a new residential practice. The deliverables are visual direction, color, type recommendation, sample social templates, and a client presentation.

### Step 1: List tasks

- Review current brand materials.
- Summarize competitor landscape.
- Generate moodboard directions.
- Explore visual metaphors.
- Choose typography.
- Choose color.
- Draft presentation.
- Create sample templates.
- Present rationale.

### Step 2: Sort tasks

| Task | Category | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Summarize competitor landscape | Delegate | AI can cluster visible similarities. |
| Generate moodboard directions | Explore then decide | AI can widen range; designer selects. |
| Explore visual metaphors | Explore then decide | Useful for prompts, not final meaning. |
| Choose typography | Protect | Type carries tone, readability, licensing, and differentiation. |
| Choose color | Explore then decide | AI can suggest palettes; designer checks market and accessibility. |
| Draft presentation | Delegate first pass | AI can structure; designer controls argument. |
| Present rationale | Protect | This is client trust work. |

### Step 3: Apply the subtractive pass

The AI-generated directions include warm concrete textures, elegant floor-plan lines, a roofline symbol, a monogram, and a neutral palette. It looks premium. It is also too much.

The designer removes the roofline symbol. Too literal. Removes the monogram. Too generic. Keeps the floor-plan line idea but redraws it as a modular grid that can become a layout system. Changes the palette from beige-gray to charcoal, clay, and off-white because the client wants residential warmth without looking like a lifestyle brand.

### Step 4: Protect the final rationale

"We are not branding you as architects who make houses. We are branding you as the firm that gives residential clients a sense of order before construction becomes overwhelming. That is why the system uses a measured grid, warm material color, and restrained type."

That sentence is not a prompt. It is a decision.

**The lesson:** A delegation map lets you use AI hard without letting it decide what the work means.

**The limit:** The map does not answer whether the final assets are legally ownable or whether AI use should be disclosed. Chapters 4 and 5 handle that.

## Common Misconceptions

**"If AI can do a task, I should delegate it."**  
Capability is not the same as suitability. AI may be able to generate a logo direction. That does not mean it should own the final mark.

**"The protect column is anti-AI."**  
No. The protect column is pro-practice. It marks where your value is concentrated.

**"More options mean better design."**  
More options often mean more sorting. Without a subtractive pass, variation becomes noise.

**"A faster workflow automatically supports higher rates."**  
Only if the client receives better judgment, faster response, or expanded value. Otherwise speed becomes a reason to pay less.

## Exercises

1. **Apply:** List ten tasks from your most recent client project. Sort each into delegate, explore then decide, or protect.
2. **Analyze:** Take one AI-assisted deliverable and remove everything that is merely decorative. What survives?
3. **Apply:** Run one Tier 1 task with AI. Record old time, new time, quality change, and one human decision still required.

## What Would Change My Mind

I would revise this chapter if design-specific evidence showed that fully automated workflows produce equal or better client outcomes in brand identity work without human control over typography, rationale, disclosure, and final selection. I would also revise it if platform tools developed reliable, auditable ways to incorporate client-specific tacit knowledge and legal constraints into final decisions.
