# Client Disclosure: When, How, and What to Say

*Disclosure is not confession. It is professional framing.*

**One-line capability:** Readers learn when AI use requires disclosure, how to have the disclosure conversation without undermining client confidence, and what a professional AI disclosure statement looks like.

## Learning Outcomes

By the end of this chapter, you will be able to:

- **Analyze** whether AI use is material enough to disclose.
- **Apply** a four-scenario disclosure framework to client situations.
- **Create** a short AI disclosure statement in your own professional voice.
- **Apply** clear language for direct client questions about AI use.

## Opening Case

You are in a client review. The work is going well. The client likes the campaign direction. They like the mockups. They like the image style.

Then the client asks:

"Did you use AI for any of this?"

There is a bad version of the answer:

"Well, technically, everyone uses AI now."

There is another bad version:

"No, not really."

There is a third bad version:

"Yes, but don't worry, it is completely safe."

The right answer is specific. It says what AI did, what you did, what is in the final deliverable, and what safeguards you used. It does not apologize. It does not hide. It does not turn the meeting into a lecture on copyright law.

This chapter gives you that answer.

## Core Content Blocks

### 1. The materiality threshold

Disclosure is required when AI use is material to the client's decision, rights, risk, or trust.

Use a three-part test:

1. **Deliverable test:** Did AI-generated material appear in the final deliverable?
2. **Rights/risk test:** Could AI use affect ownership, registration, confidentiality, brand safety, or public claims?
3. **Expectation test:** Would a reasonable client feel misled if they learned about the AI use later?

If any answer is yes, disclose.

NIST's AI Risk Management Framework emphasizes transparency, governance, and context-specific risk management (NIST, 2023). NIST's Generative AI Profile identifies transparency, data privacy, information integrity, and intellectual property as key generative AI risks (NIST, 2024). Those frameworks are not written for freelance designers, but they support the principle: disclosure depends on use context and risk.

**Practical rule:** Disclose when AI use changes what the client is buying, risking, or representing.

### 2. Four disclosure scenarios

| Scenario | Example | Recommended action |
|---|---|---|
| Internal support | Grammar cleanup, meeting summary, private brainstorm | Usually no disclosure unless contract requires it. |
| Exploration only | AI moodboards not included in final work | Often disclose if asked; document internally. |
| Material final contribution | AI image, generated logo element, synthetic mockup | Disclose before approval. |
| Confidential client data | Uploading briefs, strategy, unreleased assets | Pre-clear or avoid; contract may prohibit it. |

The U.S. Copyright Office's AI registration guidance requires applicants to identify AI-generated material in relevant registration contexts (U.S. Copyright Office, 2023). That is a copyright procedure, not a universal client rule. But it shows why hidden AI material can become a later rights problem.

**Practical rule:** The closer AI gets to final client value, the more disclosure matters.

### 3. The disclosure statement template

A strong disclosure statement has four parts:

1. What AI was used for.
2. What AI was not used for.
3. What human decisions shaped the work.
4. What safeguards were used.

Template:

> I used AI tools for [specific support task]. The final [deliverable] was selected, edited, and approved by me based on [client-specific criteria]. I did not use AI to [protected decision or prohibited use]. No confidential client materials were uploaded to tools that train on user inputs without approval. [Adjust based on actual workflow.]

Keep it under 100 words.

Do not say "AI was used" in a passive voice if the client needs responsibility. Say what you did.

**Practical rule:** Good disclosure is specific enough to create confidence and short enough to survive a meeting.

### 4. The disclosure conversation

Clients usually ask one of three questions.

**Curious client:** "Did you use AI for this?"  
Answer: "Yes, for early visual exploration and presentation cleanup. The final direction, type choice, palette, and system rules are mine. I used AI to widen the range, then narrowed and rebuilt the work around your brief."

**Concerned client:** "Does that create copyright issues?"  
Answer: "It can, depending on what appears in the final deliverable. For this project, the AI material stayed in exploration. The final mark and type system are human-built. If we decide to include generated imagery in final assets, I will flag it before approval and we can review rights."

**Angry client:** "Why didn't you tell me earlier?"  
Answer: "You are right to ask. I should have been clearer about where AI supported the process. It did not determine the final direction, and no confidential materials were uploaded without approval. I can document the workflow and update our agreement so the boundary is explicit going forward." [verify with counsel for sensitive disputes]

**Practical rule:** Answer the actual risk behind the question, not the philosophical debate.

### 5. Proactive vs. reactive disclosure

Reactive disclosure happens after the client asks. Proactive disclosure happens before the question becomes a trust problem.

Use proactive disclosure when:

- AI-generated material appears in final deliverables.
- You use client-confidential material with a tool.
- The client works in a regulated, sensitive, or public-trust field.
- The deliverable may be registered, assigned, or publicly represented as original.
- Your contract requires it.

The Federal Trade Commission has warned businesses not to make deceptive or unsubstantiated AI claims (FTC, 2023). For designers, the parallel is simple: do not let your process representation become misleading.

**Practical rule:** If late disclosure would feel like bad news, disclose earlier.

## Worked Example

### Conversation 1: Curious client

Client: "Did you use AI for any of this?"

Designer: "Yes. I used it for early image exploration and to test several layout directions quickly. The final recommendation is mine: I selected the visual direction, rebuilt the system, chose the type, and removed the generated elements that did not fit the brand."

Client: "So this is not just AI output?"

Designer: "No. AI helped widen the field. I made the decisions you are approving."

### Conversation 2: Concerned client

Client: "Can we own this?"

Designer: "The final wordmark and layout system were built by me. The generated imagery in the concept deck is not part of the final identity. If you want to use generated campaign images publicly, we should review the tool terms and decide whether to commission or license final art instead."

### Conversation 3: Angry client

Client: "I wish you had told me before the meeting."

Designer: "That is fair. I should have named the role AI played in the process. Here is the workflow: AI was used for exploration and mockup placeholders; it was not used to create the final mark or upload confidential strategy. I will add an AI-use clause to our working agreement so this boundary is clear next time."

**The lesson:** Disclosure works when it names both assistance and responsibility.

**The limit:** Disclosure cannot cure a bad rights position or a broken confidentiality promise.

## Common Misconceptions

**"I should disclose everything."**  
Over-disclosure can bury the material facts. The client needs to know what affects rights, risk, trust, and final value.

**"I should disclose nothing unless asked."**  
That turns AI use into a surprise. Surprises are bad for one-client relationships.

**"Disclosure makes me look less professional."**  
Vague disclosure can. Clear disclosure often does the opposite: it shows control.

**"Legal language is safer."**  
Not in the meeting. Use plain professional language, then put precise terms in the contract.

## What Would Change My Mind

I would revise this chapter if a clear, widely adopted design-industry standard emerged that defined AI disclosure thresholds differently from the materiality approach here. I would also revise it if client research showed that proactive disclosure consistently harms trust even when the AI use is bounded, documented, and professionally framed.

## Closing and Bridge

Disclosure protects the transactional layer. It gives the client a clear account of what happened and why it is safe enough to proceed.

But the deeper layer is the relationship itself: the knowledge, trust, and context that built the engagement before AI entered the room.

Chapter 6 protects that layer.
