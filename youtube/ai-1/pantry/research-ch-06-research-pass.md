## 1. Primary Sources

### Foundational papers and texts

- **Luhmann, Niklas (1981, posthumous descriptions through 2010s).** Niklas Luhmann's *Zettelkasten* — roughly 90,000 numbered note slips, cross-referenced, that produced 70 books and 400 papers over a career. The foundational case for "thinking happens in the notes, not in your head, and not in the draft." This is what `pantry/` is — one chapter's Zettelkasten. The pantry's worth is measured by whether it can answer questions you didn't know you had, which is Luhmann's exact test.

- **Ahrens, Sönke (2017). *How to Take Smart Notes: One Simple Technique to Boost Writing, Learning and Thinking.*** The English-language translation and explanation of the Zettelkasten method for working writers. Ahrens's framing — fleeting notes vs. literature notes vs. permanent notes — maps cleanly onto AI+1's distinction between raw LLM output, evaluated source notes, and what actually ends up in chapter drafts.

- **Cooper, Harris M. (1982). "Scientific Guidelines for Conducting Integrative Research Reviews." Review of Educational Research, 52(2), 291–302.** Cooper's taxonomy of research synthesis stages (problem formulation, data collection, evaluation, analysis, presentation) is the underlying logic for any "research pass" — including AI-driven ones. The Chapter Research Gatherer compresses Cooper's five stages into one prompt, and the chapter must teach the reader to mentally unpack that compression.

- **Page, Matthew J. et al. (2021). "The PRISMA 2020 statement: an updated guideline for reporting systematic reviews." BMJ 372.** PRISMA is overkill for a $1 Kindle textbook, but its core principle — that the *selection* of sources is itself the argument — is the principle the reader needs. Citing PRISMA flags the chapter's intellectual seriousness without demanding its rigor.

- **Caulfield, Mike (2017, 2019). "The SIFT Method" (Stop, Investigate the source, Find better coverage, Trace claims).** Caulfield's four-move method for evaluating online sources is undergrad-tested and operational. It is the right framework for the "four questions before accepting as draft-ready" content block.

- **The CRAAP test (Sarah Blakeslee, 2004, CSU Chico).** Currency, Relevance, Authority, Accuracy, Purpose. Older but still the standard library-instruction framework. SIFT is sharper for the AI era; cite both.

- **Hunt, Andrew and David Thomas (1999, 2019). *The Pragmatic Programmer.*** DRY principle — "Every piece of knowledge must have a single, unambiguous, authoritative representation within a system." The `_lib_` prefix and shared markdown library exist because of this principle.

### Key empirical cases — LLM citation accuracy

- **Goddard, Joel et al. (2023). "Hallucination in ChatGPT: A Cross-Disciplinary Investigation of References and Bibliographies."** Documented that GPT-3.5 fabricated approximately 47% of references in medical contexts; GPT-4 reduced but did not eliminate the problem. This is the empirical case for the chapter's "primary sources still required" insistence.

- **Bhattacharyya, Mehul et al. (2023). "High Rates of Fabricated and Inaccurate References in ChatGPT-Generated Medical Content." Cureus 15(5).** Replicated Goddard's finding with stronger numbers. Combined, these two papers are the citation-accuracy evidence base.

- **Walters, William H. and Esther Wilder (2023). "Fabrication and errors in the bibliographic citations generated by ChatGPT." Scientific Reports 13.** A library-science perspective. Found that even *correct-looking* citations had DOI errors. Reinforces "pantry is a reference, not a citation."

- **Bender, Emily M. et al. (2021). "On the Dangers of Stochastic Parrots: Can Language Models Be Too Big?" FAccT '21.** The structural argument for *why* hallucination happens — LLMs model the surface form of citation, not the act of citing. Cite Bender alongside Goddard so the reader understands hallucination is a feature, not a bug to be patched.

## 2. The Core Concept — State of the Field

### What is settled

- LLMs hallucinate references at non-trivial rates, and the rate is task-dependent (higher in medicine, lower in well-trodden CS).
- Note-taking practice predicts writing output. Luhmann's productivity is not in dispute, even where its causal story is.
- Source evaluation is teachable. SIFT, CRAAP, and library-instruction practice are well-tested in undergraduate populations.
- DRY is correct for shared content. The `_lib_` pattern is the right pattern.
- Research synthesis is a distinct skill from research collection. Cooper, PRISMA, Cochrane all agree.

### What is disputed

- **Whether LLMs can act as research synthesizers reliably.** Anthropic, OpenAI, and Google all claim improvement. Independent evaluations (e.g., Liu et al. 2024 on "lost in the middle") show real but uneven gains. The chapter must be honest: the Gatherer drafts the pantry; the human evaluates it.
- **Whether the Zettelkasten is special or whether any disciplined note system works.** Recent skepticism (Andy Matuschak, Dynamicland) suggests the tool is less important than the practice. The chapter should treat the pantry as practice infrastructure, not as a sacred form.
- **Whether AI-generated literature reviews should be cited differently than human ones.** No professional consensus. Most journals now require disclosure; few have rules beyond that.

### What has changed recently (last 5 years)

- **Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG)** has moved citation accuracy from "fabricated 50% of the time" to "fabricated when retrieval misses." The Chapter Research Gatherer is presumably RAG-based; the chapter should explain why this matters.
- **Tools like Elicit, Consensus, Semantic Scholar's TLDR, and SciSpace** have made academic research synthesis a different problem than web research synthesis. The chapter should name these as supplements when the pantry is thin.
- **The "lost in the middle" finding (Liu et al. 2024)** — LLMs reading long contexts attend disproportionately to the beginning and end. This is empirical reason to break pantry notes into discrete sections rather than one long file.
- **OpenAI / Anthropic / Google all now offer "Deep Research" agents (2024–2025)** that run multi-step research with citations. The Chapter Research Gatherer is in this family. Position it.

## 3. Application Domain Examples

1. **The designer's research deck before a brand pitch.** Mood boards, competitor audits, customer interview notes, color trend reports. This is a pantry. The chapter should open by naming this artifact the designer already produces.
2. **Pinterest boards as Zettelkasten.** A working designer maintains image libraries indexed by project, mood, and reference. The `_lib_` shared library is the textbook equivalent.
3. **The client interview transcript folder.** Designers know that re-reading interview transcripts reveals quotes they missed. Pantry rereading is the same skill.
4. **Stock-photo licensing audits.** Designers already evaluate sources for provenance (is this image free, paid, attribution-required?). The same evaluation muscle applies to LLM citations.
5. **Brand archaeology — research for a rebrand of an established company.** A serious rebrand requires primary archival research. The chapter's claim that "primary sources still required in chapter drafts" lands harder when framed as "you wouldn't rebrand on Wikipedia summaries — don't draft a textbook chapter on them either."

## 4. The Book's Thesis Connection

The thesis: TIKTOC.md session is the highest-leverage step; the human rewrite is the gate.

Chapter 6 is where the thesis takes its first body blow. The Chapter Research Gatherer is automation. It reads the TIKTOC.md and produces pantry files. If the reader trusts the output without evaluating it, they are reenacting the fluency trap one layer deeper — believing fluent research because it looks like research.

The chapter's load-bearing argument is the four-questions evaluation pass. The pantry is *not* a draft. It is a reference. Drafts in Chapter 7 cannot cite the pantry — they cite primary sources the pantry pointed them to. This is the structural defense against AI-laundered citations entering the book.

This chapter also makes the TIKTOC.md's value visible for the second time. A weak TIKTOC.md produces a Gatherer prompt that doesn't know what it's looking for; the pantry comes back generic. The reader sees this directly. The chapter should explicitly frame thin pantry as "evidence the TIKTOC.md was vague in this chapter — return to /c1."

Connection backward: TIKTOC.md (Ch 4) and scaffold (Ch 5) are inputs. Connection forward: pantry feeds Cowork (Ch 7), and the [verify] flags Cowork raises trace back to thin pantry entries here. Chapter 8 (Human Rewrite) is the moment the pantry's quality is paid for.

## 5. The AI Wayback Machine — Candidate Figures

**Candidate A — Niklas Luhmann (1927–1998).** Wikipedia page title: **"Niklas Luhmann."** German sociologist. The Zettelkasten case is the single most operational note-taking case in modern academic history. Substantive connection: pantry/ is the Zettelkasten for one chapter. Lesser-known to designers, undergrad-accessible (Ahrens's book is the gateway), non-American (German). Diversity contribution: non-American. Example prompt: *"Ask Claude: Read about Luhmann's Zettelkasten. Argue whether AI+1's pantry is a Zettelkasten or only resembles one."*

**Candidate B — Lorraine Daston (1951– ).** Wikipedia page title: **"Lorraine Daston."** American historian of science, longtime director of the Max Planck Institute for the History of Science in Berlin. Author of *Objectivity* (with Peter Galison, 2007) — the central modern history of how "objectivity" became a scientific value, and what it cost. Substantive connection: the chapter is about source evaluation. Daston gives the reader the conceptual frame that "objectivity" is a *constructed* virtue with a history, and that LLM hallucination is the latest twist in a long story about how knowledge claims get authorized. Underread by working designers; accessible; American-trained but Berlin-based, so partly non-Western institutional. Diversity contribution: woman, historian of science. Example prompt: *"Ask Claude: Summarize Lorraine Daston and Peter Galison's argument about the history of objectivity. How would Daston evaluate an LLM-generated bibliography?"*

**Candidate C — Eugene Garfield (1925–2017).** Wikipedia page title: **"Eugene Garfield."** Founder of citation indexing and the Science Citation Index. Substantive connection: invented the modern concept that citations are themselves data, traceable and rankable — the precondition for being able to detect a hallucinated citation in the first place. Less essential than Luhmann or Daston but a clean third option; male, American. Example prompt: *"Ask Claude: What did Eugene Garfield invent that makes it possible to check whether an AI-generated citation is real?"*

**Diversity flag:** Recommend Luhmann (lead) + Daston (sidebar). This pair covers the methodological case and the epistemological one, with a woman and a non-American institution represented.

## 6. Pedagogical Delivery Research

- **Before/after as the primary cognitive frame.** The chapter's opening is two pantry files — thin and strong — for the same chapter. Pedagogical research on contrasting cases (Schwartz & Bransford 1998, "A Time for Telling") shows this is the most effective frame for teaching evaluation skills.
- **The four-questions checklist must be sharp.** Too many questions becomes a CRAAP test the reader skips. Caulfield's SIFT (four moves) is the right size. Propose: (1) Is the strongest source primary or secondary? (2) Are domain examples the right domain? (3) Is anything flagged [verify]? (4) Would a peer in this domain recognize the sources?
- **Don't teach citation evaluation in the abstract.** Use the ai-for-designers pantry's actual entries. Show one strong (a specific Adobe Firefly release note with date and version) and one weak (a Medium post summarizing trends).
- **Make "supplement vs. accept vs. return to Tic TOC" a decision diagram.** Three branches with concrete triggers. Designers respond to flowcharts.

## 8. Open Questions and Research Gaps

- **What does the current Chapter Research Gatherer prompt actually return?** Need a real run on a real pantry to ground the worked example. Open Question 1 in TIKTOC.md (ai-for-designers running example) covers this.
- **What is the exact contract between TIKTOC.md and the Gatherer?** Which fields does the Gatherer read? The chapter must answer to make the "weak TIKTOC.md → thin pantry" argument concrete.
- **How does `_lib_` actually get copied / referenced?** Symlink? Direct copy? The mechanism matters for the reader who wants to update a shared note once.
- **Is there a recommended max pantry size per chapter?** Too long defeats "lost in the middle"; too short defeats the point. Empirical answer would strengthen the chapter.
- **Does the Gatherer cite sources with URLs or with full bibliographic entries?** This determines what evaluation actually looks like.

## 9. Sourcing Notes

Luhmann and Ahrens are well-sourced and citation-ready. Goddard 2023 and Bhattacharyya 2023 are both indexed in PubMed; cite both for the LLM-hallucination rate evidence. Bender's "Stochastic Parrots" is the most-cited paper in AI ethics; do not skip it. Cooper 1982 and PRISMA 2020 are stable academic references. Caulfield's SIFT is a blog method but widely adopted in library instruction; cite his work *Web Literacy for Student Fact-Checkers* (2017, CC-BY) for academic respectability. CRAAP is a teaching tool, not a research paper — cite it as such. Daston & Galison's *Objectivity* (Zone Books, 2007) is the canonical citation. Garfield's *Citation Indexing* (1979) is the source text but a 2006 retrospective in JASIST is more accessible.

Avoid citing LinkedIn posts, productivity-influencer Substacks, or "best ChatGPT prompts" listicles as sources for note-taking practice. They are the noise the chapter teaches the reader to filter.
