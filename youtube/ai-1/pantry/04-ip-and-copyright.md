# IP and Copyright: What You Can and Cannot Give a Client

*You cannot assign rights you do not have.*

**One-line capability:** Readers learn the nemo dat principle, the platform IP risk hierarchy, and how to add the 5-Clause AI Rider to their client agreements this week.

## Learning Outcomes

By the end of this chapter, you will be able to:

- **Understand** the nemo dat principle in plain language and apply it to client IP assignments.
- **Analyze** whether an AI-assisted deliverable has identifiable human authorship.
- **Evaluate** platform risk using a current-state IP hierarchy.
- **Create** draft AI rider language for disclosure, tool use, human authorship, confidentiality, and warranty limits.

## Opening Case

A designer delivers an AI-generated logo. The client pays $3,000. The presentation looked good. The mockups looked real. The designer sends the final PNGs and moves on.

Later, the client asks for the source vector file and registration support. The designer has no vector construction. The original prompt does not reproduce the same image. There is no clear record of human modification beyond selecting a favorite output.

Now the client asks the question that should have been asked before delivery:

"What exactly did we buy?"

This opening is illustrative. It compresses several real legal issues into one scenario. Copyright registration, trademark registration, and contract assignment are not the same thing. Do not blur them in final publication. The practical problem, however, is real: if you promise ownership you cannot transfer, the client relationship is exposed.

## Core Content Blocks

### 1. Nemo dat, in plain language

Nemo dat quod non habet means "no one gives what they do not have."

In client language: if you do not own the rights, you cannot transfer those rights to the client.

Most design agreements include some form of intellectual property assignment or license. The client expects that when they pay for the final logo, campaign, or system, they receive the rights described in the contract. AI complicates that promise because some AI-generated material may not be copyrightable, and some platform terms may give use rights without giving copyright ownership.

The U.S. Copyright Office's 2023 guidance states that copyright protects human authorship and that material generated by AI may need to be disclaimed in registration contexts (U.S. Copyright Office, 2023). Its 2025 copyrightability report continues to center human creative contribution (U.S. Copyright Office, 2025).

**Practical rule:** Before promising assignment, identify what human-authored expression you actually own.

### 2. The human authorship test

The question is not "Did I work hard on the prompt?" The question is "Who determined the expressive elements?"

Human authorship is stronger when the designer:

- Draws or rebuilds the mark manually.
- Creates custom typography or meaningful modifications.
- Selects and arranges elements creatively.
- Edits AI material into a new human-authored work.
- Documents the process and source files.

Human authorship is weaker when the designer:

- Prompts only.
- Selects one output with minimal changes.
- Upscales and exports a generated image.
- Cannot reproduce or edit the source.
- Cannot identify what expressive choices were human-made.

In *Thaler v. Perlmutter*, courts affirmed refusal of copyright registration for a work identified as autonomously generated by an AI system, reinforcing that copyright requires human authorship (Thaler v. Perlmutter, 2023; D.C. Circuit, 2025) [verify court posture before publication].

**Practical rule:** Prompting alone is not a safe basis for promising copyright ownership.

### 3. Platform IP risk hierarchy

Platform risk changes. Use this hierarchy as a current-state decision aid, not permanent law.

| Relative risk | Example use | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Lower | Licensed/trained-for-commercial-use tools with enterprise protections, such as some Adobe Firefly enterprise contexts | Better training-data posture and possible indemnification, but terms matter [contested — see pantry flag]. |
| Medium | Paid Midjourney exploration kept out of final deliverables or rebuilt by the designer | Useful exploration, but output ownership, public generation, and authorship remain separate questions. |
| Higher | Open or custom Stable Diffusion workflows using unclear models, LoRAs, or web-trained material | License, training-data, and output-similarity risks vary widely. |
| Highest | Final client logos generated directly from any tool without human reconstruction | Authorship and assignment risk are central. |

Adobe markets Firefly around licensed Adobe Stock and public-domain training data and offers indemnification options for some enterprise customers (Adobe Firefly Enterprise materials, current-state source). Midjourney's terms grant users rights subject to plan and conditions, but platform use rights are not the same as copyright authorship (Midjourney Terms of Service, current-state source). Litigation involving Stability AI and Getty Images shows that training-data and output issues remain active and jurisdiction-dependent (Getty Images v. Stability AI, ongoing/current-state source).

**Practical rule:** "Commercial use allowed" does not mean "copyright safely assignable."

### 4. The 5-Clause AI Rider

Add AI-specific language to your client agreement. Have counsel review it before relying on it.

| Clause | Plain-language purpose |
|---|---|
| AI-use disclosure | Says when and how AI tools may be used. |
| Permitted tools and data | Defines which tools are allowed and what client material may be uploaded. |
| Human-authorship warranty limits | Promises only what you can actually stand behind. |
| Third-party claims and indemnity | Allocates risk if an AI-related claim arises. |
| Confidentiality and retention | Protects client material from improper tool use or storage. |

Drafting posture:

> Designer may use AI-assisted tools for research, ideation, production support, and non-final exploration unless prohibited in writing. Designer will not knowingly submit confidential client materials to AI systems that train on user inputs or retain data for model improvement without client approval. Final deliverables will identify material AI-generated components where such use affects ownership, registration, confidentiality, or public representation. [verify with counsel]

This is not legal advice. It is a starting point for counsel and client conversation.

**Practical rule:** Put AI use in the contract before the project becomes uncomfortable.

### 5. Past deliverables

Do not panic. Do not conceal. Do not volunteer legal conclusions you cannot support.

For past AI-assisted work:

1. Identify which deliverables included AI-generated material.
2. Check whether the AI material appears in final client assets.
3. Locate source files and process records.
4. Identify human modifications.
5. Review the contract promise.
6. If ownership or registration matters, refer the client to counsel.

**Practical rule:** For past work, document first, diagnose second, communicate carefully third.

## Worked Example

**Situation:** Your standard agreement says: "Designer assigns all right, title, and interest in final deliverables to Client upon final payment."

You now use AI for concept exploration and occasional image generation.

### Before

The clause assumes you own everything in the final deliverable. It does not say what happens if material is AI-generated, licensed from a platform, not copyrightable, or only partially human-authored.

### After: practical rider structure

**AI use:** "Designer may use AI-assisted tools for ideation, internal exploration, and production support."

**Confidentiality:** "Designer will not input Client confidential information into AI tools that retain or train on user-submitted data without Client's prior written approval."

**Final deliverables:** "Designer will disclose material AI-generated components included in final deliverables where such inclusion may affect ownership, registration, licensing, or public representation."

**Human authorship:** "Designer warrants human authorship only for elements created, selected, arranged, edited, or otherwise contributed by Designer to the extent protectable under applicable law."

**Counsel threshold:** "Client is responsible for trademark clearance, registration strategy, and legal determinations regarding protectability unless separately agreed in writing."

**The lesson:** The AI rider narrows the promise to what you can responsibly give.

**The limit:** Contract language cannot make uncopyrightable material copyrightable. It only manages expectations, disclosure, and risk.

## Common Misconceptions

**"If I pay for the tool, I own the output."**  
You may have use rights under platform terms. That is not the same as copyright authorship.

**"Adobe Firefly is definitely safe."**  
It may be lower risk in some contexts, especially where enterprise terms and indemnification apply, but it does not erase human authorship or client-contract questions [contested — see pantry flag].

**"Prompting counts as authorship because I made creative choices."**  
Maybe some human contribution exists in the overall process, but current U.S. guidance focuses on human control over expressive elements, not effort alone (U.S. Copyright Office, 2023; 2025).

**"My existing contract covers this."**  
Most older design agreements were not written for AI-generated material, tool retention, training data, or mixed authorship.
