### CHAPTER 7: Faster and Faster
**Core Claim:** Social tools not only lower coordination costs but increase coordination speed, creating new forms of group action — flash mobs, rapid protest — that repressive states cannot interdict in advance.

**Supporting Evidence:**
- Leipzig 1989: information cascade from small protest to 400,000-person march and GDR collapse — demonstrates the logic of shared awareness at three levels (everyone knows; everyone knows everyone knows; everyone knows everyone knows everyone knows)
- Belarus Minsk flash mobs: ice cream eating, book reading, smiling — state police arrested people for each — demonstrating that the threat lies in coordinated presence, not the specific behavior
- Northwest Airlines Flight 1829 (1999): passengers stranded 7 hours, direct call to CEO's home produced a gate — but resulted in no systemic change
- American Airlines Austin tarmac delays (2006): Kate Hanni organized Coalition for an Airline Passengers' Bill of Rights within days, congressional lobbying within weeks, JetBlue CEO resignation and passenger bill of rights adoption
- HSBC graduate overdraft reversal: Facebook group of students threatening to move accounts reversed a policy HSBC had formally announced

**Logical Method:** Comparative case pairs (1999 airline vs. 2006 airline; Leipzig 1989 vs. Belarus 2006) isolating the variable of social tool availability.

**Logical Gaps:**
- The Leipzig case is presented as the logical ancestor of flash mob coordination, but the mechanisms are distinct. Leipzig worked through an information cascade over months; flash mobs work through rapid pre-event coordination. The "shared awareness" framework applies to both, but the speed and reversibility differ fundamentally.
- The Belarus analysis is acute but the conclusion is notably uncertain. Shirky himself notes that flash mobs "may not succeed in toppling the government" and that Western commitment to Belarusian democratization was limited. Given this honest hedging, the chapter's framing — that social tools fundamentally shift the power balance — overstates what the evidence shows.
- The airline comparison (1999 vs. 2006) is the most directly probative case in the chapter. But it conflates the effect of social tools with the effect of a single motivated organizer (Kate Hanni). The tool enabled Hanni's action; whether a similar outcome would have occurred without Hanni's energy and skill is unknowable.

**Methodological Soundness:** The comparative case design is the chapter's strength. The Leipzig historical analysis is solid. The Belarus cases are more illustrative than probative. The airline comparison is the strongest contemporary evidence.
