# BOOKMAP: Data-ism: The Revolution Transforming Decision Making, Consumer Behavior, and Almost Everything Else
**Steve Lohr (2015) | Harper Business**

---

## PART 1: SECTION-BY-SECTION LOGICAL MAPPING

---

### SECTION 1: How Big Is Data? (Chapter 1)
**Core Claim:** Big data is transitioning from a phenomenon of the consumer internet into the physical and institutional world—healthcare, logistics, agriculture—and in doing so, it is transforming not just efficiency but epistemology: the way decisions are made and should be made.

**Supporting Evidence:**
- McKesson reduced inventory by $1 billion (~13% efficiency gain) by mining product, location, and transport sensor data
- Emory University ICU generates 160,000 data points per second across 20 beds; doctors make approximately 100 decisions per patient per day; software trained on vital-sign data streams is being developed to provide GPS-style early warning of patient deterioration
- 90% of all data in recorded history was created in the prior two years (as of the book's writing); IDC estimated 4.4 zettabytes in 2014
- IBM's Watson unit received a $1 billion investment commitment; IBM realigned 3,000 researchers to prioritize big data projects in 2014

**Logical Method:** Opening juxtaposition of two cases—industrial logistics and medical care—to establish that the phenomenon is not confined to consumer internet. Framing via Bloom's "dataism" concept, then rapid historical contextualization back to Herman Hollerith's 1890 census punch cards.

**Logical Gaps:**
- The 90% figure for recent data creation is introduced without source or methodological basis. It is likely true of stored digital data volume but conflates new data creation with image/video file accumulation, which Lohr himself immediately critiques ("not all data is created equal"). The number does the rhetorical work of scale-establishment while the text partially undercuts it.
- "Making the invisible visible" (McKesson executive Donald Walker) is treated as a straightforward win. The chapter doesn't ask whether making more visible also means more opaque in a different sense—whether algorithmic optimization of inventory displaces the tacit knowledge of experienced distribution managers in ways that create new fragilities.
- The Emory ICU case is vivid but the chapter presents the early-warning system as a research project under development, not a deployed success. The framing implies more resolution than the evidence warrants.

**Methodological Soundness:** The chapter functions as advocacy for the book's thesis, not as analysis. The two case studies are illustrative, not probative. The distinction between big data as "tool" and "philosophy" (dataism) is introduced but not yet examined critically.

---

### SECTION 2: Potential, Potential, Potential (Chapter 2 — Hammerbacher Biography)
**Core Claim:** Jeffrey Hammerbacher's career trajectory—from factory worker's son in Indiana to quant at Bear Stearns to data team founder at Facebook to medical AI researcher at Mount Sinai—illustrates both the social mobility of the data economy and the substantive argument that data science should be directed at problems of genuine human importance rather than ad optimization.

**Supporting Evidence:**
- Hammerbacher coined "data scientist" at Facebook (2006–2008), co-founded Cloudera; Cloudera valued at $4.1 billion in April 2014 on Intel investment
- Hammerbacher's own critique: "The best minds of my generation are thinking about how to make people click ads. That sucks." (Bloomberg Businessweek, 2011)
- His reading list exceeds 1,100 books; described by former colleague Adam DeAngelo as unusually extroverted for someone with deep technical skill; described by Rothschild as entrepreneurially self-confident
- Hammerbacker's educational trajectory: failed all Harvard sophomore finals, was fired from two jobs, returned to Harvard after a year of factory and retail work—what the book frames as a "turning point"

**Logical Method:** Full biographical narrative—an extended character study used to personify and humanize the abstract concept of the data-first mindset. Hammerbacher is the book's representative individual.

**Logical Gaps:**
- The biography is engaging but its argumentative function is muddled. Is Hammerbacker evidence that the data economy is a meritocracy? That exceptional people drive technological eras? That directing data science toward medicine is better than ad targeting? The chapter implies all three without establishing any rigorously.
- Survivorship bias is unacknowledged. Hammerbacker's unconventional path worked out spectacularly. The chapter provides no evidence about the typical outcomes of similar profiles—brilliant, non-conformist, institutionally resistant data people who did not happen to arrive at Facebook in 2006 and did not have Rothschild introduce them to VCs.
- His critique of consumer internet ad targeting is presented sympathetically but without the counterargument Lohr later makes (printing press → religious tracts → democratization of knowledge). Both arguments are in the book, but in different sections without direct engagement.

**Methodological Soundness:** A profile piece, not an argument. The rhetorical function is character-building for the book's human throughline. The logical contribution to the central thesis is indirect.

---

### SECTION 3: Bet the Company (Chapter 3 — IBM and the Smarter Planet)
**Core Claim:** IBM's strategic bet on big data—formalized through the "Smarter Planet" marketing campaign in 2008 and later crystallized into the Watson business unit—represents both an institutional commitment to data-driven decision-making and an honest test case of its limits: the decision to launch "Smarter Planet" was itself not data-driven, but intuitive.

**Supporting Evidence:**
- IBM invested $25 billion+ in data analytics acquisitions since 2005; 40,000 employees in the data business by early 2014
- The "Smarter Planet" concept was launched with zero market testing (Virginia Rometty's own admission); Paul Palmisano called it a "judgment call"
- IBM's Center for Business Optimization (est. 2004 under William Pulleyblank) documented pilot projects in road pricing (Stockholm: 25% traffic reduction, +40,000 mass transit riders), accident prediction (Singapore), Medicaid fraud detection (New York State), and cold chain tracking (Norway)
- Rometty: "We are betting the company on this." IBM sold its low-end server business to Lenovo for $2.3 billion in 2014 (that line had $4B in annual revenue)
- In fall 2014, IBM abandoned its 2015 profit target amid declining revenues; new businesses showed growth but insufficient scale

**Logical Method:** Institutional case study + dual historical framing (IBM's century-long history of adaptation; the 1958 Hans Peter Luhn paper as origin of "business intelligence" concept).

**Logical Gaps:**
- The core paradox Lohr surfaces is genuinely valuable: Palmisano's decision to launch "Smarter Planet" was made on intuition, not data. Lohr notes this explicitly but does not resolve the logical implication—that the most important decisions may systematically lie outside the reach of the big data framework being advocated.
- IBM's "we're betting the company" framing is taken largely at face value. The evidence presented (falling revenue, abandoned profit targets, insufficient scale in new businesses) would suggest the bet was losing as of publication. Lohr acknowledges this but frames it as a transition problem, not a thesis-challenging result.
- The Smarter Planet campaign is credited with "lifting the sales of rivals" (SAS CEO James Goodnight: "IBM gins up the demand, and we get the sales"). This is a significant admission—the principal beneficiary of IBM's most celebrated marketing campaign may have been competitors—that the text handles lightly.

**Methodological Soundness:** The IBM case is the book's richest institutional case study. The tension between the advocacy framing and the evidence of struggle is the most analytically honest tension in the book.

### SECTION 4: Site and Insight (Chapter 4 — McKesson and Denihan Hotels)
**Core Claim:** The near-term value of big data is less in sophisticated AI and more in the "more mundane realm of counting, monitoring, and seeing things with greater clarity." Decisions fortified by data outperform experience and intuition, but the appropriate division of labor assigns data the role of disciplined assistant to human judgment.

**Supporting Evidence:**
- McKesson: centralized inventory of high-cost cancer/antibiotic drugs improved on-time delivery to 99% (from <90%) while cutting inventory by 50% for that category; overall network inventory reduced by $1 billion
- Denihan Hotels: 300% ROI on a "win-back" program for lapsed customers; general manager Brian Gelick reports "double-digit" productivity and revenue improvement (estimated, not audited)
- Steve Jobs anecdote: "no market research" behind the iPad; decision-making as cultivated intuition, not data extraction
- Kahneman's "thinking fast and slow" framework applied: fast/System 1 thinking navigates daily life efficiently but is error-prone; data systems should act as "over-the-shoulder critics" supplying slow/System 2 perspective
- Philip Tetlock's superforecasters: quantified, collaborative forecasting with continuous accuracy tracking improves human prediction within time horizons of ~1 year

**Logical Method:** Paired case studies (industrial logistics + hospitality) + psychological framing (Kahneman + Tetlock) to establish that data enhances but does not replace human judgment.

**Logical Gaps:**
- The Denihan "double-digit improvement" estimate is the general manager's own assessment, not an audited finding. Lohr explicitly notes that Denihan never conducted a cost-benefit analysis of its data investment. Presenting this as a success case without that caveat being foregrounded is analytically inconsistent with the book's stated rigor.
- The Jobs anecdote is used to illustrate the virtue of cultivated intuition, but Jobs is an extreme outlier in product judgment (and also happened to be deeply wrong about market research in some documented cases). Treating Jobs as a model for how intuition works is a survivorship bias problem.
- The Kahneman framing is used in service of "data as assistant" but Kahneman's own work suggests that the value of data-based nudges is largest precisely when human experts resist them—not when they welcome data as a collaborative tool. The chapter underplays the human resistance to data-informed correction that Kahneman's research consistently documents.

**Methodological Soundness:** The McKesson case is the most rigorously documented case study in the book. The Denihan case is suggestive but unverified. The psychological framing is appropriately hedged.

---

### SECTION 5: The Rise of the Data Scientist (Chapter 5 — Facebook and Cloudera)
**Core Claim:** Data science emerged not as a predefined discipline but as an organizational need at web-scale companies that required people who could combine deep quantitative skill with broad intellectual curiosity. Facebook under Hammerbacher operationalized A/B testing, Hadoop adoption, and measurement infrastructure that became industry standard. Cloudera's mission was to democratize this toolkit.

**Supporting Evidence:**
- Hammerbacher's team at Facebook grew from 0 to ~30 people in ~2.5 years (2006–2008); introduced A/B testing at Facebook
- Hadoop: in spring 2006, sorted 2TB of data in ~2 days; by 2008, same job in minutes (3-order-of-magnitude improvement)
- Hive (developed under Hammerbacher at Facebook): open source SQL-like query layer on Hadoop enabling non-engineers to query large datasets
- Cloudera valued at $4.1 billion (2014); employee #8 was Philip Zeifman, formerly of D.E. Shaw and Google
- The term "data scientist" coined by Hammerbacker and his team: a merger of "data analyst" and "research scientist" job titles at Facebook
- John Tukey identified as founding data scientist; credited with coining "bit" and first published use of "software" (1958)
- Three universities (UW, NYU, Berkeley) received $38M joint grant from Moore and Sloan foundations in 2013 for data science programs

**Logical Method:** Historical genealogy of data science (Tukey 1962 → Cleveland 2001 → Hammerbacher 2006) + institutional narrative of Facebook's data infrastructure build-out.

**Logical Gaps:**
- The claim that Hammerbacher "coined" the term data scientist requires scrutiny. The book acknowledges that the origins of data science "reached back half a century or more" and that Cleveland used the term in 2001. The more defensible claim is that Hammerbacker popularized the term at scale through Facebook's visibility and his own public profile—not that he invented it.
- The Hadoop performance improvement (2TB in 2 days → 2TB in minutes within two years) is presented without source or verification. It may be accurate but is a significant claim stated as fact.
- The democratic framing ("bringing big data power tools to the rest of the economy") for Cloudera is not tested against evidence. As of 2014, Cloudera's customers were primarily large enterprises and tech companies—the "rest of the economy" was largely unreached. The aspiration is real; the achievement is projected.

**Methodological Soundness:** The intellectual genealogy is the chapter's strongest contribution. The institutional narrative of Facebook's data infrastructure is well-documented. The democratic ambition is aspiration, not demonstrated achievement.

### SECTION 6: Data Storytelling — Correlation and Context (Chapter 6)
**Core Claim:** Correlation is powerful and sufficient for many commercial decisions, but its limits are real and dangerous when mistaken for understanding. The progression from correlation → association → cause-and-effect represents a ladder of comprehension that requires both data and theory. Neither alone is adequate.

**Supporting Evidence:**
- Google Flu Trends (2008): accurately predicted H1N1 spread in 2009; overestimated flu prevalence by ~2x in January 2012–13 peak (reported 11% vs. CDC's 6%); a 2014 Science paper found the service "consistently overestimated" across a 2+ year period; even after the 2013 algorithm update, overshot by ~30%
- ZestFinance: machine learning for payday loan underwriting reduced default rates by 50% compared to typical payday lenders; 45,000 data signals; example signals: length of current cell phone number tenure, case style of name entry, "walking dead" borrowers (appear dead in credit bureau records) default at lower rates than average
- Walmart Pop Tarts and Beer case: consumers in hurricane paths bought strawberry Pop Tarts at 7x normal rate; beer was the best-selling pre-hurricane item; inventory adjusted without causal explanation
- Google Flu Trends overestimation attributed to "Big Data Hubris" (Nature, 2013): implicit assumption that big data sets trump traditional data collection
- IBM's Nell (Carnegie Mellon): scanned hundreds of millions of web pages; 2.3M facts accumulated with estimated 87% accuracy; required human correction sessions every few weeks
- Watson initially answered "Who was the first woman astronaut?" with "Wonder Woman" due to inability to separate fictional from historical text references

**Logical Method:** Inductive case studies across domains (public health, consumer credit, retail inventory) + IBM/CMU technology cases + synthesis through Kahneman/Tetlock/Farouci frameworks to build toward the "measurements plus models" conclusion.

**Logical Gaps:**
- The ZestFinance correlations (typing case style, phone number tenure) are presented as effective without examining their disparate impact on protected classes. The book is silent on whether proxy variables in credit scoring that correlate with demographic characteristics constitute legally or ethically problematic discrimination—a live regulatory and civil rights debate at the time of writing.
- The Google Flu Trends case is the chapter's strongest methodological contribution, but the analysis stops at "missed context." The more fundamental problem—that the algorithm was trained on historical search-flu correlations that broke down when media-driven health anxiety decoupled search behavior from actual illness—is described but not used to derive a generalizable principle about when correlation-based systems fail (specifically: when the behavioral substrate generating the signal changes).
- David Ferrucci's claim that "in a purely data-driven approach, there is no real understanding" is presented as wisdom but is contested. The chapter doesn't engage with the opposing view that "understanding" may be an unnecessary theoretical luxury in prediction tasks where calibrated correlations work reliably.

**Methodological Soundness:** The strongest analytical chapter in the book. The Google Flu Trends failure case is handled with appropriate rigor. The Brahe-Kepler analogy (measurements without theory vs. theory without measurements) is apt.

---

### SECTION 7: Data Gets Physical (Chapter 7 — Agriculture and GE Industrial Internet)
**Core Claim:** Big data is moving from the digital world into physical systems—vineyards, jet engines, power turbines—where the underlying laws of physics provide a more tractable modeling substrate than human behavior. Sensor-enabled physical systems can achieve measurable efficiency gains: the Gallo vineyard produced 25% more grapes at higher quality in the pilot. GE's industrial internet strategy projects $10–15 trillion in added global economic value over 20 years.

**Supporting Evidence:**
- Gallo/IBM vineyard pilot (2013): two 10-acre sections, same variety; precision-managed section yielded 25% more grapes at higher quality; used satellite imaging (Landsat resolution), soil sensors, GPS-equipped harvesting tractors, National Weather Service data, USDA data
- Precision system costs ~4x standard approach in the pilot (requires extra human oversight to run); commercialization dependent on technology cost reduction
- Climate Corporation (founded by ex-Google engineers) sold to Monsanto in 2013 for ~$1 billion based on big data weather prediction for crop insurance
- GE's San Ramon software center: 800 staff as of early 2014; $1 billion planned investment by 2015
- GE report (Annunziata and Evans, 2013): industrial internet could add $10–15T to global economy over 20 years; 300 million labor hours per year spent servicing power turbines, aircraft engines, locomotives, and medical scanners at $20 billion annual cost; moving toward "zero unplanned downtime" via predictive maintenance
- 1% improvement in aircraft fuel efficiency = ~$3 billion in annual savings for the commercial aviation industry
- Robert Gordon (Northwestern) counter-argument: computing and internet productivity gains "peaked out" in early 2000s; innovation since then is mainly in consumer electronics, not fundamental productivity drivers

**Logical Method:** Two case studies (agriculture and industrial equipment) + economic forecasting + Gordon skeptic counterpoint + Brynjolfson/McAfee moderate optimist framing.

**Logical Gaps:**
- The Gallo vineyard 25% yield increase is a single-season, 10-acre pilot. The authors acknowledge it requires scaling ("can it be done across 10,000 or 20,000 acres?") but frame the pilot as validation of commercial potential. A single-season experiment at demonstration scale with extra human oversight is a hypothesis, not a finding.
- GE's $10–15 trillion economic value projection is produced by GE's own chief economist and director of global strategy—a structural conflict of interest the book does not flag. The projection covers 20 years and contains no confidence interval, no downside case, and no methodology beyond the assertion that "small gains across large installed bases compound."
- The Gordon rebuttal is presented fairly but then dismissed by noting that "artificial intelligence" does not appear in his paper. This is accurate but does not engage Gordon's underlying argument: that the productivity contribution of any technology must be measured in output statistics, not in the technological impressiveness of the demonstrations. Krugman's "tentative conversion" is used as a counterweight, but Krugman explicitly frames it as tentative.

**Methodological Soundness:** The vineyard case is the most detailed, best-evidenced physical case study in the book. The industrial internet section relies heavily on self-reported GE projections. The economic debate is framed fairly but resolved too easily in the optimist's favor.

### SECTION 8: The Yin and Yang of Behavior and Data (Chapter 8 — Nest and Retail)
**Core Claim:** As intelligent machines extend from the digital realm into physical environments and consumer behavior, the central challenge shifts from technical to psychological: when will people trust algorithms to make decisions on their behalf? The answer involves both design (the Nest green leaf), accumulated experience, and the machines' ability to explain themselves.

**Supporting Evidence:**
- Nest learning thermostat: infrared sensors detect human presence and thermostat adjustments; within one week, categorizes household into one of four groups (young children, older children, empty nesters, roommates); early prototype "took control" automatically and was rejected by testers; revised to green leaf nudge system
- Nest homeowners who installed the learning thermostat "most of the time let the Nest algorithms take over" (checking temperatures twice daily on average via smartphone)
- Google acquired Nest in January 2014 for $3.2 billion cash
- Macy's/IBM millennial research: 40 million Twitter, Facebook, and blog posts from 18–30-year-olds over 3 months; 12 million authors winnowed to 2.3 million; 100+ professional bloggers identified; customer categories built from phrases indicating lifestyle, intent, and need
- Haydock's data SWAT team: core of 3 people (Haydock + Paul Rydall programmer + Kevin Keane data analyst/social media)
- VALS (SRI International, 1977): 9 personality types; described as the state of the art before data science made fine-grained segmentation possible
- Danny Hillis (Applied Minds): "The algorithms have to explain themselves... the key thing that will make it work and make it acceptable is storytelling"

**Logical Method:** Product case study (Nest) + B2C data science case (Macy's/Starbucks research) + behavioral/trust framework synthesis.

**Logical Gaps:**
- The Nest "most of the time let the algorithms take over" finding is reported without a source, survey methodology, or sample size. It is presented as established fact but may be internal Nest data unavailable for external verification.
- The Google acquisition framing is presented as validation of Nest's data thesis. But the $3.2 billion valuation for a company with 300 employees and consumer thermostat hardware does not straightforwardly follow from the data value argument—it could equally reflect Google's strategic interest in the home network as a data collection surface, which raises the privacy concerns that Lohr explicitly defers to a later chapter.
- Haydock's discussion of retailer data science does not engage with the accuracy problem: social media text mining for consumer intent is a notoriously noisy signal. The "30 million messages → 2.3 million relevant authors" filtering process is described but its precision (true positive rate) is never examined. The business results of the Macy's or Starbucks projects are explicitly withheld due to confidentiality.

**Methodological Soundness:** The Nest design story (green leaf as behavioral nudge vs. autocratic thermostat) is the chapter's strongest contribution—a concrete, documented case study of how algorithm trust is built. The retail section is largely aspirational in the absence of verifiable outcomes.

### BRIDGE: Synthesizing the Logical Architecture

The book's argumentative structure is built on a central claim that generates a series of internal tensions it never fully resolves.

**The Central Claim:** Decisions should increasingly be made based on data and analysis rather than experience and intuition—this is the normative core of "dataism." The book presents this as both a descriptive trend and a prescriptive imperative.

**Three cross-cutting tensions:**

*Tension 1: The Most Important Decisions Lie Outside the Data.* The book's most analytically honest moment is Paul Palmisano's admission about "Smarter Planet": "If it didn't take judgment, the computer could do it." This is a concession that the high-stakes, high-uncertainty decisions—the ones that matter most—are precisely the decisions data cannot make. The book never confronts the implication: if data-driven decision-making works best for optimizable, measurable, repeatable decisions, and those decisions are already being made fairly well by experienced humans, then the marginal value of data may be concentrated at the wrong end of the decision-importance distribution.

*Tension 2: Correlation Versus Understanding.* Chapter 6 makes the case against correlation supremacy, and Ferrucci's "measurements plus models" synthesis is the book's intellectual high point. But chapters 4, 5, and 8 repeatedly celebrate correlation-based wins (ZestFinance, Walmart Pop Tarts, Nest's household categorization) without engaging the causal questions. The book effectively argues that correlation is sufficient for some things and insufficient for others, but never establishes a principled criterion for distinguishing the cases in advance of failure.

*Tension 3: Democratization vs. Concentration.* Hammerbacher's explicit mission was to democratize big data tools through Cloudera and open source. But the actual story the book tells is one of massive concentration: IBM's $25B acquisition spree, Google's $3.2B Nest acquisition, Monsanto's $1B Climate Corporation acquisition. The companies deploying the most powerful data capabilities are the largest and most capitalized. The democratization argument is aspirational; the evidence is oligopoly.

**The paper's most proven claims:**
- Data-driven optimization of measurable, constrained physical systems (pharmaceutical distribution, vineyard irrigation, industrial equipment maintenance) produces documented efficiency gains
- The demand for people who combine quantitative depth with domain breadth ("T-shaped" people) is real and growing
- Correlation-based systems (Google Flu Trends) fail when the behavioral substrate generating the signal decouples from the target variable—particularly under conditions of media-driven behavioral change
- Algorithmic trust by consumers requires both effective design (nudges, explanations) and accumulated experience—autocratic AI is rejected even when correct

**The book's most significant unproven claims:**
- Big data will produce a broad-based productivity lift comparable to electrification or the internet (the economic case depends on GE self-reporting and Brynjolfson's 5–6% productivity premium finding from a 179-company survey)
- Data science tools will be democratized across the economy through open source infrastructure
- Physical system modeling (vineyards, turbines) will scale from pilot to broad commercial reality without loss of the pilot-scale precision

**The book's most significant acknowledged gaps:**
- Privacy and surveillance implications (explicitly deferred to a "later chapter")
- The political economy of who controls and benefits from data concentration
- Whether the productivity gains observable in controlled pilots survive the transition to broad deployment at scale

## PART 2: LITERARY REVIEW ESSAY

---

# The Assistant Who Wants to Drive

There is a paradox at the center of Steve Lohr's *Data-ism*, and to his credit, he places it there deliberately. Near the end of the chapter on IBM's "Smarter Planet" campaign, Lohr reports that Paul Palmisano, the IBM chief executive who bet the company's identity on big data, made the most important decision of that bet—whether to launch the campaign in the weeks after Lehman Brothers collapsed—not on data but on intuition. When Lohr asked him about it years later, Palmisano's explanation was precise: "If it didn't take judgment, the computer could do it."

That sentence is doing more work than Lohr's surrounding prose acknowledges. It is an admission, from the institutional champion of data-driven decision-making, that the decisions most worth making lie systematically outside the reach of data. The optimizable, measurable, repeatable decisions—inventory levels at pharmaceutical distribution centers, vine-by-vine water allocations in California vineyards, room pricing at midsize hotel chains—can be improved by clever software. Palmisano's decision about timing, about what kind of cultural moment demanded a particular kind of public bet, was not one of those decisions. It was a judgment call. He said so himself.

Lohr reports this honestly and then, characteristically, moves on. *Data-ism* is a book that surfaces important tensions and then declines to inhabit them. It is better journalism than argument—rich with well-reported cases, intelligent about technology, genuinely fair-minded about the skeptics—but it stops asking the harder questions at precisely the moment they become most interesting.

---

The book's thesis, stated in its first pages, is that "decisions of all kinds will increasingly be made based on data and analysis, rather than on experience and intuition. More science, then less gut feel." This is presented as both descriptive forecast and normative recommendation. The descriptive part is almost certainly correct. The normative part is what requires examination.

Consider the book's strongest case: McKesson's $1 billion inventory reduction. The pharmaceutical distributor mined product, location, and transport data to build a "flight simulator for decision-making"—a digital replica of its distribution network that could answer what-if questions. The result was a specific insight: centralize high-cost specialty drugs at the Memphis hub, ship by air, cut inventory in half, improve on-time delivery from below 90% to 99%. It worked. The data was clean, the physical system was constrained, the variables were measurable, and the feedback between decision and outcome was tight.

This is the environment in which data-driven decision-making is most powerful. A closed physical system with clean data, measurable outcomes, and a well-defined objective function. McKesson's distribution network satisfies all three conditions. The interesting question—which Lohr never asks—is what fraction of decisions in the economy have these properties. The implication of the McKesson case is not that "decisions of all kinds" should be data-driven. It is that a specific class of decisions, in specific institutional environments, with specific data quality characteristics, benefit enormously from quantitative modeling. That's a more modest and more accurate claim.

---

The chapter on correlation and context is the book's intellectual high point, and not coincidentally, the chapter where Lohr is most willing to sit with difficulty.

Google Flu Trends is the centerpiece, and it earns its place. The service used search query patterns—45 flu-related terms, tracked across billions of searches—to predict flu incidence approximately two weeks ahead of CDC reports. It worked beautifully in 2009, when the H1N1 pandemic spread in the pattern the training data anticipated. It catastrophically failed in 2012–13, estimating nearly double the actual flu prevalence at the January peak. A subsequent Science paper found consistent overestimation across more than two years.

The failure mechanism was precisely what makes the case analytically valuable: when flu became heavily covered in news and social media, people searched for flu information regardless of whether they were ill. The correlation between "flu-related search behavior" and "flu incidence" was real in 2009. It collapsed in 2012–13 because the behavioral substrate generating the signal—why people search for flu-related terms—changed. People were searching because they were anxious, not because they were sick.

Lohr names this as a "missing context" problem and leaves it there. But the generalizable principle is more specific and more alarming: correlation-based systems are brittle when the mechanism generating the signal is subject to social influence. In consumer finance, in public health, in any domain where the act of measuring and publicizing a correlation changes the behavior that generated it, the correlation degrades or inverts. This is a version of Goodhart's Law—when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure—and it is one of the central challenges facing the entire data-driven decision-making enterprise. ZestFinance's correlations (type your name in all caps? higher default risk) are probably stable for now, precisely because borrowers don't know about them. If they became public knowledge, they would immediately be gamed.

Lohr surfaces the pieces of this argument but does not assemble them. The book needed this synthesis more than it needed the eighth chapter on Nest thermostats.

---

The book's most consequential gap is political economy.

The two narrative throughlines—Jeffrey Hammerbacher as the individual, IBM as the institution—tell superficially different stories that converge on the same uncomfortable fact: the democratization of data is a slogan, not a description. Hammerbacher left Facebook because he believed consumer internet companies were wasting the best minds of his generation on ad targeting. He co-founded Cloudera to bring big data tools to "the rest of the economy." He joined Mount Sinai to work on medical AI. These are genuine commitments, and Lohr reports them fairly.

But the actual institutional story the book tells is one of rapid concentration. IBM spent $25 billion acquiring data analytics companies. Google paid $3.2 billion for a startup thermostat company with 300 employees. Monsanto paid $1 billion for a weather prediction startup whose value was entirely in its proprietary model trained on historical agricultural and weather data. Walmart pioneered supply chain data science to build a competitive moat so deep that it contributed to the destruction of the retail industries around it. These are not stories of democratization. They are stories of how superior data access becomes a structural competitive advantage that concentrates wealth and power in the already-large.

Lohr is a New York Times technology reporter, and he is good at the both-sides-have-a-point move. His portrait of Hammerbacher's discomfort with consumer internet ad targeting is followed by the concession that "the early markets for the printing press, in addition to the Gutenberg Bible, were religious tracts, political screeds, and pornography. Only later did the printing press become a vehicle for democratizing knowledge." This is the standard frame for technology optimism: it starts ugly and extractive, then democratizes. But it is not self-evidently true in the data economy. The printing press created a structural shift from scribal monopolies to distributed print shops. The data economy's structural logic—where the value of a data asset increases with scale, and scale requires capital—points toward oligopoly rather than broad distribution.

Lohr flags privacy concerns, then explicitly defers them to a chapter he doesn't write in this excerpt. He acknowledges that Haydock, the IBM retail data scientist, has "the ability to be as creepy as you can possibly imagine." He doesn't ask what it means that the person doing this work is an IBM Distinguished Engineer rather than a regulatory framework.

---

The book's central image of man-machine collaboration is Licklider's 1960 vision: the computer as assistant, augmenting human intelligence rather than substituting for it. Every case study in the book is framed through this lens. The Nest thermostat learns your habits and adjusts your home. IBM's Watson helps doctors find diagnoses they might have missed. McKesson's model gives distribution executives the confidence to make decisions they already knew were right. Data as servant.

But the book's own evidence keeps complicating this frame. Nest prototype users rejected the autocratic version—the thermostat that just made the decision—and Matsuoka redesigned it as a nudge. But Lohr also reports that "most of the time, they let the Nest algorithms take over." The humans "check in" on the decisions. The Nest algorithms make them. This is Licklider's frame inverted: not computer-as-assistant, but human-as-supervisor of an autonomous system. The functional reality is that the humans are ratifying algorithmic decisions most of the time, which means the algorithms are the effective decision-makers. The residual human involvement is less a check on the system and more a ritual of oversight that confers legitimacy.

This is what "the assistant who wants to drive" means. The framing of data science as assistance is politically and psychologically necessary—humans require the narrative of control—but it increasingly mischaracterizes the actual distribution of decision authority. IBM's Sam Adams says it directly in the chapter on data storytelling: the future will be "machines augmented by human experts." Not the other way around. Lohr notes this is "Licklider's vision being flipped," then describes it as "reassuring." This is the book's most significant analytical evasion. The flip is not reassuring. It is the central question of the era the book is writing about.

Lohr is too good a reporter not to have seen it. But *Data-ism* is, finally, a book written in the early years of a transformation, by someone whose instinct is to document rather than prosecute. The cases are real. The tensions are real. The resolution—"the appropriate goal for computing is to augment human intelligence rather than substitute for it"—is a statement of aspiration borrowed from 1960 and applied to 2014 without sufficient examination of the evidence that the aspiration may already be obsolete.

The data economy is moving faster than the philosophy that is supposed to govern it. That is the story *Data-ism* almost tells.
