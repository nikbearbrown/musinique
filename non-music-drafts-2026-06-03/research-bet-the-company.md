### SECTION 3: Bet the Company (Chapter 3 — IBM and the Smarter Planet)
**Core Claim:** IBM's strategic bet on big data—formalized through the "Smarter Planet" marketing campaign in 2008 and later crystallized into the Watson business unit—represents both an institutional commitment to data-driven decision-making and an honest test case of its limits: the decision to launch "Smarter Planet" was itself not data-driven, but intuitive.

**Supporting Evidence:**
- IBM invested $25 billion+ in data analytics acquisitions since 2005; 40,000 employees in the data business by early 2014
- The "Smarter Planet" concept was launched with zero market testing (Virginia Rometty's own admission); Paul Palmisano called it a "judgment call"
- IBM's Center for Business Optimization (est. 2004 under William Pulleyblank) documented pilot projects in road pricing (Stockholm: 25% traffic reduction, +40,000 mass transit riders), accident prediction (Singapore), Medicaid fraud detection (New York State), and cold chain tracking (Norway)
- Rometty: "We are betting the company on this." IBM sold its low-end server business to Lenovo for $2.3 billion in 2014 (that line had $4B in annual revenue)
- In fall 2014, IBM abandoned its 2015 profit target amid declining revenues; new businesses showed growth but insufficient scale

**Logical Method:** Institutional case study + dual historical framing (IBM's century-long history of adaptation; the 1958 Hans Peter Luhn paper as origin of "business intelligence" concept).

**Logical Gaps:**
- The core paradox Lohr surfaces is genuinely valuable: Palmisano's decision to launch "Smarter Planet" was made on intuition, not data. Lohr notes this explicitly but does not resolve the logical implication—that the most important decisions may systematically lie outside the reach of the big data framework being advocated.
- IBM's "we're betting the company" framing is taken largely at face value. The evidence presented (falling revenue, abandoned profit targets, insufficient scale in new businesses) would suggest the bet was losing as of publication. Lohr acknowledges this but frames it as a transition problem, not a thesis-challenging result.
- The Smarter Planet campaign is credited with "lifting the sales of rivals" (SAS CEO James Goodnight: "IBM gins up the demand, and we get the sales"). This is a significant admission—the principal beneficiary of IBM's most celebrated marketing campaign may have been competitors—that the text handles lightly.

**Methodological Soundness:** The IBM case is the book's richest institutional case study. The tension between the advocacy framing and the evidence of struggle is the most analytically honest tension in the book.
