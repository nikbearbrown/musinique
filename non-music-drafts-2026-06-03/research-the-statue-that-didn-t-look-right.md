### INTRODUCTION: The Statue That Didn't Look Right

**Core Claim:** Expert intuition—rapid, unconscious pattern recognition—can outperform months of systematic analysis, as demonstrated by art experts who detected the Getty kouros as a fake in seconds while the museum's scientists and lawyers failed over 14 months.

**Supporting Evidence:**
- Geologist Stanley Margolis conducted electron microscope analysis, X-ray diffraction, and mass spectrometry, concluding the statue was genuine aged dolomite marble
- Federico Zeri, Evelyn Harrison, Thomas Hoving, Georges Dontas, and Angelo Stelvarius all experienced immediate, visceral repulsion upon first viewing
- The statue's supporting documents were subsequently proved fraudulent (postal codes predating their claimed dates, bank accounts opened after the letters referencing them)
- The kouros was ultimately traced to a Rome forgery workshop, validated by a Swiss forgery fragment sharing marble composition and stylistic features

**Logical Method:** Anomaly-driven argument—a counterintuitive case where expert snap judgment proved correct and institutional analysis proved wrong—used to establish the book's thesis.

**Logical Gaps:**
- The sample size is one case. The introduction treats a single high-stakes instance of correct intuition as evidence for a general thesis about the superiority of expert rapid cognition. This is the seed of the book's central methodological problem: the cases are selected because intuition worked, not because they represent a random sample of expert judgment situations.
- The framing elides a crucial distinction: Zeri, Harrison, and Hoving were domain experts with decades of exposure to authentic Greek sculpture. Their "intuition" was compressed expertise. The introduction does not clearly separate expert rapid cognition from lay rapid cognition, a distinction that becomes critical in later chapters.
- The 14-month scientific analysis failed partly because of motivated reasoning (the Getty desperately wanted the statue to be real). This alternative explanation—institutional bias, not the inferiority of deliberate analysis—receives only a brief mention.

**Methodological Soundness:** Rhetorically effective but logically insufficient as a foundation for a general theory. One correct expert intuition proves that expert intuition can be correct, not that it is systematically more reliable than analysis.
