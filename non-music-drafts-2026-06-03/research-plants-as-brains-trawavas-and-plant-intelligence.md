### CHAPTER 7: Plants as Brains — Trawavas and Plant Intelligence
**Core Claim:** Plants exhibit adaptively variable behavior within the lifetime of the individual (Stenhouse's 1974 definition of intelligence), including foraging, competition, communication, and learning via molecular signaling mechanisms similar to those in neurons.

**Supporting Evidence:**
- Anthony Trawavas (University of Edinburgh, FRS): plants have senses for light, water, temperature, chemicals, vibrations, gravity, and sound; respond by changing growth patterns
- Stilt palm: changes location by growing new prop roots toward sunlight, letting shaded roots die—moves over months
- Ground ivy: puts down roots earlier in nutrient-rich patches, skips over poor ground—demonstrates resource sensing and selective foraging
- Daughter (parasitic plant, *Cuscuta*): correctly assesses host nutritional quality within an hour, adjusts coil number accordingly—foraging efficiency equals animal foragers (Kelly, early 1990s)
- Calcium signaling: tobacco plants glow when touched (response in milliseconds) due to calcium elevation—same charged atoms implicated in animal neuron learning
- When plants are threatened (e.g., drought), same molecules (calcium, enzymes) as in animal learning are unleashed; cells modify ion channels, synthesize proteins, change morphology—paralleling memory formation in animals
- Plant intercellular communication signals include RNA transcripts and proteins—"large molecules can handle large amounts of information"

**Logical Method:** Behavioral observation (field and controlled) + molecular biology convergence → attribution of intelligence via Stenhouse's definition.

**Logical Gaps:**
- Stenhouse's 1974 definition—"adaptively variable behavior within the lifetime of the individual"—is invoked as definitive, but it is one of many competing definitions. Using it resolves the debate by definitional fiat rather than by evidence. If one adopts a stricter definition requiring centralized processing or learning from error, the conclusion changes.
- The calcium signaling parallel is real and documented. But "same mechanism" does not mean "same function." Calcium is a ubiquitous signaling ion across all of biology—its presence in both plant responses and animal learning is not surprising and does not establish that plants are doing something equivalent to what neurons do. The inference requires more mechanistic precision.
- Trawavas's claim that "plant communication is likely to be as complex as within a brain" is presented as an expert judgment, not a demonstrated finding. The author himself notes: "it was still not clear to me how and where computation occurs in a plant." This acknowledged gap is significant—if the site and mechanism of plant computation cannot be specified, the intelligence claim remains inferential.
- The daughter plant experiments by Kelly are described in some detail but without the key control: do daughter's correct foraging decisions exceed chance at a statistically significant rate? The claim that "foraging strategies have the same efficacy as animal foragers" is a strong equivalence claim that requires head-to-head comparison studies.

**Methodological Soundness:** Trawavas is a credentialed source (FRS, University of Edinburgh) whose research on calcium signaling is published. His attribution of "intention" to plants, however, moves beyond the molecular data into philosophical territory he himself acknowledged is contested. The chapter's most defensible claim is: plants have molecular mechanisms for sensing and responding to environmental variables that share components with animal learning machinery. The strongest claim—that plants are intelligent—requires more definitional and mechanistic work.
