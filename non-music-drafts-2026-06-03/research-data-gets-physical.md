### SECTION 7: Data Gets Physical (Chapter 7 — Agriculture and GE Industrial Internet)
**Core Claim:** Big data is moving from the digital world into physical systems—vineyards, jet engines, power turbines—where the underlying laws of physics provide a more tractable modeling substrate than human behavior. Sensor-enabled physical systems can achieve measurable efficiency gains: the Gallo vineyard produced 25% more grapes at higher quality in the pilot. GE's industrial internet strategy projects $10–15 trillion in added global economic value over 20 years.

**Supporting Evidence:**
- Gallo/IBM vineyard pilot (2013): two 10-acre sections, same variety; precision-managed section yielded 25% more grapes at higher quality; used satellite imaging (Landsat resolution), soil sensors, GPS-equipped harvesting tractors, National Weather Service data, USDA data
- Precision system costs ~4x standard approach in the pilot (requires extra human oversight to run); commercialization dependent on technology cost reduction
- Climate Corporation (founded by ex-Google engineers) sold to Monsanto in 2013 for ~$1 billion based on big data weather prediction for crop insurance
- GE's San Ramon software center: 800 staff as of early 2014; $1 billion planned investment by 2015
- GE report (Annunziata and Evans, 2013): industrial internet could add $10–15T to global economy over 20 years; 300 million labor hours per year spent servicing power turbines, aircraft engines, locomotives, and medical scanners at $20 billion annual cost; moving toward "zero unplanned downtime" via predictive maintenance
- 1% improvement in aircraft fuel efficiency = ~$3 billion in annual savings for the commercial aviation industry
- Robert Gordon (Northwestern) counter-argument: computing and internet productivity gains "peaked out" in early 2000s; innovation since then is mainly in consumer electronics, not fundamental productivity drivers

**Logical Method:** Two case studies (agriculture and industrial equipment) + economic forecasting + Gordon skeptic counterpoint + Brynjolfson/McAfee moderate optimist framing.

**Logical Gaps:**
- The Gallo vineyard 25% yield increase is a single-season, 10-acre pilot. The authors acknowledge it requires scaling ("can it be done across 10,000 or 20,000 acres?") but frame the pilot as validation of commercial potential. A single-season experiment at demonstration scale with extra human oversight is a hypothesis, not a finding.
- GE's $10–15 trillion economic value projection is produced by GE's own chief economist and director of global strategy—a structural conflict of interest the book does not flag. The projection covers 20 years and contains no confidence interval, no downside case, and no methodology beyond the assertion that "small gains across large installed bases compound."
- The Gordon rebuttal is presented fairly but then dismissed by noting that "artificial intelligence" does not appear in his paper. This is accurate but does not engage Gordon's underlying argument: that the productivity contribution of any technology must be measured in output statistics, not in the technological impressiveness of the demonstrations. Krugman's "tentative conversion" is used as a counterweight, but Krugman explicitly frames it as tentative.

**Methodological Soundness:** The vineyard case is the most detailed, best-evidenced physical case study in the book. The industrial internet section relies heavily on self-reported GE projections. The economic debate is framed fairly but resolved too easily in the optimist's favor.

## Extended Research Notes

**Pantry note:** `pantry/notes-data-gets-physical.md`

Key additions: Physical data systems become persuasive only when they change operations and measured outcomes. A sensor dashboard is not the same as an irrigation, maintenance, or scheduling decision that produces audited ROI.

Settled: Industrial IoT and precision agriculture are real operational categories. Contested: macroeconomic productivity impact, especially when forecasts come from vendors with strategic incentives.

Teaching move: Use a pilot-to-scale evidence ladder: demo, controlled pilot, multi-site replication, production deployment, audited ROI.
