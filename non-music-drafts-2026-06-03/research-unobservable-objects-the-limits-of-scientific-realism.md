### CHAPTER 2: Unobservable Objects — The Limits of Scientific Realism
**Core Claim:** The existence of unobservable entities (atoms, electrons) can be legitimately postulated through "inference to the most probable cause," but this inference is itself grounded in perceptual experience. Accepting that electrons are real does not require, and should not produce, demoting the perceptual world to an epiphenomenal side-show.

**Supporting Evidence:**
- Cloud chamber tracks as perceptual signs of particle existence: "if you can spray them, they are real" (Hacking/Cartwright)
- Whitehead's argument that science presupposes the concrete reality of the perceptual world: "we may not pick and choose"
- Eddington's "Two Tables" — the ordinary table and the scientific table — is cited as a paradigm case of the bifurcation of nature in action
- The distinction between laws (which refer to ideal, non-real objects) and theories (which postulate real unobservable entities) is maintained

**Logical Method:** Philosophical analysis combined with historical case studies of particle detection. The argument distinguishes between legitimate scientific realism (unobservable entities postulated through controlled inference) and illegitimate surreptitious substitution (mathematical abstractions promoted to the status of concrete reality).

**Logical Gaps:**
- The argument that "the red of the sunset is as much a part of nature as the molecules and light waves by which scientists explain it" (Whitehead) is asserted as a principle of natural philosophy but requires more defense. Many physicists would simply deny this on the grounds that color is a brain construction, not a feature of electromagnetic radiation. The book will need to address this directly rather than treating Whitehead's assertion as conclusive.
- The concept of "indicative sign consciousness" (Husserl) is introduced to explain how physicists "see" particle tracks as particle tracks. This is a rich concept but is not developed enough to do the philosophical work it's being asked to do here.

**Methodological Soundness:** Adequate. The chapter carefully limits its claims — it is not arguing against scientific realism, only against a specific form of reductionism that degrades the perceptual world.
