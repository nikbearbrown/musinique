### CHAPTER 3: Independence — Imitation, Information Cascades, and the Limits of Social Learning

**Core Claim:** Independence of judgment is essential to group wisdom. When people observe and imitate each other sequentially, rational individual behavior produces collectively irrational outcomes (information cascades). The chapter defines the conditions under which imitation is beneficial versus destructive.

**Supporting Evidence:**
- Milgram's sky-gazing experiment: one person staring at sky attracts few; fifteen attract 45% of passersby; 80% stop when the cohort is large — demonstrating social proof
- Plank Road Fever (1840s–1850s): 1,000 plank road companies built across the US based on cascading adoption of George Gettys's claim that roads lasted 8 years; actual lifespan was 4 years, making them economically unviable
- The Bikchandani-Hirschleifer-Welch information cascade model: rational agents observing predecessors' choices can rationally discard accurate private information in favor of the crowd's (wrong) signal
- NFL fourth-down strategy (David Romer's analysis of 700 games 1998–2000): coaches kick the ball 992 out of 1,100 times when going for it has positive expected value — a systematic error driven by imitation and risk aversion
- William Sellers's screw standardization campaign (1860s): a deliberate information cascade orchestrated to achieve beneficial standardization
- Iowa hybrid seed corn (Ryan and Gross 1943 study): farmers waited to see neighbors' results before adopting, but tested in small plots rather than converting whole fields

**Logical Method:** Historical case study, mathematical model, empirical sports analysis, and sociological survey.

**Logical Gaps:**
- The cascade model rests on the assumption that decisions are made *sequentially* and that private information is *imperfect*. Surowiecki acknowledges (citing Schiller) that in many real markets, people decide simultaneously rather than sequentially, reducing cascade susceptibility. He does not then explain when sequential decision-making obtains versus when simultaneous decision-making obtains.
- Romer's NFL analysis is genuinely compelling but Surowiecki overstates the interpretation: he says coaches "would have been better off going for it even if the Rams had needed nine yards." Romer's own paper uses league-average data; any specific team's success rate deviates from the mean. The direction of Romer's finding is robust; the specific threshold is not a universal fact.
- The Sellers screw story demonstrates that cascades can be *beneficial* — but Surowiecki does not develop what determines whether a deliberately-triggered cascade produces good versus bad outcomes. The logic requires the instigator to know the right answer. This is exactly the condition that fails in most cascades.

**Methodological Soundness:** The cascade literature is real and well-documented. The chapter makes important conceptual contributions. The weakness is that the conditions distinguishing good imitation from bad are stated as intuitions rather than derived rules.
