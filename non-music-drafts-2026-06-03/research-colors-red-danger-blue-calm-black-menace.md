### CHAPTER 7: Colors — Red Danger, Blue Calm, Black Menace
**Core Claim:** Colors shape human physiology, cognition, performance, social behavior, and moral perception through two mechanisms: direct biological effects (blue light regulating circadian rhythm; red elevating heart rate and blood pressure) and associative effects (red signaling danger/stop/sexuality; blue signaling police/calm; black signaling aggression/evil).

**Supporting Evidence:**

*Blue light biological effects:*
- Montreal sawmill shift-worker study: blue-green light exposure reduced error rates from 5% to 1% over 4 days as workers adapted to shift changes.
- Seasonal affective disorder: SAD affects 1% of Floridians vs. 10% of New Hampshirites; blue-green light therapy shown effective in "dozens" of studies.
- Glasgow blue street lights: crime declined in blue-lit areas (specific statistics not provided).
- Nara Prefecture blue lights: crime fell 9%; zero suicide attempts on train lines 2006–2008.

*Red physiological effects:*
- Red vs. blue light tremor study: both groups shook more under red light; some schizophrenic patients urinated uncontrollably when room turned red.
- Red vs. blue heart rate/blood pressure: heart rate and blood pressure elevated under red vs. blue or white light in normal males.
- Color and walking disorders: woman with cerebellar disease more symptomatic in red dresses than green or blue.
- Red web page loading: users rated red/yellow backgrounds as taking longer to load than blue; reported lower likelihood of recommending site.

*Colors and academic performance:*
- Red pen grading study: students using red pen found 24 errors vs. 19 with blue pen; graded essays 76/100 vs. 80/100.
- Red ID number study: students solved 22% fewer anagrams after writing ID in red vs. black or green pen.
- Red test booklet study: students solved 18% fewer number-string problems and 37% fewer analogy questions with red first page vs. gray, white, or green.
- Vigilance task: students performed better at proofreading and word memorization against red backgrounds (where vigilance, not creativity, is required).

*Colors and romance:*
- French hitchhiking study: 21% of male motorists stopped for red-shirted women vs. 12–14% for other colors; female motorists unaffected.
- Online dating photo study (n=64, 9 months): women received 21% of emails when wearing red vs. 14–16% in other colors.
- Red clothing attractiveness ratings: identical photos rated more sexually attractive in red across American, English, German, and Chinese raters; men moved chairs ~1 foot closer to perceived red-shirted partner.

*Colors and sport:*
- 2004 Athens Olympics: red competitors won 55% of 457 bouts across wrestling, boxing, and Taekwondo; 62% in evenly matched bouts.
- Taekwondo referee study: referees awarded red-uniformed competitors more points (8 vs. 7) even when viewing digitally color-reversed footage of identical performances.
- NHL/NFL black uniforms: black-uniformed teams received more penalties; Pittsburgh Penguins' penalty minutes rose from 8 to 12/game after switching to black uniforms.

*Colors and morality:*
- Stroop task (moral/immoral words in black/white text): people faster to associate virtue words with white text and immoral words with black text; slower for reversed pairings.

**Logical Method:** Biological mechanism → associative mechanism → domain-specific applications (health, academia, romance, sport, morality). The chapter argues that the same color (red) produces different effects across contexts depending on which mechanism dominates.

**Logical Gaps:**
- The Glasgow and Nara blue-light studies are reported as successes without control conditions or attempts to isolate color from brightness or novelty. Crime displacement (to non-lit areas) is mentioned as a concern but not resolved.
- The athletic color studies raise a serious fairness concern that the book names explicitly ("performance-enhancing red uniform") but does not follow to its logical conclusion: if the effect is real and large enough to matter, randomizing color assignment in competitive sports is unjust by design.
- The color-morality (white/black Stroop) findings are from a clean paradigm, but the inference from "processing speed" to "racial bias in real decisions" is a significant leap. The book acknowledges this but continues to treat the findings as explaining "one reason why police officers are more likely to stop... a black man" — a causal claim that the Stroop data cannot support.
- The historical review of color therapy (Roger-Tone films, Felix Deutsch) is appropriately treated as scientifically dubious. But the transition from "bad old color therapy" to "rigorous modern color science" glosses over the fact that the modern studies also have small samples and sometimes single-site replications.

**Methodological Soundness:** The academic performance studies are clean and use within-subject or between-subject designs with proper controls. The athletic color effects are robust across a large real-world dataset. The romantic red effect has two independent replications. The biological blue-light effects are the best-supported, with multiple replications and a clear mechanism.

## Extended Research Notes

**Pantry note:** `pantry/notes-colors-red-danger-blue-calm-black-menace.md`

Key additions: color effects need mechanism separation: biological light effects, associative/cultural priming, and task-specific context. The chapter should foreground effect size and replication before translating lab effects into policy or moral/social claims.

Settled: light affects circadian physiology, and colors can influence perception and behavior. Contested: subtle color-psychology effects, especially broad real-world generalizations.

Teaching move: identify mechanism, control condition, effect size, replication, and practical consequence.
