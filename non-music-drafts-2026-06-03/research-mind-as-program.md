### PART IV: MIND AS PROGRAM

#### Selections 13–15: "Where Am I?" (Dennett); "Where Was I?" (Sanford); "Beyond Rejection" (Leiber)
**Core Claim (composite):** Personal identity cannot be located in a specific physical substrate; it tracks the system of functional organization that generates unified first-person experience. But "unified first-person experience" cannot be fully understood as a feature of programs — the hard problem survives.

**Supporting Evidence:**
- Dennett: radio-linked disembodied brain; experiment with computer duplicate (Hubert); inability to project "here" into the vat; three candidate principles for personal location, all unsatisfactory.
- Sanford: full-body telepresence (eyevideos, skintact, MARS); point of view attached to robot body despite brain remaining in laboratory; location as function of perceptual system.
- Leiber: mind-tape implantation, tuning problem (each brain differently structured), rejection rate, the Flowers for Algernon problem of a mind aged beyond its body's capacity.

**Logical Method:** Imaginative thought experiments → failed location principles → open problem.

**Logical Gaps:**
- Dennett's "Dennett is wherever he thinks he is" principle is acknowledged to be circular: how does he know where he thinks he is? The thought experiment exposes the problem without resolving it.
- The Hubert experiment (brain duplicate operating in perfect synchrony) is itself "outrageous" as Dennett acknowledges — the premise required is effectively miraculous. The philosophical point about functional equivalence is interesting, but it is delivered by a story that cannot, even in principle, be instantiated.
- Leiber's mind-tape scenario requires brain-hardware independence that is biologically implausible. Dennett's Reflections note this clearly. The philosophical work done by an implausible thought experiment is limited.

**Methodological Soundness:** Valuable as explorations of conceptual terrain. The implausibility of the scenarios limits their evidential weight.

#### Selection 16: "Software" (Rucker)
**Core Claim:** Consciousness is a property of organizational pattern, not physical substrate — "software immortality" is possible in principle, and the soul is the pattern, not the matter.

**Logical Gaps:** Rucker's fiction dramatizes the thesis but does not argue for it. Ralph Numbers's self-reflection at the moment of "death" ("What is this that is I?") is moving and philosophically resonant. The Reflections correctly identify the self-symbol problem: having a self-symbol (a representation of the self) is not the same as having consciousness, and the narrative conflates them.

---

#### Selection 17: "The Riddle of the Universe and Its Solution" (Cherniak)
**Core Claim:** There may exist a thought — perhaps a self-referential paradox about the limits of the human cognitive system — that is literally mind-arresting: anyone who understands it cannot continue thinking.

**Supporting Evidence:** The story is a thought experiment, not an empirical report. Its power derives from the Gödel/Whitely structure: a formalized "Whitely sentence" for a given system is a statement that system cannot believe without self-contradiction.

**Logical Gaps:** The story's premises require that a single proposition could simultaneously (a) be understood and (b) produce total cognitive arrest. This conflates the logical structure of a paradox with its psychological effect. Understanding a paradox typically produces confusion or oscillation, not lockup. The "riddle" is more evocative than argued.

**Methodological Soundness:** Functions as an extended meditation on Gödelization rather than an argument. Its contribution is pointing toward the primal loop between self-reference and consciousness.
