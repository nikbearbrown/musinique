### CLUSTER D: IPOs and Public Markets
**Authors:** Jos van Bommel; Hung-Gay Fung; Lena Booth; Janusz Brzeszczynski; Seth Armitage; Wondimu Mekonnen; Meziane Lasfer

**Core Claims (van Bommel):** IPO price discovery is a structured information production process. Book-building dominates because targeted information exchange between informed buyers and the underwriter produces better price discovery than auctions, despite higher cost. Underpricing compensates informed investors for costly analysis. Allocation discretion allows underwriters to reward accurate bidders and discipline free-riders. The Google IPO case: auction method produced 18% underpricing (roughly market average) despite Google's high quality — suggesting the auction failed to fully extract market information.

**Core Claims (Booth, Underpricing):** IPO underpricing averages 18-20% in the U.S. and is observed globally, though magnitudes vary. Underpricing causes "money left on the table" — wealth transfer from original owners to new investors. Theories include signaling, winner's curse mitigation, informed investor compensation, legal liability reduction, ownership dispersion, and media attention effects. Book-building IPOs show higher underpricing than auctions but better post-IPO liquidity and analyst coverage.

**Core Claims (Fung, IPO Process):** The IPO process for SMEs involves selecting underwriters (typically 7% fee), SEC registration, red herring prospectus, roadshows, exchange selection, bookbuilding, and lockup periods (typically 180 days). New price formula: simultaneous determination of offer price and share quantity using old/new asset values and issuance costs. Example: $100M existing firm, $70M raise, $4.9M (7%) direct cost → P_new = $9.51, N = 7,875,920 new shares.

**Core Claims (Brzeszczynski, Emerging Markets):** IPO activity correlates strongly with stock market phases — lagged approximately one year behind market returns. Polish data (1997-2008): correlation of 0.024 simultaneously, but 0.568 with one-year lag. IPOs peak in bull markets and collapse in bear markets. Underpricing is more severe in emerging markets (can exceed 100% first-day return) than in developed markets (<20% typically).

**Core Claims (Armitage, Rights Issues):** Rights issues preserve preemption rights and allow existing stockholders to maintain ownership proportions. Firm commitments, placings, and open offers have replaced rights issues in several markets despite being more expensive and offering no obvious preemption advantage. Bradford & Bingley case: mid-offer price cut from 82p to 55p during 2008 financial crisis — extremely rare event requiring TPG's concurrent private placement to prevent underwriter losses.

**Logical Gaps:**
- Van Bommel's Google IPO analysis — that the auction underperformed book-building — rests on the observable first-day price jump of ~18%. But van Bommel argues Google should have gotten a higher offer price under book-building. This is counterfactual and unverifiable. An equally valid interpretation is that the 18% discount simply equals the market's required compensation for the standard uncertainty of any IPO. The essay presents one interpretation without acknowledging the alternative.
- Booth's underpricing theories are enumerated rather than evaluated. Multiple theories cannot simultaneously be the primary driver of underpricing, yet no hierarchy or evidence weighting is offered. The essay acknowledges this implicitly by presenting them as a list.
- Fung's worked example (P_new = $9.51) is mathematically correct and pedagogically useful — one of the few quantitative derivations in the book that shows full working.
- Brzeszczynski's one-year lag finding from Polish data is interesting and plausible, but a single-country analysis cannot establish the universality of the lag. The essay presents the Polish correlation coefficients (0.024 vs. 0.568) as striking, which they are, without addressing whether this reflects genuine causal lag or data artifacts from Poland's particular transition economy characteristics.

**Methodological Soundness:** Van Bommel's essay is theoretically rigorous and well-structured. Booth's is a competent literature survey. Fung's worked example is the most pedagogically complete in the book. Brzeszczynski's single-country time-series claim should be treated as hypothesis-generating rather than confirmed.

## Extended Research Notes

**Pantry note:** `pantry/notes-cluster-d-ipos-and-public-markets.md`

Key additions: IPO underpricing is persistent but multi-causal. The Google auction case is counterfactual and should not be used as proof that auctions are inferior; bookbuilding, allocation, market windows, and investor information all interact.

Settled: IPO underpricing exists globally and varies by market/institution. Contested: the dominant cause in each context and whether auctions or bookbuilding perform better after selection effects.

Teaching move: ask who benefits from underpricing, what information is produced, and what would happen if the deal were priced higher.
