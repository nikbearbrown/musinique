### CHAPTER 1: How Did We Get Here? The Surreptitious Substitution
**Core Claim:** The blind spot is not science itself but a philosophical worldview grafted onto science, originating with Galileo and reaching maturity in classical physics. Husserl diagnosed this as the "surreptitious substitution" — mistaking the abstract mathematical constructs of physics for concrete reality — and Whitehead identified the resulting error as the "fallacy of misplaced concreteness."

**Supporting Evidence:**
- Six elements of blind spot metaphysics are enumerated: bifurcation of nature, reductionism, objectivism, physicalism, reification of mathematical entities, epiphenomenalism about experience
- Husserl's analysis of thermometry is elaborated: the development of thermodynamic temperature follows an ascending spiral of abstraction from bodily sensation, but the abstraction cannot legitimately replace the concrete sensation it was distilled from
- Whitehead's critique of the bifurcation of nature: "The poets are entirely mistaken. They should address their lyrics to themselves... Nature is a dull affair, soundless, scentless, colourless."
- Francis Bacon's workshop vision and its link to the commodification of nature is introduced

**Logical Method:** Historical-philosophical diagnosis. The authors trace the genealogy of the blind spot elements and show they form a coherent constellation — not a required feature of scientific practice but an optional metaphysics that became entrenched.

**Logical Gaps:**
- The six elements of blind spot metaphysics are not always mutually consistent. Reductionism and physicalism have had vigorous philosophical debate within them; the book treats them as a single block for rhetorical efficiency, but this papers over real distinctions.
- Bacon's colonial and gendered conception of nature's subjugation is introduced but not integrated into the core epistemological argument until the final chapter. This is a structural gap — the political economy strand and the epistemological strand are identified here but not braided.
- Husserl's phenomenological solution is gestured toward but not yet defended. The reader must accept the problem before the solution appears, which is reasonable, but the book will need to provide more than diagnosis.

**Methodological Soundness:** The historical and philosophical analysis is rigorous by the standards of philosophy of science. The identification of the surreptitious substitution and the fallacy of misplaced concreteness as distinct but related errors is analytically precise and will prove to be the most important conceptual contribution of the book.
