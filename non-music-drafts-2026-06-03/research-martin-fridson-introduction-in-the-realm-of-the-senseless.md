### SECTION 2: Martin Fridson Introduction — "In the Realm of the Senseless"
**Core Claim:** Both Mackay's *Extraordinary Popular Delusions* and de la Vega's *Confusión de Confusiones* endure because they offer complementary explanatory frameworks for market irrationality—Mackay attributing it to mass hysteria, de la Vega to coordinated manipulation—and investors find comfort in at least one framework for understanding their losses.

**Supporting Evidence:**
- Documented reception: Mackay's book was championed by Baruch (1932 republication), Templeton, and has been continuously cited for 150+ years
- Empirical challenge to Mackay: Peter Garber's research shows Mackay plagiarized Johann Beckmann's tulip account and that the iconic "sailor eating the tulip bulb" anecdote likely postdates the events; Garber argues the tulip price swings were rational given bulb genetics and reproduction economics
- Empirical challenge to the South Sea myth: Larry Neal argues the South Sea Company served legitimate debt-conversion purposes before overreaching
- Modern parallel: the MMM pyramid scheme in Russia (1994), where investors returned to buy shares *after* the collapse, explicitly acknowledging it was a Ponzi scheme

**Logical Method:** Comparative reception analysis + challenge from economic historiography + contemporary parallel.

**Logical Gaps:**
- The Garber demolition of tulipomania is presented and then essentially dismissed: "no amount of scholarly debunking is likely to extinguish the fondness of investors for extraordinary popular delusions." This is an accurate sociological observation but not an epistemological argument. The fact that a false narrative is beloved does not rehabilitate it.
- The MMM example is deployed as proof of "the timelessness of de la Vega's confusions and Mackay's delusions." But the MMM case arguably proves only that Ponzi schemes recur—which requires no historical precedent from 1688 Amsterdam to explain.
- Fridson presents Mackay (mass hysteria) and de la Vega (rational manipulation) as complementary. But these frameworks are in genuine tension: if crashes are caused by coordinated bear syndicates, they are not "popular delusions"; if they are caused by mass irrationality, manipulation is secondary. The introduction does not resolve this tension.

**Methodological Soundness:** The introduction is honest about Mackay's historiographical weaknesses while insufficiently exploring the implication: if the foundational anecdotes are fabricated or misattributed, what survives of the argument?
