### SECTION 2: Chapter 1 — Analyze
**Core Claim:** Before positioning, you must understand (1) what competitors are offering, (2) what investors need, and (3) what your firm uniquely provides. This analysis is the foundation for all subsequent capital raising strategy.

**Supporting Evidence:**
- Private equity client example: Competitive analysis led Wilson to redirect the firm toward family offices and HNW individuals and away from traditional institutional LPs (endowments, pension funds) because their track record was "likely too small for serious consideration" from top institutional investors
- Investor Pyramid diagram showing how target investor type shifts with fund size (friends/family → HNW → family offices → institutional)
- Rockefeller quote: "I would rather earn 1% off a 100 people's efforts than 100% of my own efforts" — used to argue against excessive protectiveness of equity terms

**Logical Method:** Three-part analytical framework with investor segmentation by AUM tier and fund stage.

**Logical Gaps:**
- The competitive analysis checklist (10 items) is prescriptively complete but offers no prioritization. Not all 10 items are equally relevant for all capital raisers. A real estate developer raising a first fund needs a different analytical frame than a $500M hedge fund seeking institutional allocation.
- "What potential investors are there to choose from for your specific investment type?" is framed as an analytical question without providing any methodology for answering it. How does a first-time fund manager identify "the most common investor for your asset type" without the industry relationships that come from experience? The analysis section presupposes knowledge it is supposed to help develop.
- The Rockefeller equity flexibility argument is presented as generalizable wisdom, but the appropriate degree of equity flexibility depends entirely on the specific leverage position of the fundraiser. A manager with a strong 5-year track record should not negotiate the same terms as a first-time fund. The advice to "be smart but don't be stupid" about equity is analytically vacuous without a framework for determining when giving up equity makes sense.
- "Identify Inevitabilities" — the section advising capital raisers to spot market trends before competitors do — is sound in principle but is illustrated entirely with examples from Wilson's own business (founding Family Office Club, buying FamilyOffices.com). These are n=1 success stories presented as broadly applicable strategy.

**Methodological Soundness:** The three-part analysis framework is structurally sound. The specific sub-questions within each category are useful as orientation. The methodological gap is that the chapter describes what to analyze without providing reliable methods for how to conduct the analysis when you lack existing industry relationships or access to competitive intelligence.
