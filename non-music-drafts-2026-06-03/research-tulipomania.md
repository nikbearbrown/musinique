### SECTION 6: Tulipomania (Mackay)
**Core Claim:** Dutch society between 1634–1637 experienced a collective speculative delusion in tulip bulbs that penetrated all social classes, drove prices to 5,500 florins for a single bulb, and collapsed suddenly to near-zero, ruining thousands.

**Supporting Evidence:**
- Mackay provides a specific price table: Admiral Leifken (400 parrots) = 4,400 florins; Admiral van der Eyck (446 parrots) = 1,260 florins; Semper Augustus (200 parrots) = 5,500 florins; a single Viceroy bulb traded for an itemized basket of goods (2 lasts of wheat, 4 fat oxen, 8 fat swine, wine, beer, butter, cheese, clothing, silver cup) totaling 2,500 florins
- The "sailor eating the tulip bulb" anecdote and the "English botanist dissecting an Admiral" anecdote

**Logical Method:** Anecdote accumulation as proof of collective irrationality.

**Logical Gaps:**
- The Garber critique, acknowledged in the Fridson introduction, is devastating: Mackay assembled this account from secondhand sources, plagiarized Beckmann, and the signature anecdotes likely postdate the events. More importantly, Garber shows that comparable price swings in rare bulb varieties occurred in periods before and after 1636–1637, and that the most extreme prices applied to informal futures contracts in common varieties—not the rare varieties Mackay describes.
- The Fridson introduction cites a Columbia University economist's argument that high prices for "Semper Augustus"-type bulbs were rational: a single prized bulb could produce millions of descendants over decades, each carrying the distinctive coloration. The net present value calculation makes the high price defensible, not lunatic.
- Mackay attributes the crash to "the breath of popular mistrust." Garber attributes it to the collapse of informal futures markets in common bulbs—a much more specific and testable mechanism. Mackay's explanation is untestable.

**Methodological Soundness:** Of Mackay's three major case studies, tulipomania is the weakest. The primary sources are secondhand; the signature anecdotes are unverifiable; the mechanism of contagion is asserted not demonstrated; and the most rigorous subsequent scholarship substantially revises the standard account. Read as cultural history, this chapter is vivid. Read as economic analysis, it is unreliable.
