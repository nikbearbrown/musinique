### CHAPTER 3: The Warren Harding Error — Why We Fall For Tall, Dark, and Handsome

**Core Claim:** The same rapid-cognition machinery that enables expert intuition also produces systematic biases—particularly around physical appearance—that override rational evaluation of ability. This is the "dark side of thin slicing."

**Supporting Evidence:**
- Warren Harding: universally agreed by historians as one of the worst presidents in American history; elected largely on the basis of his extraordinary physical presence and "presidential" appearance
- IAT (Implicit Association Test): 80%+ of IAT takers show pro-white associations; approximately 50% of black IAT takers show the same pro-white implicit associations
- Fortune 500 CEO height data: average male CEO is just under 6 feet vs. 5'9" for the average American male; 58% of Fortune 500 CEOs are 6 feet or taller (vs. 14.5% of American men); nearly 1/3 of sampled CEOs were 6'2" or taller (vs. 3.9% of the general population)
- Height-salary correlation: each inch of height worth approximately $789/year in salary, producing ~$5,525 annual advantage for someone 6 feet vs. 5'5"; compounded over 30 years, "hundreds of thousands of dollars"
- Ian Ayres' car dealership study: 38 confederates (varied by race and gender, otherwise matched) negotiated at 242 Chicago dealerships. Initial offers above invoice: white men +$725; white women +$935; black women +$1,195; black men +$1,687. After 40 minutes of bargaining, black men's final price still ~$800 higher than white men's initial offer.
- Kura Neilimi Ambady's malpractice prediction study: 40-second content-filtered audio snippets (only tone of voice, no words intelligible) predicted whether surgeons had been sued; dominant tone = more likely to be sued; concerned tone = less likely

**Logical Method:** The chapter demonstrates that rapid cognition produces not just expert pattern recognition but also systematic discrimination along lines of race, gender, height, and appearance. These are shown to be statistically robust effects, not anecdotal.

**Logical Gaps:**
- The Ayres car dealership study is one of the book's strongest empirical pieces. But Gladwell's interpretation—that the discrimination is unconscious bias rather than deliberate predation—requires assuming that salespeople who have been told matching-dressed professionals are "bank analysts from Lincoln Park" are nonetheless sticking with unconscious lay-down stereotypes. This is plausible but not proven. The study demonstrates differential pricing; it cannot distinguish mechanism.
- The IAT interpretation is presented as "unconscious attitudes may be utterly incompatible with our stated conscious values," which is a reasonable reading. But Gladwell does not engage with the substantial methodological debate around IAT's predictive validity for actual discriminatory behavior (a debate that was ongoing even in 2005). The IAT measures reaction-time associations; its translation into real-world discriminatory acts is more complex than Gladwell suggests.
- The chapter proposes that the solution to Warren Harding errors is to "change your life so that you are exposed to minorities on a regular basis"—a behavioral remedy for implicit bias. The evidence base for this specific intervention (exposure = reduced implicit bias = reduced discriminatory behavior) is presented thinly.

**Methodological Soundness:** The empirical studies cited are real and substantial. The chapter's core argument—that rapid cognition produces systematic bias—is better supported than any other chapter's central claim. The mechanism-interpretation gap is the main weakness.
