### CHAPTER 12: Democracy — The Limits of Collective Wisdom for Value Questions

**Core Claim:** Democratic wisdom is real but fundamentally different from market wisdom. Democracy does not solve cognition problems (there is no objectively correct answer to most policy questions), but it solves coordination and cooperation problems: it institutionalizes the experience of not getting everything you want, which is the foundation of social contract.

**Supporting Evidence:**
- James Fishkin's Deliberative Poll (Philadelphia, January 2003): 343 representative Americans deliberated for a weekend on foreign policy; before-and-after polls showed meaningful position changes
- Kinder and Kiewiet (1980s): no correlation between voters' personal economic conditions and their votes; correlation between voters' assessments of the *national* economy and their votes
- David Sears's studies: ideology predicts political attitudes better than self-interest (conservatives without health insurance still opposed national health insurance)
- University of Maryland polls: Americans believe the US spends 24% of its budget on foreign aid; actual figure is less than 1% — demonstrating mass ignorance but not demonstrating that votes are thereby distorted in systematic ways
- Public choice theory (Buchanan and Tullock): voters, politicians, and regulators are self-interested; government grows because individual incentives point toward getting more from the state

**Logical Method:** Survey research, political science theory, experimental deliberation.

**Logical Gaps:**
- The chapter's core argument — that democracy solves coordination/cooperation problems but not cognition problems — is the right distinction, but Surowiecki does not explain *which* coordination and cooperation problems democracy solves or how to tell the difference from the inside.
- The praise of Fishkin's deliberative polls is conceptually disconnected from the book's main argument. Deliberative polls change views through argument and information — not through aggregation of independent judgments. If anything, deliberative polls are the *opposite* of crowd wisdom; they are a mechanism to reduce diversity of views through persuasion.
- Public choice theory is summarized as "remarkably perceptive and remarkably obtuse" — which is fair — but Surowiecki does not engage its strongest claims. If interest groups systematically distort policy, then democratic "crowd wisdom" may track the wisdom of organized factions rather than the dispersed population.
- The claim that "democracy helps people learn the virtues of compromise" is a political-theoretical assertion, not derived from evidence presented in this book.

**Methodological Soundness:** The weakest analytical chapter. The move to democracy requires a different kind of argument than the rest of the book supplies, and Surowiecki is appropriately humble about the limits of the crowd-wisdom framework here — but the positive account of what democracy does accomplish is underdeveloped.

## Extended Research Notes

**Pantry note:** `pantry/notes-democracy-the-limits-of-collective-wisdom-for-value-questions.md`

Key additions: Democracy should not be treated as a prediction market. It aggregates preferences, values, identities, and judgments under institutions, often where there is no single objectively correct answer.

Settled: Deliberative polling is a real method for producing more informed public opinion. Contested: whether democratic outcomes are systematically distorted by rational ignorance, polarization, and interest groups.

Teaching move: Classify policy questions as factual estimation, value conflict, coordination, cooperation, or legitimacy problems before judging whether "crowd wisdom" applies.
