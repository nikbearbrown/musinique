### CHAPTER 1: Introduction — The Most Human Human

**Core Claim:** The Turing Test, designed to measure machine intelligence, is more usefully understood as a test of human presence and authenticity. The question "can machines think?" is less interesting than "what does it mean to be fully human in conversation?"

**Supporting Evidence:**
- Bloom's (1984) "2 sigma problem" and the resulting Loebner Prize frame
- AutoTutor and the 0.8σ claim are not present here; this is Brian Christian's personal Turing test, not educational technology
- Person et al. (2002) "bystander Turing test" is not referenced in this text; this is the Loebner Prize/Brighton 2009 setting
- Robert Epstein fell in love with a chatbot named Ivana for four months before realizing she was software; the anecdote illustrates that in digital environments "all communication is a Turing test"
- 2008's most human computer came within a single vote of Turing's 30% threshold
- The "most human human" award has gone to Confederates who were "moody, irritable, and obnoxious" (Charles Platt, 1994)

**Logical Method:** Personal narrative → philosophical generalization. Christian uses his preparation for the Brighton contest as the organizing spine, but the real inquiry is into what makes human communication distinctive.

**Logical Gaps:**
- Christian assumes that "just be yourself" is naive advice without yet proving what "being yourself" involves—the book is organized as the investigation of that question
- The claim that Epstein's experience "erodes our trust" and simultaneously could improve his future relationships rests on optimistic inference, not evidence

**Methodological Soundness:** The chapter is introduction-as-inquiry rather than introduction-as-claim. Christian is unusually honest about the question being genuinely open.
