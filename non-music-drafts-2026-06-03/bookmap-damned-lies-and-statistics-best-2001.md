# BOOKMAP: Damned Lies and Statistics: Untangling Numbers from the Media, Politicians, and Activists
**Joel Best (2001) | University of California Press**

---

## PART 1: SECTION-BY-SECTION LOGICAL MAPPING

---

### INTRODUCTION: The Worst Social Statistics Ever

**Core Claim:** Statistics about social problems are systematically corrupted at origin, in transmission, and in interpretation—and the corruption is so normalized that it reaches peer-reviewed journals and congressional testimony without challenge.

**Supporting Evidence:**
- A 1995 journal article asserted "every year since 1950, the number of American children gunned down has doubled"—a claim that, taken literally, implies 35 trillion child gunshot victims by 1995, more than twice the Earth's total human population across all recorded history
- The article's author had misread a Children's Defense Fund statistic ("doubled since 1950" becoming "doubles each year")—a single transposition that produced a logical impossibility
- The error was published, peer-reviewed, and then copied by a graduate student into a dissertation prospectus

**Logical Method:** Proof by reductio ad absurdum. Best takes the published claim at face value and calculates its implications to demonstrate its impossibility. The rhetorical strategy is precise: if the claim were true, it would contradict verifiable facts (total US homicides in any given year) by orders of magnitude.

**Logical Gaps:**
- The introduction frames this as an extreme case ("my nomination for the worst social statistic ever"), which implicitly suggests other bad statistics are merely less extreme on a continuous scale. This framing underestimates the book's central argument: the problem is not exceptional outliers but structural—bad statistics are the predictable output of normal social processes.
- Best attributes the mutation to a "misread" without examining whether the original CDF claim ("doubled since 1950") itself withstands scrutiny. The CDF figure may be defensible, but the introduction does not demonstrate that it is.

**Methodological Soundness:** The introduction is a well-constructed persuasion piece. Its evidentiary standard is demonstrably met for the specific example. The implicit extrapolation—this kind of error is common—is asserted rather than proven here; the book's subsequent chapters must carry that weight.

### CHAPTER 1: The Importance of Social Statistics

**Core Claim:** Social statistics are not discovered facts but social products—created by people with interests, shaped by organizational practices, and interpreted within ideological frameworks. Their apparent authority as numbers conceals their social construction.

**Supporting Evidence:**

*Historical origin of statistics as persuasion tools:*
- 19th-century New York reformers estimated 10,000 prostitutes (c. 10% of the city's female population); police simultaneously reported 1,223—a tenfold difference explained entirely by the competing interests of each party
- The term "statistics" itself originates in 17th-century political arithmetic, explicitly designed to help governments devise policies, not to neutrally describe reality

*Organizational construction of official statistics:*
- Marriage statistics depend entirely on couples' compliance with licensing procedures; couples who marry outside the system are invisible to the record
- Suicide statistics depend on coroners' judgments in ambiguous cases—judgments that vary systematically by coroner and jurisdiction
- Domestic violence statistics depend on police officers' discretionary decisions at the scene, decisions influenced by shift timing, department policy, and the officer's own interpretation of the situation

*Structural bias toward large numbers:*
- Activists require big numbers to justify concern; officials require small numbers to demonstrate competence; both parties produce statistics that serve their institutional interests rather than approximate reality

**Logical Method:** Sociological constructivism applied to a typically exempted domain (official government statistics). Best's argument is that the social construction of knowledge applies even to the hardest-seeming numbers.

**Logical Gaps:**
- The chapter establishes that organizational practices shape statistics, but does not specify what conditions make official statistics more or less reliable. The argument could be read as implying that all official statistics are equally unreliable—which is not Best's position, but the chapter does not clearly delineate when the bias is severe versus negligible.
- The prostitution example conflates two separate problems: definitional differences (who counts as a prostitute?) and measurement differences (how do you find and count them?). These are distinct sources of error that Best later separates in Chapter 2 but conflates here.

**Methodological Soundness:** The historical and sociological analysis is well-founded and conventionally accepted in sociology of knowledge. The implicit normative claim—that statistics should be more accurate than they are—requires a standard of accuracy that Best does not define in this chapter.

### CHAPTER 2: Soft Facts — Sources of Bad Statistics

**Core Claim:** Bad statistics originate through four distinct mechanisms: (1) guessing when no measurement exists, (2) definitional choices that expand or contract what is counted, (3) measurement choices that shape what responses a survey or instrument elicits, and (4) sampling choices that determine whose experience is counted.

**Supporting Evidence:**

*Guessing:*
- Mitch Snyder, leading 1980s homeless activist, acknowledged on Nightline that the 2-3 million figure was fabricated through phone calls: "They have no meaning, no value"
- Government drug enforcement agencies face the same pressure—reporting large addict counts justifies their budgets—and produce comparably unreliable figures

*Definitional choice:*
- Homelessness: definitions range from "sleeping outdoors tonight" to "lacking stable permanent housing" to "doubled up in someone else's home." Each produces a fundamentally different numerical universe. The broadest definitions encompass populations many times larger than the narrowest.
- Illiteracy: "cannot read at all" vs. "cannot read at the sixth-grade level" produces estimates that differ by an order of magnitude

*Measurement:*
- Gun control survey wording: "Do you favor cracking down against illegal gun sales?" (gun control advocates) vs. "Would you favor giving police the power to decide who may own a firearm?" (NRA) both claim to measure "public opinion on gun control" but reliably produce opposite results
- UFO abduction study: researchers counted "symptoms" rather than direct reports of abduction, concluding 2% of adult Americans had been abducted—a figure premised on the assumption that people who had been abducted would not report it directly

*Sampling:*
- Convenience sampling (studying the population that presents itself—runaways who come to shelters, for example) systematically excludes people with different experiences and may produce estimates entirely unrepresentative of the phenomenon

**Logical Method:** Taxonomic—Best identifies four types of error and illustrates each with at least one clear empirical example. The taxonomy is analytically prior to the empirical work; it structures what follows.

**Logical Gaps:**
- The four mechanisms are presented as distinct but they interact significantly in practice. A researcher who uses a broad definition will also need a broad measurement instrument, which will likely be administered to a wider sample. Best acknowledges this interaction without systematizing it.
- The section on sampling conflates two problems: biased samples (convenience sampling introduces systematic error) and small samples (insufficient power to detect effects). These require different remedies and produce different error profiles.
- Best's guidelines for "good statistics" at the chapter's close—broad definitions stated, measurements explained, samples described—are necessary but not sufficient conditions. A study can meet all four criteria and still produce badly misleading results if the underlying phenomenon is inherently unmeasurable.

**Methodological Soundness:** The taxonomy is analytically sound and widely recognized in survey methodology and sociology of knowledge. The empirical examples are well-chosen and well-explained. The UFO example is the chapter's high point—it demonstrates how an entire methodology can be constructed to produce a predetermined result without any single step being technically dishonest.

### CHAPTER 3: Mutant Statistics — Methods for Mangling Numbers

**Core Claim:** Even accurate statistics can be corrupted in transmission through four mechanisms: (1) inappropriate generalization from flawed definitions, measurements, or samples; (2) transformation—changing a number's meaning while preserving the number; (3) confusion about complex statistical concepts; and (4) compound error—using a bad statistic as the foundation for calculating a new statistic.

**Supporting Evidence:**

*Transformation (the book's richest case):*
- 150,000 anorexic women became 150,000 deaths from anorexia per year—a figure that would require anorexia to kill every young woman aged 15-44 in the United States. The mutation was so obvious that basic numeracy should have caught it; it survived because advocates found the higher figure useful.
- A psychologist's estimate that 6% of priests in treatment were sexually preoccupied with minors was transformed, through four separate steps, into "6% of all priests have had sex with children"—expanding the population (treatment patients → all priests), the behavior (attraction → contact), and the target (adolescents → children)

*Confusion (compound statistical misinterpretation):*
- Workforce 2000's finding that white males would represent roughly 15% of net additions to the workforce was transformed by the report's own executive summary into "only 15% of new workers will be white males"—conflating net additions (a small marginal group) with all new entrants (a much larger group). The press then extrapolated further: "by 2000, 65% of the total workforce will be women"—a claim with no relationship to the original data.
- Cardiac catheterization study: a finding that blacks and women had odds ratios of 0.6 for referral was reported as "blacks and women are 40% less likely to receive cardiac testing"—conflating odds ratios with risk ratios, a technically distinct but easily conflated measure. The correct statement was approximately "7% less likely."

*Compound error:*
- Claim that one-third of teen suicides involve gay or lesbian adolescents is built from: (1) the Kinsey 10% homosexuality estimate, itself based on a non-random sample; (2) a "2-3x higher suicide rate" estimate for homosexuals, based on research with its own methodological problems; multiplied together and rounded up, these produce 30-33%. If the underlying estimates are revised down even moderately (3% homosexual prevalence, 2x suicide rate), the figure falls below 6%.

**Logical Method:** Process tracing—Best follows the chain of transmission for specific statistics, identifying the precise point at which meaning changes.

**Logical Gaps:**
- The four mechanisms (generalization, transformation, confusion, compound error) are not exhaustive and the boundaries between them are sometimes arbitrary. The anorexia case is called "transformation" but involves both confusion (misunderstanding prevalence versus mortality) and generalization (applying a single-year mortality figure to an annual rate).
- Best is primarily interested in errors that inflate numbers. He acknowledges that downward distortions also occur (officials minimize counts to show competence) but does not provide equivalent examples of compound errors that produce underestimates. The book's systematic focus on overestimates may itself reflect a selection bias in the examples Best found compelling.

**Methodological Soundness:** The process-tracing approach is well-suited to demonstrating how specific statistics mutate. The compound error section is the chapter's most analytically valuable contribution—it shows how a chain of questionable estimates can produce a conclusion that is orders of magnitude from reality while each individual step seems defensible.

### CHAPTER 4: Apples and Oranges — Inappropriate Comparisons

**Core Claim:** Statistical comparisons—across time, across places, across groups, and across social problems—are systematically distorted by comparing things that are not comparable: using different definitions, different measurement methods, different base populations, or different units in ways that make change, difference, or severity appear larger or smaller than it actually is.

**Supporting Evidence:**

*Comparisons over time:*
- Child abuse statistics show a dramatic increase from roughly 150,000 reported cases in 1963 to nearly 3 million in 1995. Best demonstrates that this reflects the expansion of mandatory reporting laws, broader definitions, and increased professional training to recognize abuse—not necessarily an increase in abuse itself. Comparing 1963 and 1995 figures using the same methodology would likely show a very different (and possibly flat or declining) trend.

*Geographic comparisons:*
- U.S. lawyer counts appear dramatically higher than Japan's until the comparison accounts for Japan's distinction between licensed Bengoshi and the much larger population of law graduates who provide legal services without that designation. Correcting for this definitional difference, the U.S. and Japan have comparable proportions of people performing legal work (~28 vs. ~32 per 10,000 population).

*Group comparisons:*
- Victimization data: "Whites account for more than 80% of violent crime victims" is arithmetically true but misleads because whites are 83% of the population—roughly the proportion you would expect under random distribution. The meaningful comparison is the rate per 1,000 population, which shows blacks are victimized at higher rates. Advocates can choose either framing to support opposite conclusions from the same data.
- Racial differences in crime statistics may be artifacts of class differences—if blacks are disproportionately poor and poverty predicts crime involvement, then a "racial" effect in arrest statistics may actually be a class effect. The data to test this (class of those arrested) is typically not collected.

*Comparisons across social problems:*
- "Suicide is the second leading cause of death among adolescents" is technically accurate but depends on subdividing the "accident" and "disease" categories into many subcategories while lumping suicide in a single category. More importantly, it exploits the fact that adolescents die at very low rates from most other causes, making it easy for suicide to rank highly by default.

**Logical Method:** Methodological critique—Best examines the conditions under which comparisons are valid and demonstrates specific cases where the conditions are violated.

**Logical Gaps:**
- The discussion of group comparisons (race and class) correctly identifies the problem of confounding variables but does not provide a model for when racial comparisons are appropriate. If class explains some racial differences in arrest rates, it may not explain all of them—the remainder could reflect genuine discrimination, differential policing, or other factors. Best's point is that we need to control for class, but he does not address what that analysis would actually show.
- The projections section (crack babies, AIDS) demonstrates overestimation but does not examine the opposite problem: projections that deliberately underestimate future harm (industry-funded projections of tobacco deaths, for example, or climate change damage estimates). The chapter's examples skew toward activist-generated overestimates.

**Methodological Soundness:** The chapter's analytical framework is rigorous and the examples are well-chosen. The child abuse comparison-over-time analysis is the chapter's strongest section—it precisely identifies the mechanism by which changing organizational practices produce apparent statistical trends that may have no relationship to underlying trends in the phenomenon itself.

### CHAPTER 5: Stat Wars — Conflicts Over Social Statistics

**Core Claim:** Statistical disputes are not primarily about accuracy but about competing interests—the party that controls the dominant statistic shapes the terms of the policy debate. Understanding a stat war requires understanding the interests of the parties, not just evaluating the methodologies.

**Supporting Evidence:**

*Million Man March:*
- March organizers claimed 1.5-2 million attendees; park police estimated 400,000. A Boston University team using aerial photography produced an intermediate estimate of 837,000-870,000. The difference between the BU and park police estimates reduced to a single variable: crowd density assumption. Park police assumed 3.6 sq. ft./person (consistent with listening crowds); BU assumed 1.8 sq. ft./person (elevator density). Best implies the park police assumption is more defensible, but does not state this explicitly.
- Media covered the debate by reporting competing numbers without explaining how either was derived or evaluating which methodology was more credible.

*Census undercounting:*
- The 1990 census undercounted by approximately 2% net, with a ~1.5% undercount for non-blacks and ~5% for blacks—a systematic disparity that advantages groups (predominantly white suburbs) with smaller undercounts in congressional apportionment and federal funding allocation.
- Democrats favored adjustment (which would increase the count for urban minority populations); Republicans opposed it (which would preserve the existing count advantaging suburban white populations). Both framed the debate in terms of accuracy, but the real stakes were the political consequences of each methodology.

*Income inequality:*
- Whether the economy improved or deteriorated in the 1970s-1990s depends entirely on the choice of measure: per capita income (rose), average hourly wages (fell), real hourly compensation including benefits (rose if you accept the conservative correction to the CPI, fell if you don't), household income controlling for household composition (complicated by rising workforce participation by women). All figures are technically correct; the choice among them is political.

**Logical Method:** Interest-based analysis of statistical conflict—Best treats the statistics themselves as secondary to the social and institutional processes that produce and deploy them.

**Logical Gaps:**
- The chapter argues that stat wars are driven by competing interests but does not provide a method for determining which statistic is more accurate when both parties' figures are technically defensible. Interest-based analysis explains why the dispute exists; it does not resolve it.
- The crowd density example is the chapter's best analytical case—Best effectively shows that the dispute reduces to a single, empirically testable assumption—but he does not state what the correct assumption is or how one would determine it. Specifying crowd density in a large demonstration is an empirical question that crowd science has studied; Best does not cite this literature.

**Methodological Soundness:** The census section is the chapter's strongest analytical contribution. The income inequality section exemplifies a genuine methodological problem—there is no single correct measure of "economic wellbeing," and reasonable people can prefer different measures—but Best does not adequately distinguish between this genuinely contested methodological problem and cases where one measure is simply better than another.

### CHAPTER 6: Thinking About Social Statistics

**Core Claim:** The correct response to statistical unreliability is neither naïve acceptance nor cynical rejection but critical analysis: evaluating each statistic's definitions, measurements, and sampling choices, assessing whether comparisons are appropriate, and maintaining proportionate skepticism calibrated to the quality of the underlying methods.

**Supporting Evidence:**
- Three orientations toward statistics: the *awestruck* (numbers are magical, beyond question); the *naïve* (numbers are probably accurate, accept them); the *cynical* (numbers are always manipulated, reject them). Best argues all three are epistemically dysfunctional.
- The critical orientation asks: Who created this statistic and why? How was it measured? What was sampled? Is the comparison appropriate? These questions do not require technical sophistication—only the habit of asking.
- Best frames statistics as fetishes: objects to which we attribute magical properties (accuracy, objectivity, truth) that they do not inherently possess.

**Logical Method:** Epistemological prescription—the chapter argues for an approach to statistical evidence rather than demonstrating an empirical claim.

**Logical Gaps:**
- The three-category typology (awestruck/naïve/cynical/critical) is a useful heuristic but slightly overpopulated. Best's distinction between the awestruck and the naïve is not crisp—both accept numbers uncritically; the difference seems to be degree of mathematical sophistication, not orientation toward skepticism. The critical approach is more carefully delineated.
- The chapter does not address what happens when critical analysis is applied to a statistic and the result is "this statistic is likely somewhat biased, but I cannot determine in which direction or by how much." This is the most common outcome of careful statistical analysis—and Best's prescriptions do not equip readers to act under this kind of residual uncertainty.

**Methodological Soundness:** The chapter is the book's weakest analytically—it is primarily normative prescription rather than empirical argument. The fetish metaphor is evocative but does not advance the analysis beyond what earlier chapters established. The chapter functions as a conclusion rather than a contribution.

## BRIDGE: Synthesizing the Logical Architecture

**The book's argumentative spine:** Every social statistic is a social product, shaped by the interests of its creators, the organizational practices of the institutions that collect it, the definitions that bound what gets counted, the instruments that translate experience into numbers, the samples that determine whose experience is counted, and the rhetorical choices made in transmission. The question is never "is this statistic accurate?" but "what specific choices produced this number, and how do those choices systematically favor particular conclusions?"

**Three cross-cutting tensions:**

*Tension 1: Cynicism versus utility.* Best's book could easily function as a demolition of statistical reasoning: if statistics are social products shaped by interests, why trust any of them? Best explicitly resists this conclusion, arguing that we need statistics and that some are better than others. But the analytical framework he provides—every number is constructed, construction reflects interests—does not readily generate the distinction between "good enough" and "too biased to use." The book is much stronger as an analysis of how bad statistics are produced than as a guide to identifying better ones.

*Tension 2: The dark figure problem.* Throughout the book, Best acknowledges that advocates are often right that official statistics systematically undercount the phenomena they care about—crime does have a dark figure, child abuse does go unreported, homeless people are missed by census procedures. The question is not whether the dark figure exists but how large it is. Best correctly notes that advocates consistently overestimate the dark figure, but he does not provide a method for estimating it more accurately. The book explains why current estimates are biased; it does not explain how to correct for that bias.

*Tension 3: Interest and accuracy.* Best's interest-based analysis implies that all statistics serve someone's agenda. But the alternative—statistics with no constituency—is not obviously better. Statistics that nobody cares about are not produced, not scrutinized, and not improved. The organizations that routinely produce better statistics (the Bureau of Labor Statistics, the Bureau of the Census) do so precisely because powerful interests have invested in their accuracy. Interest and accuracy are not simply opposed.

**The book's most proven claims:**
- Guessing is the default when no measurement exists, and guesses are systematically biased toward large numbers by advocates' interests
- Definitional choices are the single most powerful determinant of statistical outcomes for social problems and are almost never disclosed in press reporting
- Numbers mutate in transmission through enumeracy—people who do not understand a statistic's derivation will unintentionally change its meaning when repeating it
- Organizational practices, not social reality, are the primary determinant of variation in officially recorded statistics across jurisdictions

**The book's most significant limitation:**
Best provides excellent diagnosis but thin prescription. His three questions (who created this? why? how?) are necessary but rarely sufficient—a critical reader who has applied these questions may still be unable to determine whether a given statistic is reliable or by how much it is biased. The book teaches suspicion without teaching resolution.

**The book's most significant acknowledged gap:**
Best does not address what the reader should do when all available statistics on a topic are likely biased. He implies that waiting for better research is the appropriate response—but most policy decisions cannot wait, and politicians and advocates who have internalized his skepticism still need to act on the best available evidence, however imperfect.

## PART 2: LITERARY REVIEW ESSAY

---

# The Statistic That Cannot Lie and the People Who Create It

Joel Best begins with a number so wrong it is funny: a social scientist's claim that the number of American children gunned down has doubled every year since 1950, implying 35 trillion child victims by 1995. Best uses this example to establish the genre. *Damned Lies and Statistics* (2001) is not a book about lying statisticians. It is a book about the predictable social processes—advocacy, media, organizational routine, innumeracy—that produce bad statistics as their normal output. The distinction matters enormously, and Best is careful to maintain it throughout. If bad statistics required dishonesty, the remedy would be moral. But Best's diagnosis is structural: the same processes that create good statistics create bad ones, and the same audiences that reject obvious lies accept statistical errors that are logically equivalent.

The book's central argument is stated without hedging in Chapter 1: statistics are social products. They are created by people with interests, within organizations with practices, through methodological choices that are rarely disclosed and routinely consequential. This is not a claim about corruption. It is a claim about epistemology—the same claim that philosophers of science have made about scientific knowledge generally, applied to the domain where most people still believe they are receiving unmediated facts. The number you hear on the evening news is not a measurement of reality. It is the output of a chain of choices: what to define as an instance of the problem, how to measure it, whose experience to sample, what language to use in survey questions, how to interpret ambiguous cases. Each choice reflects someone's judgment, and many choices reflect someone's interests.

Best's taxonomy of error in Chapter 2—guessing, definition, measurement, sampling—is analytically careful and pedagogically useful. But the most important distinction in the book is not among these four types. It is the distinction between the *dark figure problem* (the acknowledged gap between recorded cases and true cases) and its exploitation. Best demonstrates that every social problem has a dark figure—crime goes unreported, child abuse goes undetected, homeless people avoid census takers. Activists are not wrong to insist that official statistics undercount. The question is always: by how much? And Best's answer is that advocates consistently overestimate the dark figure because overestimation serves their cause. The process has no brake. Any estimate, however large, can be defended by arguing that the true dark figure is larger still.

Consider the homeless population figures from the early 1980s. Activists claimed 2-3 million homeless. The Reagan administration countered with 300,000. Social scientists who conducted careful field research confirmed a figure closer to the administration's estimate. The activists' response—widely repeated in the press—was not to revise their estimate but to attack the social scientists' methodology and question their political sympathies. This is the epistemically troubling moment Best documents repeatedly: when an estimate becomes politically necessary, evidence against it becomes evidence of bias. The number cannot be wrong because the problem is real.

---

Best's Chapter 3, on mutant statistics, is the book's strongest analytical contribution. The anorexia case is the chapter's best illustration: 150,000 people with anorexia became 150,000 deaths from anorexia per year, a transformation that requires only a momentary confusion between prevalence and mortality. The figure should have been obviously impossible—roughly 8,500 women aged 15-24 die annually from *all causes* in the United States. An annual anorexia death toll of 150,000 would require the disease to kill more young women than exist. The claim circulated for years in books, newspaper columns, and talk show discussions. No one asked the arithmetic.

This is Best's core argument about enumeracy (his term, borrowed from mathematician John Allen Paulos): we do not extend to statistical claims the same basic logical scrutiny we apply to ordinary claims. If someone told you there were 37 million cars in a city of 300,000 people, you would immediately recognize the claim as impossible. The same arithmetical relationship, applied to gunshot deaths or anorexia fatalities, passes without inspection. Numbers are treated as a different category of fact—harder, more authoritative, immune to the commonsense checks that make ordinary claims tractable.

The compound error analysis in the same chapter raises a sharper problem. The claim that one-third of teen suicides involve gay or lesbian adolescents is derived from: the Kinsey 10% homosexuality estimate (based on a non-random sample with a known bias toward finding high rates of homosexuality), multiplied by a claimed 2-3x elevated suicide rate for homosexuals (itself based on research Best does not closely scrutinize). Multiplying two questionable estimates produces a third-generation estimate with compounded uncertainty. If the Kinsey figure is revised from 10% to 3%, and the suicide rate multiplier from 3x to 2x, the one-third claim collapses to less than 6%. The final number depends entirely on the assumptions—but the final number is the only thing that circulates.

I find this the book's most significant analytical point, and also its least developed. Best correctly identifies compound error as a distinct category—a statistical claim that is wrong not because any single step is dishonest but because each step introduces error that multiplies through the chain. But he does not provide a systematic method for detecting compound errors in arguments where the intermediate assumptions are not disclosed. In practice, most statistical claims in policy debates are compound in exactly this sense: they rest on multiple prior estimates, each of which carries its own uncertainty, and the final figure is presented without any of this provenance. The reader who follows Best's advice—ask who created the number and how—will often find that the honest answer is "this number is the product of three other estimates, all of which are contested."

---

The chapter on comparisons (Chapter 4) is where the book's argument has the most direct policy implications. Best's analysis of the child abuse statistics is exemplary. Between 1963 and 1995, reported child abuse cases increased roughly twentyfold. This looks like an epidemic. But the same period saw: the invention of the term "child abuse" as a category; mandatory reporting laws requiring physicians, teachers, and other professionals to report suspected cases; expanded definitions that added neglect, sexual abuse, and emotional abuse to physical battery; and training programs that taught professionals to recognize subtler signs. The resulting increase in reported cases reflects the expansion of reporting infrastructure—not necessarily any change in the underlying incidence of harm to children.

This is not a trivial methodological observation. If reported child abuse is rising because reporting practices have improved, the correct policy response is different from the response warranted if actual abuse is rising. Improved reporting may call for better services and trauma-informed institutional practices; rising actual abuse may call for interventions in the social conditions (poverty, substance abuse, housing instability) that predict it. Conflating the two—treating better measurement as evidence of worse conditions—leads to badly miscalibrated policy.

Best returns to this problem with the redesigned National Crime Victimization Survey, which elicited dramatically more reports of rape and sexual assault after being redesigned to reduce false negatives. Officials understood this—they explicitly anticipated higher reported rates from the new instrument—but a researcher who compared pre-redesign and post-redesign figures without understanding the methodological change would conclude that rape had sharply increased. This is the apples-and-oranges problem at its most consequential: not a mathematical error but a categorical one, comparing measurements that are not measuring the same thing.

---

Best's final chapter argues for a "critical" orientation toward statistics—neither naïve acceptance nor cynical rejection, but systematic interrogation. Ask who produced the number, why, and how. The framework is necessary and correctly specified. It is also insufficient, and Best is aware of this without fully confronting it.

The book's most important unaddressed question is: what do you do after the critical analysis? Suppose a reader applies Best's three questions to the 2001 homeless population estimates and determines: (1) the estimate comes from an advocacy organization with an interest in a large number; (2) it was produced through a method that likely overestimates by conflating "lacking stable housing" with "sleeping outdoors"; (3) the sample is a census of shelter residents, which systematically excludes unsheltered people while also possibly double-counting them. Having made this determination, the reader knows the estimate is probably biased upward. But by how much? And what should they accept instead?

Best does not say—and this is not a minor omission. It is the gap between his diagnosis and any usable treatment. Statistics about social problems exist because people need to make decisions: how much to fund homeless shelters, how many social workers to hire, whether to build new housing. Those decisions cannot wait for methodologically perfect statistics that may never exist. The practical question is not "is this statistic biased?" (it almost certainly is) but "is this statistic biased enough that it should not inform decision-making, and what should we use instead?"

This requires a standard of "good enough" that Best never provides. He argues that good statistics are based on more than guessing, have clear definitions, use reasonable measures, and draw on representative samples. These are necessary conditions, not sufficient ones. A study that meets all four criteria can still be systematically misleading if, say, it measures something correlated with but not identical to the phenomenon of interest, or if the sample is representative of one population but the claim is generalized to another. Best's prescriptions would filter out the worst statistics—the guesses, the circular definitions, the convenience samples—but leave a large middle category of statistics that are methodologically respectable but still substantially uncertain.

---

None of this undermines the book's core achievement, which is to change the reader's default relationship to numerical claims. *Damned Lies and Statistics* does not equip the reader to evaluate specific statistics with precision. It equips the reader to be surprised when a statistic's provenance is disclosed—surprised, and habitually suspicious. That is not a trivial accomplishment. Most statistical claims circulate without any provenance at all. The number appears in a press report, attributed to a study, and travels from there. The study is rarely examined. The definitions are not disclosed. The sample is not described.

Best's taxonomy of error—guessing, definition, measurement, sampling, mutation, inappropriate comparison—provides a vocabulary for asking questions that would otherwise not be asked. Why does this study produce a higher estimate than that study? Probably because of a broader definition, not because one study found more cases. Why has the rate of this social problem increased so dramatically in the last decade? Probably because reporting has improved, not because the problem has worsened. Why do two surveys produce opposite findings on the same question? Probably because the question wording differs in ways that elicit systematically different responses.

These are not sophisticated statistical insights. They are the most basic principles of social measurement, and they are almost universally absent from press coverage of social problems research. Best's book exists to supply them to readers who will never take a research methods course. In this it succeeds. The reader who completes it will never again encounter a large round number about a social problem without asking, at minimum, where it came from.

Whether that question will be answerable is a different matter. Best's book teaches the right question. The world, as it turns out, rarely provides the answer.
