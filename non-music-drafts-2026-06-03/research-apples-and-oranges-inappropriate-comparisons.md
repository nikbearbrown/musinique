### CHAPTER 4: Apples and Oranges — Inappropriate Comparisons

**Core Claim:** Statistical comparisons—across time, across places, across groups, and across social problems—are systematically distorted by comparing things that are not comparable: using different definitions, different measurement methods, different base populations, or different units in ways that make change, difference, or severity appear larger or smaller than it actually is.

**Supporting Evidence:**

*Comparisons over time:*
- Child abuse statistics show a dramatic increase from roughly 150,000 reported cases in 1963 to nearly 3 million in 1995. Best demonstrates that this reflects the expansion of mandatory reporting laws, broader definitions, and increased professional training to recognize abuse—not necessarily an increase in abuse itself. Comparing 1963 and 1995 figures using the same methodology would likely show a very different (and possibly flat or declining) trend.

*Geographic comparisons:*
- U.S. lawyer counts appear dramatically higher than Japan's until the comparison accounts for Japan's distinction between licensed Bengoshi and the much larger population of law graduates who provide legal services without that designation. Correcting for this definitional difference, the U.S. and Japan have comparable proportions of people performing legal work (~28 vs. ~32 per 10,000 population).

*Group comparisons:*
- Victimization data: "Whites account for more than 80% of violent crime victims" is arithmetically true but misleads because whites are 83% of the population—roughly the proportion you would expect under random distribution. The meaningful comparison is the rate per 1,000 population, which shows blacks are victimized at higher rates. Advocates can choose either framing to support opposite conclusions from the same data.
- Racial differences in crime statistics may be artifacts of class differences—if blacks are disproportionately poor and poverty predicts crime involvement, then a "racial" effect in arrest statistics may actually be a class effect. The data to test this (class of those arrested) is typically not collected.

*Comparisons across social problems:*
- "Suicide is the second leading cause of death among adolescents" is technically accurate but depends on subdividing the "accident" and "disease" categories into many subcategories while lumping suicide in a single category. More importantly, it exploits the fact that adolescents die at very low rates from most other causes, making it easy for suicide to rank highly by default.

**Logical Method:** Methodological critique—Best examines the conditions under which comparisons are valid and demonstrates specific cases where the conditions are violated.

**Logical Gaps:**
- The discussion of group comparisons (race and class) correctly identifies the problem of confounding variables but does not provide a model for when racial comparisons are appropriate. If class explains some racial differences in arrest rates, it may not explain all of them—the remainder could reflect genuine discrimination, differential policing, or other factors. Best's point is that we need to control for class, but he does not address what that analysis would actually show.
- The projections section (crack babies, AIDS) demonstrates overestimation but does not examine the opposite problem: projections that deliberately underestimate future harm (industry-funded projections of tobacco deaths, for example, or climate change damage estimates). The chapter's examples skew toward activist-generated overestimates.

**Methodological Soundness:** The chapter's analytical framework is rigorous and the examples are well-chosen. The child abuse comparison-over-time analysis is the chapter's strongest section—it precisely identifies the mechanism by which changing organizational practices produce apparent statistical trends that may have no relationship to underlying trends in the phenomenon itself.

## Extended Research Notes

**Pantry note:** `pantry/notes-apples-and-oranges-inappropriate-comparisons.md`

Key additions: valid comparisons require consistent case definitions, denominators, measurement methods, population coverage, and time windows. Child-abuse reporting trends illustrate changing surveillance as much as changing underlying incidence; raw group comparisons need rates and matched populations; "leading cause" claims can be distorted by category design and age bands.

Settled: inconsistent measurement can invalidate simple comparisons even when every individual number is technically true. Contested: how much useful inference can still be drawn from imperfect public statistics when the limitations are made explicit.

Teaching move: make students annotate every statistic with five questions before interpretation: What is counted? Who is in the denominator? How was it collected? What is the comparison population? What time window is being compared?
