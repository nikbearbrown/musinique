# Bookmap: *Nanotechnology: Science, Innovation and Opportunity*
**Lynn E. Foster (ed.), 2006**

---

## What the Book Is Actually Teaching

This is not primarily a science book. It's a **commercialization primer** dressed in scientific clothing. The central argument, threaded across all four sections, is: *nanotechnology will matter enormously, but only if the right ecosystem of investors, entrepreneurs, governments, universities, and IP lawyers get aligned.* The science is context; the lesson is institution-building.

---

## Core Principles and How Well Each Is Taught

---

### 1. The S-Curve as a Mental Model for Emerging Technology

**What it claims:** Every technology follows an S-shaped performance curve — slow start, rapid acceleration, plateau. Discontinuities between curves are where disruption happens and where investment either wins or loses.

**How well taught:** Well, by the standards of a business audience. The propeller-aircraft example is concrete and memorable. The biotechnology parallel (animal insulin → recombinant insulin) is genuinely instructive. The S-curve is introduced early and referenced repeatedly, giving the book a unifying spine.

**Where it falls short:** The model is presented as more predictive than it is. The book glosses over the hard question: *how do you know when you're on the early slope vs. approaching the plateau?* That's the actual problem investors face, and it gets no rigorous treatment. The curve also can't tell you which competing S-curve will win — carbon nanotubes vs. nanowires vs. molecular electronics, for instance — which is the real decision problem in 2005 nanotechnology investing.

---

### 2. Commercial Innovation Requires Five Simultaneous Inputs

**What it claims:** Creativity × Management × Team × Investment × Demand. Remove any one and commercialization fails.

**How well taught:** Adequately but superficially. The formula is easy to memorize and directionally correct. The biotechnology narrative (Swanson, Boyer, City of Hope, Genentech) illustrates it well. But the formula is never stress-tested. What happens when demand exists but investment doesn't? When the team is strong but management is weak? The book treats the factors as multiplicative without exploring the tradeoffs.

---

### 3. Hype Is a Structural Feature of Emerging Technology, Not a Bug

**What it claims:** Every new technology generates inflated expectations, a trough of disillusionment, and a slower plateau of productivity. Nanotechnology is not a fad, but the hype cycle will hurt legitimate development by misallocating capital and talent.

**How well taught:** Peter Coffee's chapter is the strongest writing in the book — skeptical, specific, and willing to make firm claims. His distinction between "fad" and "trend," and his point that successful technologies often disappear as labels (the adjective "solid state" becoming archaic), is genuinely insightful. The Gartner hype cycle framework is useful scaffolding.

**Where it falls short:** The chapter is better at diagnosing the problem than prescribing behavior. What should an investor *do* differently knowing that hype cycles exist? The book doesn't answer that with rigor.

---

### 4. University Technology Transfer Is a Human Problem, Not a Process Problem

**What it claims:** The standard "disclosure → patent → license" model is a fiction. Successful technology transfer depends on faculty trust, personal relationships, timing, and the quality of individual OTT staff. Every deal is unique.

**How well taught:** This is one of the book's most honest and underrated chapters (Gilbert and Krieger). The insistence that emphasizing process over unique components "has seriously undercut the potential success of many tech transfer efforts" is a genuinely contrarian and well-supported point. The taxonomy of failure modes — faculty distrust, short-term administrative pressure, OTT turnover — is practically useful.

**Where it falls short:** The chapter identifies what goes wrong but gives almost no guidance on what going right looks like operationally. Caltech's OTT is cited as a success without explaining the mechanism.

---

### 5. Intellectual Property in Nanotechnology Is a Gold Rush with Overlapping Claims

**What it claims:** The patent landscape for nanotechnology is congested, interdisciplinary, and poorly examined. Broad early patents may restrict innovation. Companies need active portfolio strategy, not just defensive filing.

**How well taught:** Serviceable but lawyerly. The Pham/Berman chapter is written for practitioners, which means it's comprehensive but dry. The core strategic insight — that citation patterns reveal licensing leverage and potential roadblocks — is useful. The picket-fence strategy around core patents is well-explained.

**Where it falls short:** The chapter says almost nothing about the specific patent conflicts that were already emerging in 2005 (e.g., competing CNT patents from Smalley's group vs. other institutions). The analysis stays at the level of general principle when readers would benefit from concrete examples.

---

### 6. Molecular Electronics as the Post-Silicon Paradigm

**What it claims:** Moore's Law will hit physical limits around 2017 (per the chapter's prediction). Molecular electronics — carbon nanotubes, self-assembling organic molecules, nanowires — will constitute the next substrate.

**How well taught:** Jurvetson's chapter is the most intellectually ambitious in the book. The 100-year version of Moore's Law across five paradigm shifts is compelling. The treatment of bottom-up vs. top-down manufacturing strategies is clear. The interface problem — how do you connect a molecular-scale device to a macro-scale system? — is correctly identified as the central engineering challenge.

**Where it falls short:** The chapter reads as optimistic VC pitch material. Timelines are compressed, obstacles minimized. The quantum computing company claiming 2008 thumbnail chips with more power than all computers ever built is presented without skepticism. Retrospectively, many of the near-term predictions were wrong. The chapter teaches *how to think like a VC* more than *how to evaluate these technologies.*

---

### 7. The NBIC Convergence Thesis

**What it claims:** Nano, bio, info, and cognitive sciences are converging at the nanoscale. Their synergistic combination will be transformative beyond what any single field could achieve.

**How well taught:** Roco's chapter is visionary but vague. The four-generation product roadmap (passive nanostructures → active nanostructures → 3D nanosystems → heterogeneous molecular systems) is the most structured framework in the book and has aged reasonably well as a schema. The "unity of nature at the nanoscale" argument is philosophically interesting.

**Where it falls short:** The chapter suffers from the weakness common to convergence arguments: it's more assertion than demonstration. The claim that NBIC convergence will improve "human cognition and communication, health and physical capabilities, group and societal outcomes, national security, science and education, and business and organizations" covers everything and therefore predicts nothing specific. The chapter would have been stronger with a single extended example of convergence already underway.

---

### 8. Ethics Must Be Built In, Not Bolted On

**What it claims:** Nanotechnology raises novel ethical questions about health risks, equity of access, and the transformation of human capabilities. These cannot be addressed after the fact.

**How well taught:** Bainbridge's chapter is intellectually honest about the limits of professional ethics as a discipline. The acknowledgment that "ethical principles are not objective or uniform across humanity" is unusual frankness for a book of this type. The organizational recommendations (delegate ethics duties, use outside consultants, keep leaders informed) are practical.

**Where it falls short:** The chapter treats ethics primarily as risk management for corporations rather than as a substantive engagement with the genuinely hard questions — enhancement ethics, equitable access to nano-medicine in developing countries, environmental risks of novel materials. The nano-specific hazard section is thin.

---

## Structural Assessment

**What the book does well:**

The multi-author format is genuinely its strength. No single author could have credibly covered S-curves, molecular electronics, patent strategy, university tech transfer, federal funding, and drug delivery. The diversity of expertise produces real signal.

The framing around the biotech parallel is smart. It gives readers with no nanotechnology background a legible historical analogy and sets appropriate expectations about timescale.

**What the book does poorly:**

The sections don't argue with each other. A venture capitalist (Jurvetson) and a government coordinator (Roco) and a patent lawyer (Pham) are all making claims that sometimes contradict each other, but the book presents them as compatible. A genuine editorial synthesis is missing.

The Feynman epilogue is a missed opportunity. Positioning the 1983 JPL talk as a capstone implies continuity between Feynman's vision and the book's contents — but Feynman's talk is about small machines with no known use, while the book is about commercialization. The irony is never addressed.

**The book's implicit lesson, rarely stated directly:**

The real principle running underneath everything is: *nanotechnology is not a technology, it's a size regime in which multiple technologies happen to converge.* This means it will not produce a single industry — it will infiltrate every industry. This is why the hype cycle analysis is correct: "nanotechnology" as a category will seem to fail even as every specific nanotechnology succeeds. The book gestures at this but never makes it the organizing principle.

---

## Who This Book Actually Teaches Well

Professionals crossing from one domain into nanotechnology — a materials scientist who wants to understand VC thinking, a lawyer who needs scientific context, a government program manager who wants industry perspective. It is a map of the ecosystem, not a guide to navigating it. The principles it teaches are real; the depth at which it teaches them is consistently one level above introductory and one level below actionable.
