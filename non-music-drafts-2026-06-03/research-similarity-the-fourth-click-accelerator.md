### SECTION 5: Similarity — The Fourth Click Accelerator

**Core Claim:** Similarity accelerates clicking by triggering in-group perception. The degree of similarity matters more than the domain of similarity — trivial shared attributes (same name, same birthday, rare fingerprint type) produce statistically indistinguishable effects from substantive shared values.

**Supporting Evidence:**
- Don Byrne's 26-attribute study (University of Texas): participants given fictional surveys 100% congruent with their views rated fictional counterparts 13/14 on attractiveness; those given 100% discordant surveys rated them 4.41/14 — "as big a difference as you can find in social psychology"; which attributes were similar (major vs. trivial) did not affect attractiveness scores
- Santa Clara University donation study: women donated twice as much ($2.07 vs. $1.00) when the solicitor wore a name tag matching their own name
- Santa Clara University favor study: 62% complied with critique request when confederate shared same birthday (vs. 33% control); 80% complied when told they shared a rare fingerprint type
- Kashy et al. longitudinal study (n=300 engaged couples): similarity levels unchanged after decades of marriage — maintained through shared experience accumulation

**Logical Method:** Experimental manipulation of similarity type and degree → behavioral outcome measurement → longitudinal validation of persistence.

**Logical Gaps:**
- Byrne's study used fictional survey responses calibrated by the researcher at his kitchen table. The artificiality is acknowledged but under-examined. Real-world similarity is rarely presented as a clean profile; it is discovered gradually and ambiguously. The experimental design optimizes for a clean effect that may not replicate in messy natural interactions.
- The finding that trivial similarity produces identical effects to substantive similarity is the section's most striking result, but it has an important implication the authors underplay: if any similarity suffices, then similarity is functioning as an *in-group signal*, not as actual evidence of compatibility. The authors acknowledge the in-group mechanism but don't draw the implication that this means similarity-based clicking may be a cognitive shortcut with high false-positive rates.
- The Kashy longitudinal finding (similarity unchanged after decades) is presented as confirming that click relationships are stable. But the authors' own interpretation is that shared experience *maintains* similarity over time — which means the relationship is generating the apparent similarity, not the reverse. This is a chicken-and-egg problem the authors do not resolve.

**Methodological Soundness:** Byrne's work is foundational and replicated. The Santa Clara donation and favor studies are small-scale experiments that would benefit from larger replications. The overall argument is logically coherent but overstates the ecological validity of laboratory findings.
