### PREFACE & INTRODUCTION
**Core Claim:** The big questions — What is the mind? Who am I? Can matter think? Where is the soul? — have no easy answers, and currently no consensus exists on what those answers would even look like. The book's goal is to disturb, not to resolve.

**Supporting Evidence:**
- Thought experiment of Teleclone teleportation establishes the identity problem concretely: if your body is dismantled and reconstructed from different atoms, are you the same person?
- The split-brain cases, dichotic listening experiments (Lackner & Garrett 1973), and Freud's "unconscious" are cited as empirical demonstrations that the mind is not transparent to itself.
- E. R. John's functionalist definition of consciousness (a unified multidimensional representation of state and environment) is presented as an attempt to objectify consciousness — followed immediately by the observation that skeptics fear the "truly mysterious phenomenon" will be left behind by any such definition.

**Logical Method:** Motivational framing via thought experiment. The teleporter puzzle forces the reader to confront that personal identity cannot be resolved by pointing to physical continuity (different atoms), psychological continuity (same memories), or functional continuity (same behavior) without circularity.

**Logical Gaps:**
- The Teleclone example assumes that "you" have a continuous perspective throughout the process. But the puzzle only bites if there is a "you" to track. The introduction does not distinguish between whether the question is conceptual (what do our concepts commit us to?) or empirical (what actually happens to persons?).
- The citation of Freud's expansion of the "thinkable" is historically correct but methodologically uncritical. The introduction treats the acceptance of unconscious mental states as a vindication of expanding conceptual possibility — it does not acknowledge that much of Freudian theory has poor evidential standing.

**Methodological Soundness:** The introduction functions as provocation, not argument. Its claims are appropriately hedged ("we believe," "perhaps"). It establishes the intellectual landscape without pretending to map it definitively.
