# BOOKMAP: Calling Bullshit: The Art of Skepticism in a Data-Driven World
**Carl T. Bergstrom & Jevin D. West (2020) | Random House**

---

## PART 1: SECTION-BY-SECTION LOGICAL MAPPING

---

### PREFACE: The Problem Stated
**Core Claim:** Bullshit is ubiquitous and increasingly dangerous, and has evolved from rhetorical ("old school") to quantitative ("new school") forms that exploit public deference to numbers and data.

**Supporting Evidence:**
- Harry Frankfurt's observation that "one of the most salient features of our culture is that there is so much bullshit"
- Old school bullshit: corporate jargon, political speechifying
- New school bullshit: spurious statistics, p-values deployed as rhetorical shields, "clinically important" results that are not statistically significant
- Authors' course at University of Washington as empirical testbed

**Logical Method:** Taxonomic differentiation between bullshit types, followed by normative claim about the purpose of education (detecting "rot").

**Logical Gaps:**
- The distinction between old school and new school bullshit is intuitive but not rigorously defined. Both involve disregard for truth; the authors have identified a delivery mechanism change, not a conceptual change.
- The civic motive claim ("adequate bullshit detection is essential for survival of liberal democracy") is asserted without derivation—a normative claim treated as axiomatic.

**Methodological Soundness:** The preface functions as advocacy; its claims should be treated as motivating hypotheses.

---

### CHAPTER 1: Bullshit Everywhere
**Core Claim:** Bullshit is evolutionarily ancient, cognitively sophisticated, and proliferates because it is asymmetrically cheap to produce relative to the cost of refuting it (Brandolini's Principle).

**Supporting Evidence:**
- Mantis shrimp bluffing during molting (deception without theory of mind)
- Raven false-caching experiments demonstrating theory of mind in corvids
- Brandolini's Principle: "the amount of energy needed to refute bullshit is an order of magnitude bigger than needed to produce it"
- Wakefield MMR vaccine fraud as case study: 1998 Lancet paper, subsequent retraction, ongoing anti-vax movement despite overwhelming scientific refutation
- Boston Marathon bombing Twitter rumor: 92,000 shares for false story vs. 2,000 corrective tweets

**Logical Method:** Evolutionary argument (deception → theory of mind → language-enabled bullshit) + empirical case studies of bullshit's resistance to correction.

**Logical Gaps:**
- The evolutionary continuity argument is suggestive but overextended. Raven false-caching is interesting, but the inferential leap from bird food-hiding to human political propaganda involves steps the chapter does not fully articulate.
- The Wakefield case conflates scientific fraud with bullshit. Wakefield did not show "disregard for truth"—he actively falsified data. The authors' definition of bullshit (indifference to truth) does not cleanly cover deliberate fraud; this tension is not resolved.
- Brandolini's Principle is cited as coined in 2014 but is presented as if it has been rigorously verified. The authors themselves note aphorisms "could be just more bullshit"—they should apply more skepticism to their own foundational principle.

**Methodological Soundness:** The evolutionary framing is speculative but intellectually honest. The Wakefield case is well-documented and appropriately used.

---

### CHAPTER 2: Medium, Message, and Misinformation
**Core Claim:** The internet's structural properties—zero marginal cost of publishing, click-driven economics, algorithmic amplification of outrage—have systematically degraded the information environment by rewarding emotional resonance over accuracy.

**Supporting Evidence:**
- Printing press parallel: Philippo de Strata's 1474 complaints mirror contemporary concerns
- BuzzSumo/Steve Rayson study: 100 million articles in 2017; most viral headlines promise emotional experiences ("will make you"), not information
- Facebook: 126 million US users exposed to Russian propaganda (2017 admission)
- MIT study: false news spreads faster and further on Twitter than true news
- Net neutrality comment fraud: 21.7 million comments, ~500,000 sent simultaneously in one second

**Logical Method:** Historical analogy (printing press) + structural economic analysis of click-driven incentives + empirical data on misinformation spread.

**Logical Gaps:**
- The printing press analogy is deployed as comfort ("things turned out fine before") and as warning simultaneously. The authors cannot have it both ways without specifying what conditions made the printing press's disruption ultimately benign and whether those conditions hold today.
- The chapter attributes declining information quality primarily to structural/algorithmic forces but does not adequately weigh demand-side factors: audiences actively prefer emotionally engaging content. This conflation of supply-side and demand-side causes limits prescriptive clarity.
- The Russian propaganda exposure figure (126 million users) is presented without comparison to legitimate political advertising exposure during the same period. The relative magnitude is unknown.

**Methodological Soundness:** The structural analysis is well-grounded. Individual empirical claims are sourced. The chapter's prescriptive conclusion (education is the best defense) is asserted more than derived.

---

### CHAPTER 3: The Nature of Bullshit
**Core Claim:** Bullshit is best defined as language, statistics, or data graphics intended to persuade or impress through distraction, intimidation, or overwhelming an audience with disregard for truth—distinguished from lying by indifference to truth rather than active deception.

**Supporting Evidence:**
- Frankfurt's taxonomy: bullshitter does not assert false propositions, they are simply indifferent to the truth/falsity question
- GA Cohen's test: if negating a sentence doesn't change its meaning, it's bullshit
- Latour's black box concept: claims can be shielded from scrutiny by technical complexity
- Wu and Zhang (2016) criminal face recognition paper: algorithm trained on smiling professionals vs. frowning convicts; likely detecting smile presence, not criminality

**Logical Method:** Conceptual analysis of bullshit (Frankfurt, Cohen) + Latourian sociology of science → application to concrete case (criminal face recognition).

**Logical Gaps:**
- The authors adopt Frankfurt's definition but broaden it to include quantitative forms. The extension is plausible but creates a definitional stretch: is a sloppily run study bullshit or merely error? The chapter implies these are distinct but does not specify the boundary.
- The Latour black box concept is useful but not uniformly applicable. Latour's point was specifically about scientific credibility networks; the authors deploy it more broadly. This is legitimate but the conceptual fit should be flagged.
- The Wu and Zhang critique is compelling but the authors state "it appears that the authors have confused innate facial features with facial expressions." This is strong inference from publicly available data, not a direct examination of the training set. The hedging is appropriate but should be more prominent.

**Methodological Soundness:** The Wu and Zhang case study is the book's first extended demonstration of "calling bullshit without opening the black box." The logic holds: if the training data is differentially confounded, no statistical analysis of the output is required to question the conclusion.

### CHAPTER 4: Causality
**Core Claim:** The most pervasive form of inferential bullshit is the unwarranted leap from observed correlation to claimed causation. This error occurs in peer-reviewed journals, popular press, and political discourse with regularity.

**Supporting Evidence:**
- Never Been Kissed study: self-esteem correlated with kissing, but direction of causality unclear
- Housing prices vs. fertility rate (Zillow 2018): correctly reported as correlation; Market Watch headline incorrectly implies causation
- Exercise and cancer (JAMA 2016): researchers properly hedged; Time, LA Times, US News all reported as causal
- Beer pitchers and consumption: ordering pitchers predicts quantity consumed—but likely because people order pitchers when they intend to drink more, not because pitchers cause drinking
- Marshmallow test replication failure: parental SES confounds both patience and academic success
- Fever as beneficial: experiments confirm fever reduction worsens outcomes—causal chain established through randomized manipulation and animal experiments
- Smoking and cancer: R.A. Fisher's alternative hypothesis (pre-cancerous states cause smoking discomfort) as illustration of how causation can be reversed even by distinguished statisticians

**Logical Method:** Systematic taxonomy of causality errors: reverse causation, common cause, selection effects, spurious correlation, post hoc ergo propter hoc. Multiple domains illustrate the same inferential failure.

**Logical Gaps:**
- The chapter's own examples sometimes undercut its analytical tidiness. The exercise-cancer relationship may involve genuine causal pathways (exercise reduces systemic inflammation, etc.) alongside confounds. The authors treat it as pure correlation-misrepresented-as-causation, but the scientific question is legitimately unresolved.
- The Marshmallow Test replication discussion is excellent but compressed. The key finding (SES as confound) deserves more methodological precision: how was SES controlled, and what effect size remained?
- The authors cite Fisher's tobacco-cancer alternative hypothesis as an illustration of causality errors without noting that Fisher's position was almost certainly influenced by his own smoking habit and tobacco industry funding—a conflicts-of-interest point directly relevant to the book's themes.

**Methodological Soundness:** This is the book's strongest analytical chapter. The causal diagram framework is applied consistently. The range of examples prevents any single case from carrying the entire argumentative weight.

### CHAPTER 5: Numbers and Nonsense
**Core Claim:** Numbers create false impressions of precision and objectivity; selective choice of denominator, unit, baseline, or comparison group can make the same underlying fact tell any story the presenter chooses.

**Supporting Evidence:**
- Angels' share of whiskey: same evaporation loss expressible as 58% of starting volume or 138% of final volume—mathematically equivalent, rhetorically opposite
- "25% less fat" on Whoppers: comparison group undefined, metric irrelevant to health
- 99.9% caffeine-free hot cocoa: standard coffee is also 99.9% caffeine-free by weight
- Breitbart DACA crime statistics: 2,139 absolute number presented without rate comparison; DACA recipients have lower crime rates than US citizens
- Google's 0.25% problematic search queries: sounds small; equals 13 million queries per day
- Bitcoin 34% loss vs. 52% loss depending on baseline choice
- Goodhart's Law applied to: journal impact factors, auto dealership bonuses, college rankings, standardized testing
- US News class size metric: rewards percentage of small classes, not experienced mean class size—perverse incentive documented with Marquette data

**Logical Method:** Repeated demonstration of the same logical structure: a numerically accurate claim that misleads through missing context, inappropriate baseline, or unit manipulation.

**Logical Gaps:**
- The Goodhart's Law section is excellent but taxonomically separate from the "numbers as misleading" theme. The mechanism is different: Goodhart describes measurement-induced behavioral change, while the earlier examples describe presentation-induced perceptual distortion. The authors blend these without signaling the distinction.
- The DACA example is analytically sound but the authors do not fully note that their own comparison (DACA vs. US citizens) may have its own selection bias: DACA eligibility requires clean criminal records, while the US citizen population has no such filter. This complicates but does not invalidate the comparison.
- The chapter demonstrates many forms of numerical manipulation but does not offer a unified principle beyond "context matters"—which is tautologically true. A clearer structural taxonomy would strengthen the chapter's analytical contribution.

**Methodological Soundness:** Strong. Multiple worked examples with explicit arithmetic. The Marquette class size analysis is the chapter's most rigorous demonstration—a genuine empirical contribution.

---

### CHAPTER 6: Selection Bias
**Core Claim:** Selection bias—the systematic difference between the sample studied and the population of interest—is one of the most pervasive sources of misleading inference in science, journalism, social media, and everyday reasoning.

**Supporting Evidence:**
- Solitude ski resort: skiers present at Solitude prefer Solitude; not a random sample of all skiers
- GEICO savings claim: only people who save money switch insurers; every insurer can honestly claim that switchers save money
- Criminal face recognition case (returned): training data selects smiling non-criminals and frowning convicts
- iSTART and reading: students with email addresses had higher insurance claims—because email was only collected during claims processing
- Friendship paradox: your friends have more friends than you do (formally proven, empirically confirmed with 720 million Facebook users)
- Musicians' age at death: rap/hip-hop artists appear to die younger because the genre is only 40 years old—right-censoring creates the illusion of elevated mortality
- Corporate wellness programs: participants self-select; randomized trial found minimal effect; illustrating how selection bias inflated apparent benefits in observational studies
- Berkson's paradox: attractive people seem like jerks; top coders make poor employees—both explained by selection into the observable sample

**Logical Method:** The chapter proceeds from intuitive examples to formally stated statistical concepts (right-censoring, Berkson's paradox, observation selection effects), grounding each in documented empirical evidence.

**Logical Gaps:**
- The musicians' mortality analysis is compelling but the authors note the original researcher "acknowledges the right-censoring issue in her article." The researchers' intellectual honesty is not fully credited—the authors' critique applies to the graph and media coverage, not necessarily the underlying research.
- The Berkson's paradox discussion (attractive people seem like jerks) is presented as if selection into dating pools is the complete explanation. But there is an independent psychological literature on the "what is beautiful is good" heuristic that the authors ignore—attractive people may genuinely receive more indulgence for bad behavior, creating real correlates of the Berkson effect.
- The friendship paradox proof is correct but the authors skip the full mathematical derivation, which would demonstrate that this is a necessary mathematical truth, not an empirical contingency. Stating it as a proved theorem rather than an observed pattern would strengthen the section.

**Methodological Soundness:** This is the book's most technically sophisticated chapter. The right-censoring analysis is particularly strong—both intuitive and formally rigorous.

### CHAPTER 7: Data Visualization
**Core Claim:** Data graphics can mislead through duck ornamentation (prettiness over clarity), glass slippers (forcing data into inappropriate visual forms), axis manipulation, and violations of the principle of proportional ink—even when the underlying data are accurate.

**Supporting Evidence:**
- Reuters inverted y-axis graph (Florida gun deaths): axis inversion made rising murders appear to fall after Stand Your Ground legislation; designer stated she preferred "negative" representation of deaths—not deliberate deception
- USA Today data ducks: lipstick bars, ice cream cone pie charts
- Periodic tables of everything: co-opt Mendeleev's structure without the structural logic that makes it meaningful
- Subway map metaphors: misapplied to scientists, Shakespeare plays, philosophy
- Quebec trust levels: truncated y-axis exaggerated differences; corrected after reader complaints
- Clinton Instagram wage gap: bars visually misrepresent stated percentages
- Tennessee job growth bar chart: proportional ink violation
- College football recruiting budget vs. success: correlation r=0.78; but causality unclear (does money buy wins or do wins generate revenue?)
- 3D bar charts, donut charts: geometric properties violate proportional ink principle
- Autism/MMR double y-axis chart: axes scaled to force visual correlation

**Logical Method:** Principle of proportional ink as master framework: the amount of ink representing a value should be proportional to that value. Most visualization errors are applications or violations of this principle.

**Logical Gaps:**
- The authors establish "never assume malice when incompetence explains it" as a principle but do not apply it consistently. The Reuters graph is charitably explained; the autism/MMR chart is presented without equivalent charity. The asymmetry is not justified.
- The principle of proportional ink is stated clearly but its application to line graphs (where ink use is not proportional to value) requires additional justification. The authors provide it but the reasoning is more complex than they acknowledge: position, not area, encodes value in line graphs.
- The chapter conflates aesthetic failures (ducks) with epistemic failures (axis manipulation). These are meaningfully different: ducks obscure data through distraction; manipulated axes actively deceive. The moral weight is different; the analytical treatment should distinguish them more clearly.

**Methodological Soundness:** The principle of proportional ink is a genuinely useful analytical framework. The chapter's examples are well-chosen. The Reuters case study demonstrates admirable intellectual charity.

### CHAPTER 8: Calling Bullshit on Big Data
**Core Claim:** Machine learning systems are not magical—they are only as good as their training data. Most celebrated AI failures and false promises trace not to algorithmic errors but to garbage-in-garbage-out data problems that non-specialists can identify without opening the technical black box.

**Supporting Evidence:**
- Google Flu Trends: accurate for 2 years, then missed by 2x due to Google Suggest feature changing search behavior; overfitting to a 2003–2008 window
- USPS handwriting recognition: 98% accuracy—appropriate training data, bounded problem, genuine success
- Gaydar algorithm (Wang & Kuzinsky): claimed to detect sexual orientation from facial photos; trained on self-selected dating site photos vs. official ID photos; most likely detecting grooming/self-presentation, not facial structure; claimed prenatal hormone theory support not established
- Criminal face recognition (Wu & Zhang, returned): smile detector, not criminality detector
- Facebook chatbot "inventing its own language": media panic; researchers unconcerned; chatbots had developed repetitive nonsense, not a novel language
- AlphaGo: genuinely impressive, but opacity of decision-making is a feature and a problem
- Husky/wolf classifier: learning snow backgrounds, not animal morphology
- Amazon hiring algorithm: discriminated against women because trained on male-dominated hire history
- COMPAS recidivism algorithm: misclassifies Black defendants as future criminals at nearly 2x the rate of white defendants
- Curse of dimensionality: adding variables requires exponentially more training data

**Logical Method:** The chapter's core analytical move is consistent: identify the training data, identify the labels, identify systematic bias in either, declare the output claims suspect—without examining the algorithm itself.

**Logical Gaps:**
- The Google Flu Trends case is described as a failure of overfitting, but the authors do not clearly distinguish between (a) the algorithm overfitting to 2003–2008 search patterns and (b) the environmental change caused by Google Suggest. These are different failure modes with different prescriptions.
- The Gaydar paper critique is methodologically sound (training data confound, unfair human comparison), but the authors' conclusion that "we suspect the most likely explanation involves grooming and self-presentation" is inferred, not proven. They should flag this more explicitly.
- The chapter argues that "good data" is more important than algorithm sophistication, but the USPS success story involves both good data AND a well-defined problem. The authors underweight problem structure as an independent factor.

**Methodological Soundness:** The "garbage in, garbage out" framework is the chapter's primary analytical contribution and is well-applied. The call for algorithmic transparency and accountability is appropriate but underdeveloped relative to the technical analysis.

### CHAPTER 9: The Susceptibility of Science
**Core Claim:** Science is humanity's best method for understanding the world, but the incentive structures of academic publishing—priority rules, publication bias, p-hacking, Goodhart's Law applied to p-values, and predatory journals—systematically distort the published literature away from truth.

**Supporting Evidence:**
- Publication bias: Turner's FDA clinical trials analysis: 51 of 74 antidepressant trials published; 94% of published results positive vs. 51% of registered trials positive; 11 of 14 negative results published were "reframed" as positive
- Replication crisis: Open Science Collaboration replicated 39/100 social psychology experiments; economics journals: 11/18 replicated
- P-hacking: Simmons et al. demonstrated that p-hacking can "prove" that listening to the Beatles makes people younger—a logically impossible claim—at p<0.05
- Publication bias + base rate fallacy: Ioannidis' argument that most published findings are false under realistic assumptions about hypothesis base rates and file-drawer effects
- Wakefield MMR fraud (returned): most thorough documented case of scientific fraud with public health consequences
- Predatory journals: John McCool published a Seinfeld plot as a urology case report; accepted within days for $799 fee
- Goodhart's Law applied to p-values: once p<0.05 is required for publication, p-values no longer serve as unbiased evidence
- Tech-bait science: news media covers 156 papers on disease risk; when 35 are subsequently disconfirmed, only 4 news articles report the failure

**Logical Method:** The chapter structures the problem as a series of interlocking incentive failures: individual scientists → journal editors → popular press → public. Each layer amplifies distortions from the previous layer.

**Logical Gaps:**
- The Ioannidis argument ("most published findings are false") is presented with appropriate nuance: "He is overly pessimistic because he makes unrealistic assumptions." But the authors do not provide their own estimate. What fraction of published findings are false, given more realistic assumptions? The critique of Ioannidis requires a positive alternative claim.
- The replication crisis statistics (39/100, 11/18) are presented without accounting for the non-random selection of studies chosen for replication. Replication attempts may preferentially target surprising or high-profile findings, which have lower prior probability of being true.
- The predatory journal critique is accurate but the authors conflate several distinct failure modes: low-quality peer review, fee-based publishing, and deliberate fraud. Not all open-access publishing deserves equal criticism—the chapter's framing could create collateral damage to legitimate open-access journals.

**Methodological Soundness:** The Turner FDA analysis is the chapter's strongest empirical contribution—a natural experiment that provides direct evidence of publication bias rather than inference from indirect signals. The p-hacking demonstration (Beatles making people younger) is elegantly designed.

### CHAPTER 10: Spotting Bullshit
**Core Claim:** Effective bullshit detection requires cultivated habits of mind, applied consistently: questioning sources, demanding fair comparisons, applying the too-good/too-bad-to-be-true heuristic, thinking in orders of magnitude, and actively resisting confirmation bias.

**Supporting Evidence:**
- Michael Bennett burning flag photograph: selection of Vets for Trump as source, implausible narrative (no media present, no players corroborated), poor Photoshop quality
- Airport security trays carry more germs than toilets: specific to respiratory viruses, not all pathogens
- 40% decline in international student applications: tweet misrepresented "applications declined at 40% of schools" as "applications declined by 40%"—original survey itself showed equal numbers of schools with increases and decreases
- Most dangerous cities: St. Louis and Detroit rank high partly because city boundaries exclude suburbs; violent crime rate correlates with fraction of metro population inside city limits (r² = 0.17, p = 0.005)
- John Smith Fermi estimate: ~10,000 John Smiths in UK, not 121,000; actual count ~4,700
- White Cliffs of Dover sea level rise: annual erosion displaces volume equivalent to a 3-angstrom sea level rise globally
- Food stamp fraud: $70 million in fraud vs. ~$70 billion program = <0.1% fraud rate; Fox's number was actually too low, not too high; actual fraud ~$900M/year still within retail shrinkage norms

**Logical Method:** Six heuristics operationalized through worked examples. Each example demonstrates the heuristic's application and its limits.

**Logical Gaps:**
- The "too good or too bad to be true" heuristic is epistemically circular: it advises skepticism toward extreme claims, but extreme claims are sometimes true (plate tectonics, natural selection, relativity all seemed implausible initially). The heuristic needs a better formulation: "extreme claims require proportional evidence."
- The violent crime/city boundary analysis is the chapter's most analytically sophisticated section but is presented without discussion of potential confounds (e.g., cities with tight boundaries may have specific historical or governance characteristics that independently affect crime rates).
- The confirmation bias section warns against confirming pre-existing beliefs but does not discuss motivated skepticism—the equally dangerous tendency to apply excessive scrutiny to claims that threaten one's worldview. This asymmetry is a significant omission.

**Methodological Soundness:** The Fermi estimation approach is well-taught and practically applicable. The food stamp analysis demonstrates that the too-good/too-bad heuristic cuts in unexpected directions (Fox's number was too low, not too high).

### CHAPTER 11: Refuting Bullshit
**Core Claim:** Effective refutation requires not merely spotting bullshit but constructing a public, appropriately targeted repudiation using specific rhetorical and analytical techniques: reductio ad absurdum, counter-examples, analogies, figure redrawing, and null models.

**Supporting Evidence:**
- Reductio ad absurdum: AJ Tatum et al. predicted women would outsprint men by 2156; Ken Rice's letter pointed out the model predicts sub-zero sprint times by 2636
- Dead salmon fMRI study: demonstrated that uncorrected multiple comparison testing finds "significant" brain activity in a dead fish; won an Ig Nobel Prize
- Trees as counter-example: physicist's model predicting that long-lived organisms require adaptive immune systems refuted instantly by immunologist noting trees are long-lived and lack adaptive immunity
- Euler's sum of powers conjecture: disproved by single counter-example (27⁵ + 84⁵ + 110⁵ + 133⁵ = 144⁵) after 200 years of failed proofs
- Mercer Street traffic project: "two seconds faster" headline misleading because project accommodated 30,000 more cars per day; analogy to Felix Hernandez contract and Mariners batting average
- Vaccine one-percentage-point reduction: analogy to seatbelts having comparable magnitude of risk reduction
- Null model for athlete senescence: shows same downward performance trend emerges from sample size differences without any aging effect; the original graph does not prove senescence

**Logical Method:** Each technique is defined, illustrated with historical examples, and bounded by conditions of appropriate use. The chapter closes with ethical constraints on calling bullshit.

**Logical Gaps:**
- The "be correct" imperative is stated but not operationalized. How should one calibrate confidence before calling bullshit? The dead salmon paper worked because it was deliberately absurd; real cases involve genuine uncertainty.
- The "well-actually guy" taxonomy is rhetorically effective but analytically imprecise. The authors distinguish calling bullshit from pedantry by relevance and intent, but these are often genuinely ambiguous in practice. The monotreme example the authors use is well-chosen, but most real cases are murkier.
- The psychology of debunking section draws on research suggesting that repeating a myth before debunking it can strengthen belief in the myth. The authors note this but do not fully reconcile it with their recommended technique of stating the myth clearly before refuting it.

**Methodological Soundness:** The null model section is the chapter's most technically original contribution. The concept—showing that an observed pattern arises under a null hypothesis requiring no interesting mechanism—is a powerful analytical tool underused in popular science communication.

## BRIDGE: Synthesizing the Logical Architecture

The book's argumentative structure is a 3-layer problem statement followed by a 2-layer solution:

**Layer 1 (Chapters 1–3):** What is bullshit and why does it exist? Answer: an evolutionarily grounded behavior, amplified by information technology, defined by indifference to truth rather than active deception.

**Layer 2 (Chapters 4–9):** What are the specific forms bullshit takes in quantitative reasoning? Answer: causality errors, numerical manipulation, selection bias, visualization abuse, algorithmic credulity, and scientific publication bias.

**Layer 3 (Chapters 10–11):** How do we detect and refute it? Answer: six detection heuristics, five refutation techniques, bounded by ethical constraints.

**Three structural tensions run throughout:**

*Tension 1: The Expert/Layperson Gap.* The book's central promise is that non-specialists can identify quantitative bullshit "without opening the black box." This is proven for some cases (criminal face recognition) and assumed for others (FMRI statistical correction, deep learning bias). The book does not consistently specify which problems are accessible to non-specialists and which genuinely require domain expertise. The dead salmon paper required expertise in neuroimaging statistics to design, even if its point can be appreciated by non-specialists.

*Tension 2: Science as Victim and Perpetrator.* Chapters 1–8 repeatedly appeal to scientific evidence as the corrective to bullshit. Chapter 9 then documents systematic bullshit within science itself. The book resolves this by arguing that "science works" at the institutional level despite individual failures, but this resolution is asserted rather than demonstrated. The claim that cumulative scientific self-correction overcomes p-hacking and publication bias is a faith claim about long-run dynamics that the authors do not prove.

*Tension 3: The Detection/Production Asymmetry.* Brandolini's Principle establishes that bullshit is cheaper to produce than refute. The solution chapters (10–11) describe refutation techniques that are labor-intensive: Fermi estimation, null models, figure redrawing, counter-examples. The book does not resolve the asymmetry—it teaches readers to fight expensive battles when the enemy's cost structure is fundamentally cheaper. This does not undermine the prescriptions, but it does limit them: cultivated skepticism is necessary but not sufficient.

**Most proven claims:**
- Causation cannot be inferred from correlation without experimental or quasi-experimental evidence
- Publication bias systematically inflates the proportion of positive findings in the literature (Turner FDA analysis is the strongest direct evidence)
- Selection bias can make entirely accurate data generate entirely misleading inferences
- Axis manipulation and proportional ink violations systematically distort visual perception of data

**Most significant unproven claims:**
- That adequate bullshit detection is sufficient to sustain liberal democracy (normative claim with no empirical support)
- That non-specialists can reliably open most black boxes using only input/output analysis (true for some cases, overstated as a general principle)
- That science's self-correcting properties will overcome systematic incentive failures documented in Chapter 9 (optimistic faith claim)

## PART 2: LITERARY REVIEW ESSAY

---

# The Asymmetry Problem No Bullshit Book Can Escape

There is a paradox at the center of every serious attempt to arm ordinary people against quantitative deception, and *Calling Bullshit* by Carl Bergstrom and Jevin West is serious enough to deserve having it named directly. The paradox is this: the book's most important analytical contribution—Brandolini's Principle, the observation that refuting bullshit costs an order of magnitude more than producing it—directly undermines the project the book undertakes. If the energy asymmetry is real, then teaching citizens to refute bullshit is a strategy that can never scale to the problem. You are arming infantry against an opponent who has air superiority.

Bergstrom and West know this. They are good enough scientists and honest enough authors that they cannot quite hide the tension. And the considerable achievement of their book rests precisely on what they do despite knowing it.

---

The book's ambition is large and its execution is disciplined. Bergstrom and West want to do for quantitative reasoning what media literacy did for photographic manipulation: teach a skeptical default that is calibrated to actual threat surfaces rather than anxious about everything or credulous about anything. Their taxonomy of bullshit types is among the book's most durable contributions. The distinction between "old school bullshit" (rhetoric, jargon, weasel words) and "new school bullshit" (statistical snake oil, algorithmic mysticism, data graphic manipulation) is not merely taxonomic cleverness. It identifies a genuine shift in the mechanism of misleading: where once the persuader exploited audiences' susceptibility to authority and eloquence, now they exploit audiences' deference to quantification. The Wakefield vaccine-autism fraud, Google Flu Trends, the Wu and Zhang criminal face recognition algorithm—these are all cases where the epistemic armor of "data-driven analysis" was used offensively, as a shield against scrutiny rather than a window into reality.

The black box framework is the book's most analytically original tool. The core insight is that most quantitative bullshit does not require opening the algorithm to refute. It requires examining what went in and what came out. Wu and Zhang's algorithm for detecting criminality from facial photos need not be reverse-engineered; it is sufficient to observe that their training data systematically confounded facial expression with facial structure. The criminals were photographed in government ID conditions; the non-criminals were photographed in professional headshots. That the algorithm detected the presence of smiles, not the presence of criminality, follows from training data alone. You do not need to be a machine learning researcher to see this. You need to be a careful reader.

I find the black box insight genuinely useful, and find that Bergstrom and West deploy it with admirable consistency across domains. The gaydar algorithm fails for the same structural reason as the criminality algorithm: self-selected dating site photographs are confounded with grooming, expression, and self-presentation. Google Flu Trends fails because search behavior is not a stable proxy for illness once Google begins auto-completing queries. COMPAS produces racially biased recidivism scores because it was trained on historical incarceration data that reflects a racially biased criminal justice system. In each case, the analytical move is the same: trace the training data contamination forward to the output claim. You never need to know how the model works.

---

Chapter 6 on selection bias is where the book reaches its highest level of technical rigor. The musicians' mortality analysis—demonstrating that apparent genre differences in age at death are artifacts of right-censoring (rap is 40 years old; only prematurely deceased rap artists are available for analysis)—is analytically elegant and pedagogically powerful. The right-censoring point is not obvious; it requires genuinely non-trivial statistical intuition. That Bergstrom and West can make it comprehensible without mathematics, through the chameleon mortality metaphor, represents a real pedagogical achievement.

Similarly, the Friendship Paradox—that most people have fewer friends than their friends do, a mathematical necessity proven for any network where nodes have heterogeneous degrees—is presented not as a counterintuitive curiosity but as a structural illustration of how observation selection effects can create systematic misimpressions without any deliberate deception. Your friends have more friends than you do because people with more friends are present in more friendship networks, not because you specifically chose popular friends. The extension to class sizes, bus waiting times, and airport shuttle queuing demonstrates the generality of the principle. These are the sections where the book earns its subtitle.

The Turner FDA analysis in Chapter 9 is the book's single strongest empirical contribution, and it deserves more prominence than it receives. By exploiting the FDA's mandatory pre-registration of clinical trials, Turner created a natural experiment that directly measures the file-drawer effect without relying on inference. Of 74 registered antidepressant trials, 38 were positive. Of those 38 positive trials, 37 were published. Of the 36 negative or ambiguous trials, 14 were published—and 11 of those 14 were reframed to appear positive. The result is a published literature that reads as 94% positive for antidepressants when the underlying evidence is 51% positive. This is not speculation about publication bias; it is a direct measurement of it. That the authors do not make this the book's centerpiece strikes me as a strategic error. It is their strongest gun and they keep it holstered until Chapter 9.

---

The book's deepest limitation is structural rather than technical. Bergstrom and West are trying to solve Brandolini's Principle by ignoring it. The asymmetry between bullshit production and bullshit refutation means that the solution cannot be individual. No population of individually trained skeptics can keep pace with the industrial production of motivated misinformation, algorithmic amplification of outrage, and the structural incentives of click-driven media. The book's implicit model—teach enough people to spot and call bullshit, and the information environment improves—does not account for the dynamics it has documented.

Consider the Wakefield case, which runs through the book as a recurring motif. Wakefield published in 1998. The paper was retracted in 2010. The evidence against an MMR-autism link now includes over 1.3 million children across multiple countries and multiple designs. The corrective effort consumed hundreds of millions of dollars in research funding over two decades. The anti-vaccination movement remains active and vaccine rates in wealthy coastal cities remain below herd immunity thresholds for measles. Brandolini's Principle has won. The bullshit is still operational decades after the truth established its case beyond any reasonable scientific standard.

Bergstrom and West's response to this problem is civic optimism dressed as pragmatism: "we cannot possibly do this for every piece of bullshit we find. Pick your battles." This is honest advice. It is also an admission of defeat that the book's framing does not fully acknowledge. If the asymmetry is real, individual bullshit detection is triage at best. The deeper structural interventions—algorithmic accountability, mandatory pre-registration, funding for replication science, redesigned journal economics—appear in the book as asides rather than as the main prescriptive conclusion.

---

There is one chapter where the book's tensions resolve into something genuinely satisfying: Chapter 11 on refutation techniques, and specifically the null model concept. The null model idea—demonstrate that an observed pattern arises under a boring hypothesis requiring no interesting mechanism—is analytically powerful precisely because it inverts Brandolini's Principle. A well-constructed null model is cheaper to build than the complex alternative explanation it replaces. The athletes' performance decline data seemed to show senescence; the null model showed that sample size differences produce the same pattern even with no aging. The null model required one simulation. The original interpretation required decades of physiological research.

This is the book's most useful gift to non-specialists: a technique that partially closes the asymmetry. Not by making refutation cheap in general, but by identifying a specific class of cases where the null hypothesis generates the observable pattern—where complexity dissolves into simplicity on the skeptical side. The dead salmon fMRI poster is the most memorable illustration: a single deliberate absurdity that exposed a methodological failure affecting thousands of published papers. The asymmetry, for once, ran the right direction.

The harder truth that Bergstrom and West approach but do not quite state is that this reversal is rare. Most bullshit does not have a clean null model. Most selection bias cannot be quantified from publicly available data. Most p-hacking is invisible from outside the research team. The intellectual tools in Chapters 10 and 11 are real and worth learning. They will help you navigate a world awash with misleading quantification. They will not drain that world.

What Bergstrom and West have done—and it matters—is give the literate non-specialist a framework that is sophisticated enough to be useful without being so demanding as to be inaccessible. That the framework cannot scale to the problem does not mean it is wrong. It means it is necessary but not sufficient. The book you are holding is the individual component of what must ultimately be an institutional solution. Read it as the starting point it is.
