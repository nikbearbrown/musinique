### SECTION 10: Angel Investors
**Core Claim:** Angel investment is accessible through networks, tax-efficient through EIS/SEIS schemes, and increasingly active in the UK market.

**Supporting Evidence:**
- London Funding Conference data: average angel investment £42,000, average equity stake 8%, average angel age 53, 93% male
- UKBA (Jenny Tooth) interview: EIS accounts for approximately 25% of European startup investment; angels use EIS in ~75% of investments; SEIS bringing new investors into the market
- Angels Den: 100+ applications per day; 90%+ rejected; "there is no shortage of angel funding but a real lack of deals good enough to fund"
- Beer & Partners: reviews 3,000 plans/year; accepts ~100 as clients; raises £15M+/year; 45%+ of clients raise £100K–£3M
- Angel CoFund: invests £100K–£1M alongside syndicates; 80% of companies presenting to investment committee receive funding; average investment ~£300K
- EIS: 30% income tax relief on up to £1M/year; CGT exemption on disposal
- SEIS (April 2012): 50% income tax relief on up to £100K investment; CGT exemption; qualifying companies raise up to £150K

**Logical Gap — The Rocktails Case Study:**
Helen McAvoy's Dragons' Den account is the most analytically interesting content in the book. Rocktails received an offer of £80,000 for 40% equity (implying £200K valuation) and declined it. Their stated reasoning: giving up 40% would prevent future fundraising without sacrificing nearly all remaining equity. This is correct equity management reasoning. The guide presents their decision sympathetically, which is appropriate. But the case study implicitly demonstrates a key limit of the angel ecosystem: the terms available to businesses without negotiating leverage can be highly dilutive, and a TV platform that generates enormous publicity generates offers calibrated to exploit founder desperation rather than business value.

**Logical Gaps:**
- Angels Den's Bill Morrow is the guide's most candid practitioner: "We reject over 90% of those who apply." This is a base rate. It should be the headline of the angels section, not buried in an interview. A 10% or lower acceptance rate at one of the UK's larger angel matching services, applied to the general distribution of applicants, means most readers of this guide will not raise angel capital.
- The 93% male statistic for angel investors is reported without commentary. The systematic underrepresentation of women in angel investing, and the documented funding gap for women founders, receives no analysis.
- EIS/SEIS tax relief benefits are described accurately but entirely from the investor's perspective. The guide does not analyze the implication from the business side: qualifying for EIS/SEIS imposes restrictions on the company's activities and ownership structure that constrain strategic flexibility.
- The Angel CoFund's "80% of companies presenting receive funding" is the guide's most impressive single success rate. But this applies only to companies that have already been vetted through a lead angel syndicate — the 80% is a conditional rate applied to a highly pre-screened group, not a general population of applicants.

**Methodological Soundness:** The angel section contains the guide's best base rate data (Angels Den rejection rate, Beer & Partners client conversion rate) but frames it as background rather than primary decision-relevant information. The EIS/SEIS analysis is advocacy-oriented (these schemes make the UK a great place for angel investing) rather than evaluative.

## Extended Research Notes

**Pantry note:** `pantry/notes-angel-investors.md`

Key additions: angel capital is high-risk equity, not friendly free money. EIS/SEIS can make risk attractive to investors but imposes eligibility/holding constraints. Platform and cofund success rates are conditional on heavy prescreening. UK angel gender gaps remain important.

Settled: UK angel markets are selective, networked, and tax-incentivized. Contested: whether platforms democratize access or reproduce network bias.

Teaching move: model dilution across rounds and decide whether a high-publicity, high-dilution offer is rational.
