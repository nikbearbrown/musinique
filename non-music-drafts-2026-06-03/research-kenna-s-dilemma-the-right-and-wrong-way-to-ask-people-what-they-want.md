### CHAPTER 5: Kenna's Dilemma — The Right and Wrong Way to Ask People What They Want

**Core Claim:** Market research based on direct consumer questioning systematically misleads because (a) consumers cannot accurately report their own preferences, (b) artificial testing contexts (sip tests, phone playbacks) fail to replicate real consumption conditions, and (c) truly novel products cannot be fairly evaluated by people with no frame of reference for them.

**Supporting Evidence:**
- Kenna: praised by Craig Coleman (Atlantic Records co-president), Fred Durst (Limp Bizkit), Paul McGuinness (U2 manager), MTV2 (475 airplays); failed market research on three separate occasions (Music Research study: "Free Time" scored 1.3 on rock stations, 0.8 on R&B; Pick the Hits: two average scores, eight below average; conclusion: "lacks a core audience")
- Pepsi Challenge: 57% of tasters preferred Pepsi in blind sip tests; New Coke beat Pepsi by 6-8 points in blind taste tests of hundreds of thousands of consumers; New Coke was a catastrophic failure; Coke (inferior in blind tests) remains the world's number one soft drink
- Carol Dollard (Pepsi product development): sip tests systematically bias toward sweetness; Pepsi's "citrusy burst" (advantage in sip tests) dissipates over a full can; "home use test" produces opposite results from CLT
- Louis Cheskin's sensation transference research: margarine colored yellow was rated identically to butter when context obscured the distinction; Christian Brothers brandy failed not because of taste (blind test: comparable to E&J) or brand name (named test: Christian Brothers preferred) but because of package design (bottle-present test: E&J preferred); swapping bottles reversed the result; redesigned bottle restored market share
- Herman Miller Aeron chair: comfort scores reached 8+; aesthetic scores never exceeded 6 in any prototype; facility managers called it "the chair of death" and "lawn furniture"; it became the best-selling chair in Herman Miller history and was widely imitated
- All in the Family and The Mary Tyler Moore Show both tested poorly (low 40s on a 100-point scale; negative program analyzer results) and became two of the most successful television programs in American history

**Logical Method:** Multiple parallel cases showing that consumer research failed to predict genuine hits, with systematic explanation of why (wrong context, novel product, sensation transference).

**Logical Gaps:**
- The cases presented are all cases where the research was wrong. Gladwell does not examine the base rate—how often market research correctly predicts outcomes. If it is correct 80% of the time and wrong 20% of the time, and Gladwell has found the most spectacular failures, this is survivorship bias in the critique.
- The Kenna case is the chapter's emotional centerpiece, but the outcome is ambiguous: Kenna never became a mainstream commercial success. The experts who loved him may have been right that he had artistic merit, but wrong that he had mass-market appeal. Market research may have been detecting something real (not enough people will buy this) rather than simply failing to recognize innovation.
- The Aeron chair story requires the reader to accept that consumer aesthetic preferences are not fixed but educable over time. This is probably true. But it creates a testability problem: how do we distinguish "the product is bad and people correctly detected this, but the company dismissed the research" from "the product is good but different, and people needed time to adjust"? Gladwell offers no criterion.

**Methodological Soundness:** The Pepsi/Coke case is the chapter's most rigorous evidence. The Cheskin sensation transference research is genuinely interesting. The Kenna, Aeron, and TV show cases are plausible but anecdotal.
