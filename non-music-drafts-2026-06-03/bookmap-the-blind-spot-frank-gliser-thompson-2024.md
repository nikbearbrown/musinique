# BOOKMAP: The Blind Spot: Why Science Cannot Ignore Human Experience
**Frank, Gliser & Thompson (2024) | MIT Press**

---

## PART 1: SECTION-BY-SECTION LOGICAL MAPPING

---

### INTRODUCTION: The Fire This Time
**Core Claim:** Modern science's worldview is in crisis because it has systematically suppressed the role of direct experience in generating knowledge — the "blind spot" — and this suppression is implicated in civilization-scale existential threats, especially climate change.

**Supporting Evidence:**
- COVID-19 pandemic cited as demonstration of civilization's fragility within the natural world
- The authors name three existing responses to the crisis — scientific triumphalism, science denial, and New Age pseudo-science — and argue all three are dead ends
- The parable of temperature is introduced: the entire edifice of thermodynamics is an abstraction from the concrete bodily experience of hot and cold, which the abstraction cannot replace or fully explain

**Logical Method:** The authors establish crisis by juxtaposition: science is simultaneously our most powerful cognitive tool and the generator of conditions threatening its own civilizational substrate. The blind spot is named via the temperature parable as a concrete illustration of the general structure of the problem.

**Logical Gaps:**
- The link between "blind spot metaphysics" and the specific political failures that produced climate inaction is asserted rather than demonstrated in the Introduction. The causal arrow (blind spot → climate inaction) will need to carry substantial argumentative weight that is not yet provided.
- The temperature parable is vivid but introduces an epistemological claim (direct experience is ineliminable from scientific concepts) as if it were self-evident. The argument requires more rigorous support, which subsequent chapters partially supply.

**Methodological Soundness:** The Introduction functions as a manifesto. It establishes the book's architecture clearly and honestly. Claims are appropriately flagged as arguments-to-be-made rather than established conclusions.

### CHAPTER 1: How Did We Get Here? The Surreptitious Substitution
**Core Claim:** The blind spot is not science itself but a philosophical worldview grafted onto science, originating with Galileo and reaching maturity in classical physics. Husserl diagnosed this as the "surreptitious substitution" — mistaking the abstract mathematical constructs of physics for concrete reality — and Whitehead identified the resulting error as the "fallacy of misplaced concreteness."

**Supporting Evidence:**
- Six elements of blind spot metaphysics are enumerated: bifurcation of nature, reductionism, objectivism, physicalism, reification of mathematical entities, epiphenomenalism about experience
- Husserl's analysis of thermometry is elaborated: the development of thermodynamic temperature follows an ascending spiral of abstraction from bodily sensation, but the abstraction cannot legitimately replace the concrete sensation it was distilled from
- Whitehead's critique of the bifurcation of nature: "The poets are entirely mistaken. They should address their lyrics to themselves... Nature is a dull affair, soundless, scentless, colourless."
- Francis Bacon's workshop vision and its link to the commodification of nature is introduced

**Logical Method:** Historical-philosophical diagnosis. The authors trace the genealogy of the blind spot elements and show they form a coherent constellation — not a required feature of scientific practice but an optional metaphysics that became entrenched.

**Logical Gaps:**
- The six elements of blind spot metaphysics are not always mutually consistent. Reductionism and physicalism have had vigorous philosophical debate within them; the book treats them as a single block for rhetorical efficiency, but this papers over real distinctions.
- Bacon's colonial and gendered conception of nature's subjugation is introduced but not integrated into the core epistemological argument until the final chapter. This is a structural gap — the political economy strand and the epistemological strand are identified here but not braided.
- Husserl's phenomenological solution is gestured toward but not yet defended. The reader must accept the problem before the solution appears, which is reasonable, but the book will need to provide more than diagnosis.

**Methodological Soundness:** The historical and philosophical analysis is rigorous by the standards of philosophy of science. The identification of the surreptitious substitution and the fallacy of misplaced concreteness as distinct but related errors is analytically precise and will prove to be the most important conceptual contribution of the book.

### CHAPTER 2: Unobservable Objects — The Limits of Scientific Realism
**Core Claim:** The existence of unobservable entities (atoms, electrons) can be legitimately postulated through "inference to the most probable cause," but this inference is itself grounded in perceptual experience. Accepting that electrons are real does not require, and should not produce, demoting the perceptual world to an epiphenomenal side-show.

**Supporting Evidence:**
- Cloud chamber tracks as perceptual signs of particle existence: "if you can spray them, they are real" (Hacking/Cartwright)
- Whitehead's argument that science presupposes the concrete reality of the perceptual world: "we may not pick and choose"
- Eddington's "Two Tables" — the ordinary table and the scientific table — is cited as a paradigm case of the bifurcation of nature in action
- The distinction between laws (which refer to ideal, non-real objects) and theories (which postulate real unobservable entities) is maintained

**Logical Method:** Philosophical analysis combined with historical case studies of particle detection. The argument distinguishes between legitimate scientific realism (unobservable entities postulated through controlled inference) and illegitimate surreptitious substitution (mathematical abstractions promoted to the status of concrete reality).

**Logical Gaps:**
- The argument that "the red of the sunset is as much a part of nature as the molecules and light waves by which scientists explain it" (Whitehead) is asserted as a principle of natural philosophy but requires more defense. Many physicists would simply deny this on the grounds that color is a brain construction, not a feature of electromagnetic radiation. The book will need to address this directly rather than treating Whitehead's assertion as conclusive.
- The concept of "indicative sign consciousness" (Husserl) is introduced to explain how physicists "see" particle tracks as particle tracks. This is a rich concept but is not developed enough to do the philosophical work it's being asked to do here.

**Methodological Soundness:** Adequate. The chapter carefully limits its claims — it is not arguing against scientific realism, only against a specific form of reductionism that degrades the perceptual world.

### CHAPTERS 3–5: The Ascending Spiral of Abstraction — Classical Physics and the Birth of the Blind Spot
**Core Claim:** Classical physics from Galileo to Hamilton represents a genuine achievement — an ascending spiral of mathematical abstraction that produced extraordinary predictive power — but this very success made it easy to mistake the map for the territory. Newton's absolute time, the Lagrangian, and Hamiltonian phase space are instruments for knowing how things behave; they are not descriptions of how things are in themselves.

**Supporting Evidence:**
- Detailed historical reconstruction: Mertonians' development of kinematics, Galileo's workshop method, Descartes' analytic geometry, Newton's laws, Lagrange's generalized coordinates, Hamilton's phase space
- Newton's theological grounding: absolute space and time as attributes of God, the "view from nowhere" that became the implicit epistemological ideal of classical physics
- The principle of least action as an example of abstraction that nonetheless has its roots in embodied experience of economy of effort
- Boltzmann's H-theorem and the arrow of time: time's direction emerges from coarse-graining, which itself depends on the finite precision of real observers. Time's arrow is therefore irreducibly experiential

**Logical Method:** Intellectual history combined with philosophical interpretation. The authors show that the very success of classical physics created conditions for its misinterpretation. The ascending spiral of abstraction is reconstructed not to dismiss it but to show that each step in the spiral depended on and retained connections to embodied experience that the blind spot worldview later severed.

**Logical Gaps:**
- The treatment of the arrow of time via Boltzmann is the most important and most contested section of these chapters. The argument that coarse-graining (which depends on the finite precision of actual observers) is responsible for time's arrow is one plausible view but is not the consensus position. The authors appropriately acknowledge this is contested but do not engage the strongest competing accounts (e.g., the past hypothesis, gravitational entropy).
- The "Loschmidt paradox" resolution offered — that the apparent reversibility of microscopic mechanics does not undermine temporal asymmetry because observers cannot measure with infinite precision — is stated but not defended against the objection that the real problem is the low-entropy initial condition of the universe, not measurement limitations.

**Methodological Soundness:** Strong as intellectual history. Somewhat weaker as philosophy of physics, where the authors make tendentious moves that specialists would contest without sufficient acknowledgment.

### CHAPTERS 6–7: Matter — Quantum Mechanics and the Collapse of Objectivism
**Core Claim:** Quantum mechanics is the most direct empirical challenge to blind spot metaphysics. The measurement problem and the reality of quantum superpositions cannot be resolved within an objectivist framework. Each major interpretation of quantum mechanics — GRW, Bohmian mechanics, Many Worlds, Copenhagen, Relational Quantum Mechanics, QBism — pays a price, and the price of psi-ontological interpretations is higher than their advocates acknowledge.

**Supporting Evidence:**
- The Stern-Gerlach experiment: electron spin is quantized, not continuous — the first empirical result that could not be accommodated within classical objectivism
- Superposition (equation 1: |P⟩ = A|white⟩ + B|black⟩) defined precisely as a state without definite properties prior to measurement
- Bell's theorem and its experimental confirmations: local hidden variable theories are ruled out; entanglement is real
- QBism (Quantum Bayesianism) is endorsed as the interpretation most compatible with a post-blind spot worldview: it restores the agent to the center of physics without collapsing into solipsism
- GRW and Many Worlds are subjected to ISE-standard critique: GRW commits surreptitious substitution by promoting the wave function to ontological status without empirical warrant; MWI creates an "explosion of ghosts" to save the wave function's ontological primacy

**Logical Method:** Systematic review of interpretations evaluated against the criteria of the blind spot analysis. The authors are explicitly not neutral — they favor psi-epistemological interpretations — and they state this clearly.

**Logical Gaps:**
- The endorsement of QBism is stated more strongly than the evidence supports. QBism has been criticized (including by physicists sympathetic to its aims) for being insufficiently clear about what the "external world" is that agents interact with, and what ontological commitments it requires. The authors acknowledge this but dismiss it too quickly.
- Relational Quantum Mechanics (Rovelli) is treated as a promising middle way, but its apparent consistency with a non-blind-spot worldview is asserted rather than demonstrated. RQM has its own unresolved problems regarding the reality of relative facts.
- The argument that psi-ontological interpretations all commit surreptitious substitution is correct as a general structural critique but may not apply with equal force to all versions. Bohmian mechanics, for instance, may be defensible as instrumentalism about the pilot wave combined with realism about particle trajectories.

**Methodological Soundness:** Good for the purpose of the book's argument. The authors are transparent about their interpretive preferences and the philosophical stakes. The quantum mechanics chapters are the most technically demanding in the book and also among the most honest about contested terrain.

### CHAPTER 8: Life — Biological Autonomy and the Limits of Reductionism
**Core Claim:** Life is not reducible to molecular machinery. The concept of "biological autonomy" — organizational closure, self-production, agency — captures what organisms are in a way that reductionist molecular biology cannot. Life is "based on physics, but beyond physics." The phase space of biological evolution cannot be prestated.

**Supporting Evidence:**
- Autopoiesis (Maturana and Varela): a cell is a self-producing system that generates and maintains its own boundary, which in turn enables the processes that produce the boundary — closure of constraints
- Robert Rosen's concept of "closure to efficient causation": every catalyst in an organism's metabolism is produced by that metabolism
- Kauffman's "work constraint cycles": organisms are self-constraining thermodynamic engines
- Bacterial chemotaxis as the most basic demonstration of agency: sucrose means food for a bacterium only given its status as an autonomous agent that must maintain viability
- Nicholson's review showing that cells are not static machines but dynamic, stochastic, probabilistic systems with no two cells behaving identically
- Longo, Montevil, and Kauffman: biological evolution has no prestatable phase space — evolutionary novelty is not entailed by any set of equations

**Logical Method:** Philosophical biology combined with empirical case studies. The argument proceeds from the organizational concepts (autopoiesis, closure) to their thermodynamic grounding (work constraint cycles) to the evolutionary implication (unprestatable phase spaces).

**Logical Gaps:**
- The claim that life "cannot be recognized" by a purely physicochemical AI system (Jonas) is philosophically suggestive but not proven. A sufficiently sophisticated AI system trained on biological data might identify living systems by pattern recognition without having the concept of "life" in the sense the authors mean. The argument here conflates recognition with understanding.
- The distinction between organizational concepts and evolutionary concepts is drawn correctly, but the authors do not fully address how the two must be combined: organizational closure is necessary but not sufficient for life as we know it; evolution is what explains the diversity. The relationship between these two frameworks needs more development.
- Molecular animism (Nicholson) is a real phenomenon in biological discourse, but the critique of it must be careful not to eliminate causal talk at the molecular level entirely. Some molecular entities genuinely do have privileged causal roles; the question is whether this constitutes a violation of the organizational whole, not whether molecular causation is real.

**Methodological Soundness:** Strong. This is the chapter where the authors' positive program for science beyond the blind spot is most clearly sketched. The concept of closure of constraints and the unprestatable phase space argument are the most rigorous contributions of this section.

### CHAPTER 9: Cognition — AI and the Computational Blind Spot
**Core Claim:** AI systems, particularly machine learning, do not realize relevance — the capacity to determine what matters in an open-ended situation — because relevance is not a computational property. The frame problem reveals an intractable limitation of any computational approach. AlphaGo does not "master Go" — it detects statistical patterns in Go-shaped data without knowing it is playing a game. The computational blind spot is a case of surreptitious substitution: computational models substituted for the lived world they represent.

**Supporting Evidence:**
- Dennett's frame problem story: a robot trying to retrieve a battery fails to realize the bomb on the same wagon will also move. Any attempt to computationally specify relevance reintroduces the problem of determining which information is relevant
- AlphaGo's dependence on human knowledge (Monte Carlo tree search, the rules of Go, human expert training data) is documented; the claim that it "mastered Go without human knowledge" is shown to be factually inaccurate
- Marcus and Davis on AlphaGo: the system cannot transfer knowledge between domains, cannot understand that it is playing a game, has no access to the semantics of its data structures
- Self-driving cars as illustration: functions only in geofenced areas with extensive infrastructure — we are remaking the world to fit the limitations of the devices
- Crawford on AI's material and social dependencies: AI requires mineral extraction, cheap labor, and encoded human biases — it does not transcend nature or culture

**Logical Method:** Conceptual analysis of AI limitations combined with documented empirical cases. The argument proceeds from the frame problem (theoretical), through AlphaGo (empirical), to self-driving cars (design choice), to conclude that the computational blind spot generates both bad science and socially harmful technology.

**Logical Gaps:**
- The argument against computational approaches to cognition is strong for current AI systems but may prove too much. The claim that relevance realization "cannot be reduced to heuristic rules and symbolic representations" is plausible for first-wave AI but is being generalized to all computational approaches. Future architectures may address some of these limitations without resolving the deeper philosophical issue.
- The authors endorse inactive cognitive science (enactivism) as a move beyond the computational blind spot but do not specify what a non-computational account of relevance realization would actually look like mechanistically. "Cognition is sense-making in precarious conditions" is a framing, not a mechanism.
- Crawford's political economy critique of AI is valid and important but slightly off-center from the epistemological argument. The two strands (AI's failure to realize relevance, AI's dependence on material extraction and encoded bias) are related but require separate analysis that the chapter does not fully provide.

**Methodological Soundness:** Strong on the frame problem and AlphaGo cases. The general claim that large language models "have no conceptual understanding of the outside world" is defensible but somewhat underspecified — what would "conceptual understanding" look like in a non-biological system, and how would we test for it?

### CHAPTERS 10–11: Consciousness — The Primacy Argument and Its Implications
**Core Claim:** Consciousness has three forms of primacy: existential (it is lived, not had), cognitive (it is the departure point and touchstone for all scientific knowledge), and transcendental (it is the condition of possibility for the appearance of any object). The hard problem of consciousness is an artifact of the blind spot — it arises specifically from bifurcating nature and then trying to reintroduce consciousness as an explanandum within the very framework that excluded it.

**Supporting Evidence:**
- The finger-pointing exercise (Harding): attending to visual awareness reveals that awareness is not an object but the horizon within which objects appear
- The primacy of consciousness argument: no investigation of the brain, however detailed, can step outside consciousness to measure it against something else — "we are in the hard problem of consciousness"
- The primacy of embodiment argument: consciousness does not reveal its own sources; everything we think, including thoughts about pure awareness, depends on a social, linguistic, historical background that predates and transcends us
- The strange loop: consciousness subsumes the world; embodiment subsumes consciousness; the two infold each other without either being reducible to the other
- IIT (Integrated Information Theory) is subjected to ISE-standard critique: the axioms are not self-evident (four of five are contested or underspecified), the identification of consciousness with integrated information is a surreptitious substitution, and information is itself observer-dependent and therefore cannot ground consciousness
- Predictive processing (Seth, Hohwy) is shown to be self-undermining: if perception is the brain's best guess about hidden causes, this applies equally to our perception of the brain itself, making the physical brain a hypothesis inside the model it is supposed to generate

**Logical Method:** Phenomenological analysis combined with systematic critique of existing theories. The authors use the blind spot framework (surreptitious substitution, fallacy of misplaced concreteness, amnesia of experience) as a diagnostic tool applied to each consciousness theory.

**Logical Gaps:**
- The finger-pointing exercise and the associated Husserlian arguments establish that consciousness cannot be objectified without self-contradiction. But this does not yet establish what consciousness *is* positively — only what it is not. The book is clearest in its negative program (ruling out physicalism, illusionism, naïve IIT) and underdeveloped in its positive program.
- The "primacy of consciousness" argument, as presented, risks idealism — the position that the world exists only for or in consciousness. The authors explicitly reject idealism, but their argument is less careful here than elsewhere. The claim that "the life world contains the universe" (Merlot-Ponty) needs more defense against the objection that it is simply wrong in the ordinary sense: the universe preceded life.
- The endorsement of neurophenomenology (Varela) as a research program is appropriately tentative, but the book should be clearer about what specific empirical questions neurophenomenology is supposed to answer that standard neuroscience cannot.
- Illusionism is too quickly dismissed. The most sophisticated illusionist positions (Frankish) do not claim that experience doesn't exist; they deny that qualia are intrinsic phenomenal properties. Whether this constitutes the "great silliness" the authors attribute to it requires more argumentative engagement.

**Methodological Soundness:** The negative critique of IIT and predictive processing is the strongest work in the book's positive-program sections. The self-undermining character of the predictive processing theory (the physical brain it invokes is itself a hypothesis inside the model) is a genuinely important philosophical point that has not been made this clearly elsewhere. The primacy arguments are compelling but require more defense against idealist misreading.

### CHAPTERS 12–13: Earth — The Anthropocene and the Political Economy of the Blind Spot
**Core Claim:** The Anthropocene is a direct consequence of blind spot metaphysics writ large: treating the biosphere as an inert material resource, adopting a reductionist neoclassical economics that cannot assign value to externalities, and building a political economy on mathematical abstractions (rational actors, efficient markets) that surreptitiously substituted for the lived reality of human beings and ecosystems.

**Supporting Evidence:**
- Vernadsky's biosphere concept: life is a planetary force, not a thin film of incidental organic material
- Lovelock and Margulis's Gaia theory: the biosphere maintains conditions favorable to life through recursive feedback loops between living and non-living Earth systems
- Earth System Science (ESS) as transdisciplinary alternative to disciplinary reductionism: the Brethren diagram as first representation of the planet as a coupled system
- Keeling curve and Revelle-Zeuss (1957): fossil fuel CO2 was known to be accumulating in the atmosphere by 1957; President Johnson warned Congress in 1965
- Neoclassical economics as blind spot economics: rational actors and efficient markets are idealized abstractions that cannot account for the biosphere; Nordhaus's Nobel-winning work accepting 3.5°C warming as "optimal" as a paradigm case of the fallacy of misplaced concreteness
- Complex systems theory as a glimpse beyond the blind spot: co-evolution, network architecture, non-ergodic dynamics, unprestatable phase spaces — all challenge the reductionist ontology of classical physics

**Logical Method:** Historical and political-economic analysis combined with Earth System Science. The argument is the most synthetic in the book — braiding the epistemological thesis (blind spot) with the political economy (industrial capitalism) with the scientific evidence (climate change) with the promising alternatives (ESS, complex systems theory).

**Logical Gaps:**
- The claim that industrial capitalism embodies blind spot metaphysics is the book's most ambitious and most underspecified argument. The causal pathway (blind spot → Bacon's workshop vision → capitalist political economy → Anthropocene) is plausible as intellectual history but does not demonstrate that alternative metaphysics would have produced different economic outcomes. The collapse of Soviet-style socialist economies, which also devastated ecosystems, suggests that the causal story is more complex.
- The critique of neoclassical economics is valid but targets a straw man version of the field. Contemporary environmental economics, ecological economics, and heterodox approaches within mainstream economics have been grappling with exactly these externalities problems for decades. The authors acknowledge this in one sentence but do not engage it seriously.
- Complex systems theory is presented as a "glimpse beyond the blind spot," but the authors appropriately hedge: it can also be deployed in harmful ways (social media's effects on democratic norms are cited). This is honest but leaves the positive program underdeveloped.

**Methodological Soundness:** The Earth System Science material is well-grounded. The intellectual history of neoclassical economics and its blind spot affinities is plausible but would benefit from engagement with the substantial existing literature on this topic. The Anthropocene chapter is more manifesto than analysis, which is appropriate for a book's closing movement but should be acknowledged as such.

### AFTERWORD: Best Practices for a Science Beyond the Blind Spot
**Core Claim:** Moving beyond the blind spot requires two best practices: (1) historical and philosophical awareness of how present theory-building carries submerged philosophical commitments, and (2) a better public narrative of science as a practice that takes us *deeper into* the mystery of being human, not as a means of transcending it.

**Supporting Evidence:**
- Four pathologies to avoid: surreptitious substitution, fallacy of misplaced concreteness, reification of structural invariance, amnesia of experience
- Science as self-correcting narrative: the map is not the territory, but the map is real and the project of making better maps is worthwhile
- The science-denialism problem: presenting the blind spot as "what science says" likely exacerbates science denial by feeding the "scientist as cold and not like us" stereotype

**Logical Gaps:**
- The positive program for a post-blind spot science is gestured toward but not specified. The authors explicitly decline to provide a "comprehensive scientific or philosophical perspective to replace" the blind spot, calling the task one for the 21st-century scientific community. This is intellectually honest but means the book ends more as a diagnosis than a prescription.

**Methodological Soundness:** The afterword is appropriately modest about what a philosophical critique can accomplish and appropriately ambitious about the stakes.

## BRIDGE: The Argumentative Architecture

**The book's spine is a nested loop of arguments:**

The *epistemological* argument: Direct experience is the ineliminable source and touchstone of scientific knowledge. Every ascending spiral of abstraction in physics, biology, and cognitive science begins in and remains dependent on embodied experience. To forget this — the amnesia of experience — is the blind spot's defining pathology.

The *ontological* argument: Because experience cannot be excluded from any adequate account of nature, any metaphysics that excludes it by construction (bifurcation of nature, physicalism, epiphenomenalism) will generate insoluble problems. The hard problem of consciousness, the measurement problem in quantum mechanics, and the inability to pre-state the phase space of biological evolution are not puzzles to be solved within blind spot metaphysics but artifacts of it.

The *political* argument: The blind spot metaphysics was not confined to philosophy departments. It became entrenched in the political economy of industrial capitalism, producing a worldview that treats the biosphere as an inert resource and cannot recognize the value of what cannot be quantified. The Anthropocene is the material consequence.

**Three master tensions run through every chapter:**

*Tension 1: The abstraction problem.* The authors need mathematical abstraction to be both necessary and insufficient. Necessary because without it science cannot achieve the structural invariance that makes knowledge cumulative and public. Insufficient because abstraction is always a residue of experience that cannot replace the experience from which it was distilled. The book handles this tension well in some places (the temperature parable, the H-theorem) and incompletely in others (the consciousness chapters).

*Tension 2: The realism problem.* The authors want to reject both naive scientific realism (the universe is made of the mathematical objects of physics) and anti-realism (scientific theories are merely instruments for prediction). Their alternative — science as a "self-correcting narrative" built from the life world — is philosophically sophisticated but underdeveloped. At several points the book slips toward a position that sounds like empirical idealism (the life world contains the universe) without fully defending it.

*Tension 3: The positive program problem.* The book is far stronger in its diagnosis than its prescription. The neurophenomenology of Varela, the enactivism of Di Paolo and Thompson, QBism, complex systems theory, and Earth System Science are all gestured toward as post-blind spot alternatives, but none is developed enough to constitute a positive replacement. This is appropriate epistemic humility or a significant gap in the argument, depending on what one was expecting.

**The book's most proven claims:**
- Direct experience is ineliminable from scientific knowledge — the temperature parable and its generalizations demonstrate this with genuine rigor
- The hard problem of consciousness is an artifact of the bifurcation of nature, not a freestanding metaphysical problem requiring a freestanding solution
- Life has no prestatable phase space — Kauffman, Longo, Montevil's argument is the most technically rigorous positive contribution
- Current AI systems do not realize relevance — the frame problem and AlphaGo cases are well-documented

**The book's most significant unproven claims:**
- That blind spot metaphysics causally produced industrial capitalism and thus the Anthropocene, rather than the relationship being one of mutual reinforcement or coincidence
- That QBism provides an adequate metaphysical framework for physics — the authors endorse it but acknowledge it remains contested
- That complex systems theory constitutes a post-blind spot science — it is offered as a "glimpse" without specification of what post-blind spot science would look like in practice

## PART 2: LITERARY REVIEW ESSAY

---

# Maps That Cannot Reach the Territory

There is a parable at the center of this book that its authors deploy with such clarity that it threatens to do all the argumentative work by itself. Temperature: we know it as a number on a thermometer, a measure of the average kinetic energy of particles, a scientific invariant of remarkable precision. And yet the concept of temperature was distilled over centuries from the bodily experience of hot and cold, and if you subtract that experience entirely — if you remove the scientist who feels warmth, who builds the furnace, who notices that water changes when heated — there is no way to arrive at thermodynamics from the equations alone. The abstraction always presupposes the concrete from which it was extracted. You can climb the ladder of abstraction, but you cannot kick it away.

Adam Frank, Marcelo Gliser, and Evan Thompson's *The Blind Spot: Why Science Cannot Ignore Human Experience* (MIT Press, 2024) is constructed around this parable and its systematic implications. The book's thesis is that modern science has developed a philosophical worldview — not identical with science itself, but attached to it — in which direct experience is systematically suppressed in favor of mathematical abstraction, and that this suppression produces not only insoluble philosophical puzzles but material civilizational catastrophe. The Anthropocene, they argue, is what happens when a civilization takes its epistemological pathologies seriously.

---

The book names its central pathology with Husserl's term: the "surreptitious substitution." The ideal gas, the frictionless plane, the point particle — these are not real things. They are fictional instruments, conceptual tools that allow physicists to write tractable equations. When Galileo's law of free fall specifies that all bodies fall with equal acceleration in the *absence* of air resistance, it describes an idealized case that does not occur in nature. The law is not false — it is extraordinarily useful — but it refers to objects that do not exist. The surreptitious substitution occurs when we forget this, when we promote the fictional instrument to concrete reality and demote the concrete world — the falling leaf, the actual thermometer, the lived experience of a summer afternoon — to secondary status, illusion, epiphenomenon.

The authors trace this substitution across physics, biology, cognitive science, and neuroscience with varying degrees of rigor and persuasiveness. Their strongest material is in the domains where the argument is most technically grounded. The treatment of thermodynamics and time's arrow is genuinely impressive: the argument that Boltzmann's arrow of time depends on coarse-graining, and that coarse-graining reflects the finite precision of real observers rather than a feature of the microscopic world itself, is a sophisticated philosophical point that carries the epistemic weight assigned to it. Similarly, the biological argument about "closure of constraints" — the concept that organisms are systems in which constraints on processes are themselves produced by those processes — constitutes genuine theoretical biology rather than philosophy of biology, and Kauffman's demonstration that biological evolution has no prestatable phase space is the most technically rigorous contribution to the book's positive program.

But the most philosophically important argument in *The Blind Spot* is also the one most likely to be dismissed, because it concerns consciousness. The authors argue that the hard problem of consciousness — the problem of explaining how a physical system generates subjective experience — is not a freestanding metaphysical puzzle but an artifact of the bifurcation of nature. When Descartes divided the world into extended matter (res extensa) and thinking mind (res cogitans), and when Galileo assigned color, taste, and warmth to the subjective side of that divide, the result was a picture of nature from which consciousness had been excluded by construction. The hard problem is the price of that exclusion: you cannot reintroduce as a problem-to-be-solved the very thing you deliberately removed from your ontology.

This is a genuinely important point. But it is not enough.

---

The difficulty is that demonstrating that the hard problem is an artifact of blind spot metaphysics does not dissolve it. It reassigns it. If we stop bifurcating nature — if we follow Whitehead and insist that "the red of the sunset is as much a part of nature as the molecules and light waves by which scientists explain it" — we have eliminated the problem of how physical processes generate subjective experience. But we have inherited a different and equally difficult problem: how to give a unified account of a nature that includes both electromagnetic waves and the experience of redness without collapsing into either physicalism (redness is just electromagnetic waves) or idealism (electromagnetic waves are ultimately mental).

The authors are aware of this problem. They invoke the concept of the "strange loop" — consciousness subsumes the world; embodiment subsumes consciousness; neither can be reduced to the other — to name the structure of the difficulty. This is philosophically honest and philosophically insufficient. A strange loop is a description of the problem, not a solution to it. Neurophenomenology (Varela's research program) is offered as a methodological response — braiding first-person accounts of experience with third-person neuroscience in an iterative loop — but the book does not specify what empirical questions this approach answers that standard cognitive neuroscience cannot.

The authors' critique of the competing theories is consistently stronger than their positive program. The attack on Integrated Information Theory (IIT) is the most technically rigorous section of the consciousness chapters: the demonstration that IIT's five axioms are either contestable or underspecified, and that the identification of consciousness with integrated information commits surreptitious substitution by promoting an abstract structural residue to the status of concrete experience, is analytically precise and philosophically damaging. The critique of predictive processing is similarly strong: if perception is the brain's best guess about the hidden causes of its sensory inputs, then this must apply to our perception of the brain itself, making the physical brain a hypothesis inside the model it was supposed to generate. This is a genuinely new objection — the self-undermining character of predictive processing as a theory of consciousness — and it deserves more attention than it has received.

---

The book's most ambitious and most problematic argument concerns the Anthropocene. The authors want to show not just that blind spot metaphysics is philosophically mistaken but that it produced industrial capitalism, which produced the climate crisis. This is a causal claim of enormous scope, and the book does not establish it with the rigor it applies elsewhere.

The intellectual history is suggestive. Francis Bacon's vision of a scientific workshop that "vexes" nature to extract her secrets, his use of female imagery to describe nature's subjugation, the emergence of quantitative political economy in the 18th century alongside the triumph of Newtonian physics — these are not coincidental. But the book's own analysis of the entanglement of science and political economy cites scholarship that is considerably more careful about the causal arrows than the authors themselves are. "Economies and sciences go together like body and mind" (Cook) describes a relationship of mutual constitution, not one of causal derivation.

The specific claim about neoclassical economics — that its failure to account for environmental externalities reflects the reification of mathematical abstractions over the lived reality of ecosystems — is more defensible. The treatment of Nordhaus's work as a paradigm case of the fallacy of misplaced concreteness (accepting 3.5°C of warming as "optimal" because the mathematical model cannot represent civilizational disruption) is genuinely illuminating. But the book's treatment of economics as a monolith misses the substantial tradition of heterodox environmental economics that has been grappling with exactly these problems for decades. The blind spot critique applies with full force to mainstream neoclassical models; it applies much less clearly to the field as it has actually developed.

What remains unaddressed is the most important question the political-economy argument raises: if blind spot metaphysics is responsible for industrial capitalism's inability to respond to climate change, does adopting a different metaphysics actually change anything? The Soviet Union, which operated under a very different official philosophy of nature, devastated the Aral Sea. The causal story between epistemology and ecology is not as direct as the book suggests.

---

The book's most honest moment comes in the afterword, where the authors acknowledge they have not provided a comprehensive alternative to the blind spot worldview: "Such an alternative has yet to be fully worked out. That effort we would argue is, or should be, part of the principal project for 21st-century science and philosophy of science."

This is the right epistemic position to take after a book that is primarily diagnostic. The diagnosis is largely correct, and the four pathologies — surreptitious substitution, fallacy of misplaced concreteness, reification of structural invariance, amnesia of experience — constitute a useful analytical toolkit that can be applied across domains with real critical force. The book is most convincing when it shows what the blind spot produces: the hard problem of consciousness, the intractability of the measurement problem in quantum mechanics, the frame problem in AI, the impossibility of pre-stating the biological phase space. Less convincing but still important is the argument that these theoretical problems and the civilizational crisis of the Anthropocene share a common philosophical root.

What *The Blind Spot* cannot yet provide, and is honest enough to acknowledge it cannot provide, is a map of the territory it is asking us to enter. It shows us what it would mean to take experience seriously as the irreducible source and touchstone of scientific knowledge. It shows us what we lose when we forget this. What it does not show us is what a science that remembers it would look like — not in the negative sense of avoiding the four pathologies, but in the positive sense of building new maps that do not kick away the ladder.

That project is larger than any book. The parable of temperature shows us the problem with precision. The ascending spiral of abstraction from hot and cold to thermodynamic temperature to statistical mechanics is one of the great intellectual achievements of human civilization. The question is not whether to ascend the spiral but whether, when we reach the top, we can still see all the way down.
