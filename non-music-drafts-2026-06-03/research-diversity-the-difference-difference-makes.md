### CHAPTER 2: Diversity — The Difference Difference Makes

**Core Claim:** Cognitive diversity — not demographic diversity per se, but different models, perspectives, and heuristics — is essential to group intelligence. Homogeneous groups, even of smart people, are less effective than diverse groups of mixed ability.

**Supporting Evidence:**
- Scott Page's computer-simulation experiments: groups of smart-plus-not-smart agents consistently outperformed groups of exclusively smart agents on sophisticated problem-solving tasks
- The early US auto industry (1900–1920): diversity of approaches (steam, electric, gasoline) allowed market selection of the best technology — a "waggle dance" of scouting
- The bee colony foraging model: decentralized scouts with diverse starting points ensure coverage of the solution space
- Irving Janis's groupthink analysis: the Bay of Pigs was planned by a homogeneous group that never consulted outside skeptics, ignored the CIA's Cuban desk, and convinced itself of a plan predicated on obvious errors about Cuba's size and population
- James March's organizational theory: new, less expert recruits improve group outcomes because their knowledge is non-redundant, not because they're smarter

**Logical Method:** Computer simulation, historical case study, organizational theory, and animal behavior analogy.

**Logical Gaps:**
- Page's simulations use artificial agents that can be designed to have genuinely diverse tools. In real organizations, "diverse" people may share more cognitive overlap than the model assumes.
- The auto industry example collapses *diversity in development* (many companies trying different things) with *diversity in judgment* (the wisdom of crowds selecting among options). These are distinct processes. The crowd does not always correctly select the best technology — as the chapter's own acknowledgment that "the best technology doesn't necessarily win" notes.
- March's argument about "influx of the naive and ignorant" being beneficial is stated as a discovered truth, but it is a theoretical result from formal models that may not transfer reliably to real organizations.
- Groupthink is presented as the failure mode of homogeneous groups — but groupthink has also been critiqued by organizational psychologists as overdiagnosed and imprecisely defined. Surowiecki uses it as an established fact.

**Methodological Soundness:** The theoretical case is strong; the empirical case draws heavily on case studies selected for illustration rather than systematic comparison.
