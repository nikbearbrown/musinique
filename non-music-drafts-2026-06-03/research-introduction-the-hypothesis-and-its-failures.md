### CHAPTER 1: Introduction — The Hypothesis and Its Failures
**Core Claim:** The author's prior hypothesis—that Amazonian shamans access biomolecular information in their visions—failed to gain scientific traction, prompting a redirected inquiry into whether intelligence exists throughout the natural world.

**Supporting Evidence:**
- Narby's *The Cosmic Serpent* (1995) proposed shamans reach the molecular level through ayahuasca visions; the hypothesis was not adopted by the scientific establishment
- Three molecular biologists tested the hypothesis in the Peruvian Amazon; one (Dr. Pia Malno) concluded that shamans and scientists "get their knowledge" through parallel but distinct methods
- Slime molds solve mazes; bees handle abstract concepts—recent science cited in passing as prompting the new investigation

**Logical Method:** Failed hypothesis → methodological pivot → new inquiry framing. The chapter establishes the author's intellectual biography as the warrant for pursuing indigenous-scientific synthesis.

**Logical Gaps:**
- The pivot from "shamans access DNA information" to "nature has intelligence" is a significant categorical shift. These are not equivalent claims. The first is about the mechanism of human cognition under altered states; the second is about the properties of organisms. The author treats them as continuous without justifying the connection.
- Dr. Pia Malno's conclusion that shaman knowledge and scientific knowledge share "the same origin" is reported as empirical validation, but it is a philosophical claim about epistemological parallels, not a demonstrated equivalence of content.
- The claim that *The Cosmic Serpent* "has not gained the attention I had hoped for from scientists" is the author's own assessment. No citation of critical scientific engagement or refutation is provided. Absence of attention is not equivalent to refutation, but also not to validation.

**Methodological Soundness:** The chapter functions as intellectual autobiography rather than argument. As framing, it is honest—the author situates himself as an anthropologist, not a scientist, and acknowledges that testing hypotheses is the domain of others. The pivot to a new inquiry is justified by genuine curiosity, not proven by prior evidence.
