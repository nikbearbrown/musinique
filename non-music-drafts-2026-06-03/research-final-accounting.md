### SECTION 9: Final Accounting (Chapters 28, 30–34)
**Core Claim:** Despite five years of documented fraud, confirmed by the SEC's own findings, Allied's management remained in place, was not prosecuted, and successfully extracted hundreds of millions of dollars in insider compensation while the retail shareholder base — the people the SEC exists to protect — bore the consequences.

**Supporting Evidence:**
- Insider option tender (May 2006, executed July 2007): 16.7 million options tendered at $31.75/share; Walton received $14.5M cash + $14.5M stock; executed the same afternoon as the SEC's cease-and-desist order
- SEC order (June 2007): found Allied violated Exchange Act record-keeping and internal controls provisions; confirmed valuations were unsupported; confirmed pattern of undocumented metric changes. Penalty: cease-and-desist. No fines, no personnel actions.
- Harrington guilty plea (October 2007): admitted to 96 fraudulent loans; did not cooperate with investigation
- BLX write-down ($74M, February 2007): management simultaneously described BLX as "a very healthy business with very high lending standards"
- SEC order vs. Van Wagener: Van Wagener (undervalued portfolio) received $800K fine + 7-year industry bar; Allied (overvalued portfolio, defrauded investors, facilitated taxpayer fraud) received a strongly worded letter
- Allied return 2002–2007: 5.9% per year vs. Russell 2000 benchmark return of approximately 8.1%; lag of 2.2% annually; Greenlight returned 17.7% per year in same period

**Logical Method:** Comparative analysis (Allied vs. Van Wagener), documented compensation extraction, regulatory outcome documentation.

**Logical Gaps:**
- The "moral hazard" argument in Chapter 34 — that protecting shareholders from consequences of investing in fraudulent companies removes their incentive to demand honesty — is analytically correct but is presented as general theory without proof that this mechanism was at work in the Allied investor base. The book shows Allied shareholders were largely retail and inattentive, not that they made rational calculations about fraud risk.

**Methodological Soundness:** The final section is the book's most honest in acknowledging the limits of what the author achieved. Allied continued to exist; management was not prosecuted; the retail shareholder base suffered through stock underperformance, not collapse. This is a partial victory at best, and Einhorn doesn't pretend otherwise.
