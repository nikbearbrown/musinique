# BOOKMAP: Intelligence in Nature: An Inquiry into Knowledge
**Narby, Jeremy (2005) | Tarcher/Penguin**

---

## PART 1: SECTION-BY-SECTION LOGICAL MAPPING

---

### CHAPTER 1: Introduction — The Hypothesis and Its Failures
**Core Claim:** The author's prior hypothesis—that Amazonian shamans access biomolecular information in their visions—failed to gain scientific traction, prompting a redirected inquiry into whether intelligence exists throughout the natural world.

**Supporting Evidence:**
- Narby's *The Cosmic Serpent* (1995) proposed shamans reach the molecular level through ayahuasca visions; the hypothesis was not adopted by the scientific establishment
- Three molecular biologists tested the hypothesis in the Peruvian Amazon; one (Dr. Pia Malno) concluded that shamans and scientists "get their knowledge" through parallel but distinct methods
- Slime molds solve mazes; bees handle abstract concepts—recent science cited in passing as prompting the new investigation

**Logical Method:** Failed hypothesis → methodological pivot → new inquiry framing. The chapter establishes the author's intellectual biography as the warrant for pursuing indigenous-scientific synthesis.

**Logical Gaps:**
- The pivot from "shamans access DNA information" to "nature has intelligence" is a significant categorical shift. These are not equivalent claims. The first is about the mechanism of human cognition under altered states; the second is about the properties of organisms. The author treats them as continuous without justifying the connection.
- Dr. Pia Malno's conclusion that shaman knowledge and scientific knowledge share "the same origin" is reported as empirical validation, but it is a philosophical claim about epistemological parallels, not a demonstrated equivalence of content.
- The claim that *The Cosmic Serpent* "has not gained the attention I had hoped for from scientists" is the author's own assessment. No citation of critical scientific engagement or refutation is provided. Absence of attention is not equivalent to refutation, but also not to validation.

**Methodological Soundness:** The chapter functions as intellectual autobiography rather than argument. As framing, it is honest—the author situates himself as an anthropologist, not a scientist, and acknowledges that testing hypotheses is the domain of others. The pivot to a new inquiry is justified by genuine curiosity, not proven by prior evidence.

### CHAPTER 2: Brainy Birds — Macaws at the Clay Lick
**Core Claim:** Macaws and other birds exhibit behaviors—clay selection, deception, species recognition of individuals—that are most parsimoniously explained by attributing intelligence to them rather than pure instinct.

**Supporting Evidence:**
- Charlie Munn (ornithologist, Manu Biosphere Reserve) documented that macaws select clay with significantly higher toxin-binding capacity (kaolinite-rich) over adjacent bands, allowing them to eat seeds toxic to other species
- Munn's published finding in *Nature*, "Birds that Cry Wolf": sentinel birds give false alarm calls to steal food flushed by other birds—interpreted as intentional deception
- Munn's unpublished claim: birds in the Manu distinguish Matsiganka who work with scientists from hunters

**Logical Method:** Field observation → behavioral pattern → inference of intentionality. The Matsiganka guide Toyeri provides indigenous contextual framing ("they are all friends... it's their breakfast") that parallels scientific observations.

**Logical Gaps:**
- The deception finding is the chapter's strongest evidence for bird intelligence. Munn's observation that "deception usually requires intelligence" is reasonable as a heuristic, but the causal mechanism—whether deception requires modeling another's beliefs (theory of mind) or can be explained by simpler reinforcement learning—is not resolved.
- The unpublished claim about birds distinguishing hunters from scientists is introduced as fact, attributed to a single scientist's field impression without peer review. The author acknowledges it is unpublished; including it as evidence nonetheless inflates the evidentiary base.
- The Matsiganka guide's interpretation ("they are all friends") is presented alongside scientific observation, implying convergence. But convergence of metaphor is not convergence of evidence. The birds might be behaving socially for entirely different reasons than "friendship."

**Methodological Soundness:** The macaw clay-lick findings rest on Munn's peer-reviewed research and are defensible. The chapter's broader claim—that birds "behave with intelligence"—is supported for specific behaviors but overextended to serve the book's thesis.

### CHAPTER 3: Ayahuasca and the Agnostic — Epistemological Framing
**Core Claim:** Western science's difficulty accepting intelligence in nature stems from its prohibition on teleology (goal-directedness in non-humans), but this prohibition is itself a metaphysical position, not a scientific demonstration.

**Supporting Evidence:**
- Flores (Ashaninka shaman): plants' spirits "wander from one place to another to heal people, because plants care a lot about humanity"—reported as indigenous perspective, not claimed as scientific fact
- Guillermo Aurevalo Valera (Shipibo shaman): "the spirits of animals are obliged to stop existing in this place" as the environment degrades—links spiritual vocabulary to ecological observation
- The ayahuasca session leads Narby to observe his own body as a biological organism, questioning "who programmed me" — introduced as phenomenological experience, not evidence

**Logical Method:** Phenomenological + comparative epistemology. The chapter argues that both atheism ("blind chance drives everything") and theism are positions of faith that cannot be conclusively demonstrated, positioning agnosticism as the epistemically honest stance.

**Logical Gaps:**
- The chapter's philosophical argument — that denying intention in nature is itself an act of faith — is a legitimate philosophical point (made more rigorously by Nagel, Chalmers, and others). But Narby presents it through ayahuasca vision narration, which blurs the line between phenomenological experience and logical argument.
- The "intelligent design" movement is correctly identified as conflating biological complexity with theological inference and dismissed. But the author's own subsequent arguments about plant intelligence and slime mold chi-se sometimes occupy similar logical territory—attributing a directing intelligence to processes that may be more parsimoniously explained by selection and chemistry. The author does not fully reckon with this structural parallel.
- Jaques Monod's claim that "systematically denying purpose in nature" is the cornerstone of scientific method is cited as the dominant view, then challenged. But no scientific counterarguments contemporary to Monod are presented here to show this was already contested.

**Methodological Soundness:** This is the book's philosophical scaffolding chapter. Its argument—that mechanist materialism is not neutral but ideological—is defensible and has been made rigorously elsewhere. The ayahuasca framing does not strengthen the argument and may undermine it for skeptical readers.

### CHAPTER 4: Transformers — Indigenous Knowledge and Chimera Figures
**Core Claim:** The shamanic concept of transformation—humans becoming jaguars, spirits taking animal form—converges with evolutionary biology's demonstration that all species share genetic material and descend from common ancestors. We are all transformers.

**Supporting Evidence:**
- Shawi specialist Na'wiri Rafael Chanchari: stones, trees, and ecosystems all have souls/mothers; a Tana-ashi is a "general soul" of an ecosystem
- Kandoshi specialist Usi Kamarambi: the "owner of animals" appeared as a hybrid being—feathers, human feet, long nails
- Cave art evidence: Lascaux bird-headed man (~17,000 BP), Trois-Frères sorcerer/stag-human (~13,000 BP), Chauvet woman-bison-feline (~31,000 BP)
- Evolutionary biology: 99% of mouse genes found in humans; all species share DNA/RNA machinery

**Logical Method:** Cross-cultural convergence argument: prehistoric imagery + indigenous shamanic cosmology + modern evolutionary genetics all point to human-animal kinship as a deep and ancient recognition.

**Logical Gaps:**
- The interpretation of prehistoric cave paintings as evidence of shamanic practice is contested among archaeologists. The author acknowledges "it is difficult to prove that prehistoric humans practice what we now call shamanism" — but then uses the images as supporting evidence for the convergence thesis anyway. This is acknowledged gap that remains a gap.
- The jump from "all species share genetic heritage" (evolutionary biology) to "shamans are right that humans can transform into jaguars" (shamanic cosmology) is a categorical conflation. Genetic kinship is a structural fact; spiritual transformation is a different kind of claim about a different kind of process. Treating them as evidence for the same thesis confuses metaphor with mechanism.
- The indigenous specialists' framework — animals are human beings in their own world — is reported with respect and without critical engagement. No attempt is made to specify what predictions this framework makes that differ from a secular ecological framework.

**Methodological Soundness:** The archaeological evidence is handled carefully (the author acknowledges its limits). The convergence argument is rhetorically effective but logically imprecise: convergent metaphors in human cultures do not constitute evidence that the metaphors accurately describe biological reality.

### CHAPTER 5: Cabin Fever — History of the Mechanist View of Animals
**Core Claim:** The 20th century's dominance of mechanistic, behaviorist views of animal intelligence was a historically contingent philosophical stance—traceable to Bacon, Descartes, and Morgan's Canon—not an empirically derived finding. Science is now correcting this error.

**Supporting Evidence:**
- Descartes (1637): animals are machines made by God—better than human machines but without mind or spirit
- C. Lloyd Morgan's Canon: never attribute higher mental faculties when lower ones suffice—used for decades to deny animal cognition
- B. F. Skinner: "pigeon, rat, monkey—which is which, it doesn't matter"
- Donald Kennedy (editor, *Science*, 2002): "the zone of what we think of as uniquely human is gradually shrinking"
- Japanese primatologists: first to document pre-culture in macaques (potato-washing), tool use in chimpanzees—initially ignored by Western scientists
- Crow family (Clark's Nutcracker): remembers 30,000 food-hiding locations; scrub jays modify caches when observed, indicating social memory and foresight
- Pigeon experiment: distinguish Van Gogh from Chagall nearly as well as psychology students
- Songbirds: fire neurons during sleep in patterns matching their songs—evidence of practice-in-dreams; adult canaries generate new neurons, overturning century of theory

**Logical Method:** Intellectual history + accumulating empirical counter-evidence → thesis that mechanist dogma was ideology, not science.

**Logical Gaps:**
- Morgan's Canon is presented as the proximate cause of 20th-century mechanist bias. But this is overstated: Morgan's Canon is a methodological heuristic, not a metaphysical claim about animal minds. It can be applied correctly (avoiding unnecessary complexity) or incorrectly (denying complexity that the evidence requires). The problem was misapplication, not the canon itself.
- The Japanese primatology section argues that cultural background (Buddhist/Shinto acceptance of animal minds) enabled scientific discoveries Western frameworks missed. This is plausible, but the author presents it as demonstrated rather than as a hypothesis. The actual mechanism by which cultural priors enabled specific methodological choices (long-term individual tracking) is not fully specified.
- The accumulation of evidence for bird and primate cognition in this chapter is the book's strongest scientific grounding. But the author does not distinguish between: (a) complex behavior that can be explained without attributing inner states, and (b) complex behavior that logically requires inner states. This distinction matters for evaluating what "intelligence" actually implies.

**Methodological Soundness:** The intellectual history is accurate and well-sourced. The empirical findings cited (nutcracker spatial memory, scrub jay cache modification, pigeon art discrimination, adult neurogenesis) are from published research. The chapter's framing—that mechanist bias was culturally determined—is argued, not merely asserted.

### CHAPTER 6: Insect Minds — Bee Cognition and Abstract Concepts
**Core Claim:** Honeybees can handle abstract concepts (sameness, match-to-sample) and transfer learned rules across sensory modalities, falsifying the claim that abstraction is uniquely human. Small brains do not preclude sophisticated cognition.

**Supporting Evidence:**
- Giurfa et al. (*Nature*, reported): bees trained in Y-maze with colored entrance learned "same = sugar"—transferred across colors, shapes, and cross-modally from odor to visual signs
- Comparison to primates: some primate species could not perform the same task at first testing, while bees succeeded—cited by Giurfa
- Bee navigation: superior to human navigation in field conditions
- Mushroom body (170,000 densely packed neurons): sensory integration center enabling context-dependent decisions
- Calcium imaging of living bee brains: reveals which neurons activate when bees learn about odor
- Bee lifespan: 2–3 weeks, suggesting information acquisition happens extremely rapidly
- Leaf-cutter ants: use antibiotic-producing bacteria (streptomyces) on fungal crops for ~50 million years without developing pathogen resistance

**Logical Method:** Controlled experiment → behavioral demonstration → neurological substrate identification → comparison across species.

**Logical Gaps:**
- Giurfa's critique of his own field is significant: "a negative result shows nothing in the final analysis. A positive result shows something." This is methodologically sound—absence of evidence is not evidence of absence. But it also means the cumulative force of positive results must be weighed carefully. The bee abstraction finding is one study from one lab; it had not been widely replicated at the time of writing.
- The claim that bees "extract the logical structure of the world" (from Giurfa and Menzel) is presented as a conclusion from the cognitive architecture research. This is a strong interpretive claim; "context-dependent decision-making based on sensory integration" is what the data show. Whether this constitutes "logical structure extraction" in any robust sense is a question the chapter leaves open.
- Giurfa's admission that bees "sometimes behave stupidly" (dying against glass ceilings) is important: it shows the system has rigid components alongside plastic ones. The author acknowledges this, but does not integrate it fully—the chapter's emotional arc pulls toward emphasizing plasticity.
- Leaf-cutter ant antibiotic use (50 million years without resistance) is presented as evidence of intelligence. But the mechanism—whether the resistance-avoidance is a behavioral strategy or a selection effect on the bacterial strains—is not discussed. The implication of deliberate antibiotic stewardship is not established.

**Methodological Soundness:** The bee cognition research rests on published experimental work. Giurfa is a credible source, and his self-critical observations strengthen the chapter's analytical texture. The ant antibiotic claim requires more mechanistic specificity than the chapter provides.

### CHAPTER 7: Plants as Brains — Trawavas and Plant Intelligence
**Core Claim:** Plants exhibit adaptively variable behavior within the lifetime of the individual (Stenhouse's 1974 definition of intelligence), including foraging, competition, communication, and learning via molecular signaling mechanisms similar to those in neurons.

**Supporting Evidence:**
- Anthony Trawavas (University of Edinburgh, FRS): plants have senses for light, water, temperature, chemicals, vibrations, gravity, and sound; respond by changing growth patterns
- Stilt palm: changes location by growing new prop roots toward sunlight, letting shaded roots die—moves over months
- Ground ivy: puts down roots earlier in nutrient-rich patches, skips over poor ground—demonstrates resource sensing and selective foraging
- Daughter (parasitic plant, *Cuscuta*): correctly assesses host nutritional quality within an hour, adjusts coil number accordingly—foraging efficiency equals animal foragers (Kelly, early 1990s)
- Calcium signaling: tobacco plants glow when touched (response in milliseconds) due to calcium elevation—same charged atoms implicated in animal neuron learning
- When plants are threatened (e.g., drought), same molecules (calcium, enzymes) as in animal learning are unleashed; cells modify ion channels, synthesize proteins, change morphology—paralleling memory formation in animals
- Plant intercellular communication signals include RNA transcripts and proteins—"large molecules can handle large amounts of information"

**Logical Method:** Behavioral observation (field and controlled) + molecular biology convergence → attribution of intelligence via Stenhouse's definition.

**Logical Gaps:**
- Stenhouse's 1974 definition—"adaptively variable behavior within the lifetime of the individual"—is invoked as definitive, but it is one of many competing definitions. Using it resolves the debate by definitional fiat rather than by evidence. If one adopts a stricter definition requiring centralized processing or learning from error, the conclusion changes.
- The calcium signaling parallel is real and documented. But "same mechanism" does not mean "same function." Calcium is a ubiquitous signaling ion across all of biology—its presence in both plant responses and animal learning is not surprising and does not establish that plants are doing something equivalent to what neurons do. The inference requires more mechanistic precision.
- Trawavas's claim that "plant communication is likely to be as complex as within a brain" is presented as an expert judgment, not a demonstrated finding. The author himself notes: "it was still not clear to me how and where computation occurs in a plant." This acknowledged gap is significant—if the site and mechanism of plant computation cannot be specified, the intelligence claim remains inferential.
- The daughter plant experiments by Kelly are described in some detail but without the key control: do daughter's correct foraging decisions exceed chance at a statistically significant rate? The claim that "foraging strategies have the same efficacy as animal foragers" is a strong equivalence claim that requires head-to-head comparison studies.

**Methodological Soundness:** Trawavas is a credentialed source (FRS, University of Edinburgh) whose research on calcium signaling is published. His attribution of "intention" to plants, however, moves beyond the molecular data into philosophical territory he himself acknowledged is contested. The chapter's most defensible claim is: plants have molecular mechanisms for sensing and responding to environmental variables that share components with animal learning machinery. The strongest claim—that plants are intelligent—requires more definitional and mechanistic work.

### CHAPTER 8: Predators — Nervous System Evolution and the Human Animal
**Core Claim:** Nervous systems evolved as tools for predation and predator avoidance; tracing the architecture of nervous systems from sponges through nematodes to humans reveals that intelligence is distributed across evolutionary history, not concentrated in primates.

**Supporting Evidence:**
- Sponges (no neurons): generate electrical impulses to shut intake when water is murky—decision-making system without nerve cells
- Hydra (nerve net, no centralized brain): catches prey with tentacles; nerve net concentrates near mouth—earliest head as "a net of nerve cells at the mouth"
- Nematode *C. elegans*: 959 cells total, ~300 neurons forming a ring-shaped brain near the mouth; brain responds to taste, smell, temperature, touch; uses serotonin
- Octopus (500 million neurons): run mazes as well as rats; camouflage in fractions of a second; solve concealed food problems usually associated with large-brained vertebrates
- Human brain architecture: fish brain at base → reptilian brain (cerebellum) → mammalian brain (thalamus, amygdala, hippocampus) → cortex (3x larger relative to body than expected in a primate)
- Language and frontal lobe expansion: development of language "almost certainly the springboard for the leap from hominid to human" (Carter, *Mapping the Mind*)
- 99% of mouse genes found in humans

**Logical Method:** Evolutionary anatomy of nervous systems + comparative cognitive evidence → continuity thesis: intelligence is not a discrete human trait but a gradient extending through the history of life.

**Logical Gaps:**
- The chapter conflates two distinct arguments: (1) nervous systems evolved gradually and all gradations persist in living species — this is well-established; (2) therefore intelligence is distributed along that gradient — this does not follow without defining intelligence in a way that applies to sponge electrical responses and human language use simultaneously.
- The claim that humans are the "most dangerous of all macroscopic predators" is not supported with evidence in the chapter; it is treated as self-evident. It is plausible but requires specifying in what sense (by biomass impact? species extinction rate? territorial range?) to be a scientific claim.
- The evolutionary narrative (Australopithecus → Homo habilis → Homo sapiens) is presented in a condensed form that glosses significant paleontological debates. The author acknowledges that "paleontologists do not agree on many details" but then presents a specific lineage narrative. This is a minor issue in a chapter oriented toward broader continuity claims.

**Methodological Soundness:** The nematode and octopus cognitive findings are from published research. The evolutionary anatomy is standard. The continuity thesis is genuinely supported by the evidence cited, though the inference that all organisms along the neural continuum possess "intelligence" requires stronger definitional grounding.

### CHAPTER 9: Smart Slime — Nakagaki and Maze-Solving *Physarum*
**Core Claim:** The true slime mold *Physarum polycephalum* consistently solves mazes by contracting and rerouting its body to the shortest path between food sources—demonstrating cellular computation that produces outcomes typically attributed to intelligence.

**Supporting Evidence:**
- Nakagaki et al. (*Nature*, cited): *Physarum* pieces placed throughout a maze spread to fill all corridors, merge into a single cell, then—when food is placed at entrance and exit—withdraw from dead ends and reduce to a single tube spanning the shortest path, reliably
- Biochemical mechanism: rhythmic contractions regulated by calcium oscillators; no leader or central conductor in the protoplasm—self-organized
- Nakagaki's origin story: observing *Physarum* spontaneously form tube networks connecting food sources in oat-flake distributions—tubes optimized for maximum nutrient intake in minimum time
- The maze experiment has been replicated; Nakagaki's description of media attention in Japan vs. abroad reveals cultural difference in attributing intelligence to brainless organisms (Shinto animism vs. Christian anthropocentrism)
- *Physarum* can be diced and reassemble; can enter indefinite dormancy; is one giant cell

**Logical Method:** Controlled experiment + mathematical analysis of tube network optimization + cross-cultural epistemological comparison.

**Logical Gaps:**
- The key question—*how* does *Physarum* solve the maze?—is explicitly acknowledged as unanswered: "the details remain obscure." Nakagaki himself says the mechanism is "quite unknown." The evidence demonstrates the behavioral outcome (maze-solving) but not the computational process. Attributing "intelligence" or even "chise" to the organism may be premature without understanding the mechanism.
- Nakagaki's later caution about using the word "intelligence"—after critics argued *Physarum* could not be conscious—is reported honestly. His compromise (substituting "smartness" for "intelligence") is a semantic retreat that highlights the definitional problem at the core of the entire book: without a fixed definition, the claims shift to accommodate the evidence.
- The cultural comparison (Japan easily attributes chise to slime molds; Christian-influenced Western scientists resist it) is plausible as a sociological observation. But it cuts both ways: cultural ease in attributing intelligence to non-human entities does not make those attributions correct.

**Methodological Soundness:** The *Physarum* maze experiment is one of the most methodologically solid findings in the book—controlled, replicated, and published in *Nature*. The broader conclusions drawn from it (nature has chise; life itself is intelligent) exceed what the single-organism finding can bear.

### CHAPTER 10: Japanese Butterfly Machines — Arikawa and Color Vision
**Core Claim:** Butterfly visual systems are more complex than human visual systems in certain respects (more photoreceptor types, ultraviolet sensitivity, color constancy), demonstrating that sophisticated sensory and cognitive processing occurs in brains the size of two millimeters.

**Supporting Evidence:**
- Kentaro Arikawa (Yokohama City University): Japanese yellow swallowtail butterflies trained to feed on a specific color reliably chose it from arrays under different colored lights—demonstrating color constancy
- Butterfly retina: at least 5–6 spectral receptors (UV, violet, blue, green, red, broadband); humans have 3
- UV photoreceptors serve both foraging (detecting nectar guides invisible to humans) and mate selection (detecting UV stripes on female hind wings)
- Butterflies undergo metamorphosis: caterpillar with 6 simple eyes per side → butterfly with additional large compound eyes; simple eyes have 3 photoreceptor types → compound eyes have 6
- Silkworm males: completely immobile until exposed to female pheromones, then immediately activate—demonstrating precise chemosensory triggering
- Colorblind researcher's backstory: Arikawa doubted as a child that insects couldn't see red, observed butterflies preferring red flowers—motivated scientific career

**Logical Method:** Behavioral training experiments + neurological investigation + evolutionary analysis of metamorphosis.

**Logical Gaps:**
- The chapter's most interesting philosophical moment—Arikawa stating "there is no clear border between ourselves and machines"—is asserted as a conclusion from butterfly neurology research but not derived from it. The inference is philosophical, not empirical.
- Arikawa's belief that "there must be some primitive form of mind in these animals" is presented respectfully but acknowledged as a belief rather than a finding. He explicitly says "I hope so" when asked if butterflies think. This is intellectually honest but does not strengthen the book's evidentiary case.
- The claim that butterfly color vision is "superior in some respects to our own" is accurate (more photoreceptor types, UV range) but requires qualification: "superior for butterfly ecological tasks" is not the same as "superior" in some general sense.

**Methodological Soundness:** The color vision and color constancy experiments are from Arikawa's published research. The chapter handles uncertainty honestly—Arikawa explicitly acknowledges limits of knowledge about butterfly pain, dreaming, and sleep. This is the book's most epistemically careful scientist interview.

### CHAPTER 11: Mystery Jelly — Human Cognition and the Limits of Neuroscience
**Core Claim:** The human brain is neither fully understood nor fundamentally different in kind from other biological nervous systems. Consciousness, self, and the mechanism by which gray matter generates subjective experience remain genuine scientific mysteries—a fact that should make us humble about denying these properties to simpler organisms.

**Supporting Evidence:**
- Human brain: ~100 billion neurons, each with up to 10,000 synapses; a cubic millimeter of cortex contains over 2 miles of neural wire
- Synaptic strengthening during learning: calcium influx → neurotransmitter release → protein synthesis → new synapses (same mechanism in snails and humans)
- Brain imaging (fMRI): language not localized to Broca's/Wernicke's area alone—whole distributed networks activate; saying one word like "screwdriver" activates motor cortex
- Neural tape delay: humans perceive events ~80 milliseconds after they occur; brain edits for simultaneity (sight and sound of finger snap)
- Gut brain: 100 million neurons in the enteric nervous system, capable of learning and responding to emotions independently
- Somatic markers experiment: subjects sweating near "bad" card decks before consciously identifying which decks to avoid—body knows before conscious mind
- Adult neurogenesis: new neurons throughout life (discovered through canary research, confirmed in rats and humans); new neurons essential for fear memory formation
- Colin McGinn's argument: absence of a unified self in brain anatomy may reflect limits of our knowledge, not absence of the self

**Logical Method:** Review of neuroscience research + philosophical reflection on the "hard problem of consciousness" → argument for epistemic humility regarding attributions of mind to non-human organisms.

**Logical Gaps:**
- The chapter builds a strong case that the brain is not fully understood. But the inference drawn—that we should therefore be open to intelligence in slime molds—does not follow cleanly. The *degree* of our ignorance about human consciousness does not imply that simpler organisms have comparable properties; it implies we should be careful in both directions.
- McGinn's argument that "absence of a unified self in brain anatomy may mean we lack knowledge, not that the self doesn't exist" is presented as a rebuttal to eliminativist views. This is a legitimate philosophical position, but it is a philosophical argument, not an empirical finding.
- The somatic marker experiments (card deck sweating) are from Damasio's research; the author does not cite Damasio by name in this section, which makes the source harder to verify.

**Methodological Soundness:** The neuroscience review is accurate and draws from published research (adult neurogenesis, fMRI language studies, synaptic plasticity). The chapter's philosophical conclusion—that the mystery of consciousness counsels humility—is well-reasoned even if it cannot be proven from the scientific evidence alone.

### CHAPTER 12: Chise and Knowing Nature — Synthesis
**Core Claim:** Intelligence, as a concept, has been defined in too many human-centric ways to be useful. The Japanese concept *chise* (capacity to know) — applied neutrally across organisms — better describes what the evidence shows: a continuum of knowing from proteins to humans, with no sharp boundary.

**Supporting Evidence:**
- Thermostat distinction: a thermostat appears to make decisions but is programmed by humans; its "chise" is borrowed from its designers. Organisms have genuine chise because they can solve problems not pre-programmed — *Physarum* solves new mazes; butterfly visual systems handle novel lighting conditions
- Cellular signaling: ~11,000 signaling proteins in the human body; cells receive hundreds of simultaneous signals and must integrate them before acting; bacteria use quorum sensing to coordinate attacks
- Salmonella: produces 10+ proteins to break into host cells, using regulatory proteins "like a bandit with a pair of keys"
- Ubiquitin protein: "knows its way around the cell"
- Christopher Miller (biochemist, *Nature*): "proteins are intelligent beings" — hemoglobin "knows when you are sleeping or sprinting"
- Thomas Ward (chemistry professor): proteins recognize, interact, reproduce, and die; "if this is what you mean by to know, then I find proteins undeniably have the capacity to know"
- Human knowledge transmission accumulates outside the body in artifacts (writing) — this is what distinguishes human knowledge velocity, not the capacity to know itself
- Jaguars as example: top predators that control their power, move with stealth, have no rivals but humans

**Logical Method:** Conceptual clarification (*chise* as neutral term) + evidence across scales (proteins → bacteria → plants → insects → slime molds → humans) → continuity claim.

**Logical Gaps:**
- Miller's statement that "proteins are intelligent beings" is presented as a scientific finding. It is a metaphorical claim by a scientist, not a peer-reviewed conclusion. The author does not sufficiently distinguish between using "knows" as a technical shorthand for "responds precisely to" and using it to make a genuine cognitive claim.
- The thermostat distinction (programmed vs. not-programmed) is offered as the key boundary separating genuine chise from apparent chise. But this distinction is not clean: the organism's capacities are "programmed" by evolution and selection, just as a thermostat is programmed by an engineer. The author does not address this objection, which is the most obvious one.
- Ward's claim that proteins can "reproduce themselves" is noted as "recent research" without citation. Protein self-replication (prion-like mechanisms) is a real phenomenon, but contested and limited in scope. Including it without qualification slightly inflates the case.
- The jaguar closing metaphor — jaguars "control their power" — is rhetorically effective but infers intentional restraint from ecological behavior. Jaguars hide because stealth is an effective predatory strategy, not because they have chosen to exercise restraint. The metaphor conflates behavioral outcome with motivational state.

**Methodological Soundness:** The synthesis chapter is the book's most ambitious and most philosophically precarious. The thermostat distinction is genuinely useful. The *chise* concept provides a workable neutral term. The continuity argument — from proteins to humans — is supported by the evidence as a structural claim (shared mechanisms), but the inference that structural sharing implies shared cognitive properties is not demonstrated.

## BRIDGE: The Logical Architecture of the Whole

**Three tensions run through the book's argumentative spine:**

*Tension 1: Evidence vs. Interpretation.* At every level of the biological hierarchy — bee abstraction, plant calcium signaling, slime mold maze-solving, protein recognition — Narby presents documented experimental findings and then attributes *chise* to the organism on the basis of those findings. The findings are often solid. The attribution is always a philosophical leap. The book never fully acknowledges that "exhibits behavior X" and "has the capacity to know" are different kinds of claims requiring different kinds of evidence.

*Tension 2: Indigenous Knowledge as Evidence vs. Indigenous Knowledge as Perspective.* Narby treats the shamanic frameworks (owner of animals, plant spirits, animal humanness) with consistent respect and presents them as convergently validated by science. But he never specifies what the shamanic frameworks predict that differ from the scientific findings. If slime molds solving mazes is what "plants and animals have spirits" means, then the shamanic claim is trivially true. If it means something stronger (e.g., that shamans can actually communicate with plant mothers and receive accurate botanical information), the evidence remains underdetermined. The convergence is asserted; it is never tested.

*Tension 3: The Definitional Problem the Book Cannot Escape.* The word "intelligence" has too many meanings; hence *chise*. But *chise* (capacity to know) faces the same problem. Does a thermostat know the temperature? Does a protein "know" a pathogen's molecular signature? The thermostat distinction—programmed vs. not-programmed—is offered as the boundary marker, but evolution functions as a programmer, which dissolves the distinction. The book's most important unresolved philosophical question is: what would it mean for an organism to *not* have chise? If the answer is "nothing living fails to have chise," then chise is not an empirical property but a definitional one.

**The book's most proven claims:**
- Honeybees handle abstract concepts in controlled experiments
- *Physarum* reliably solves mazes by self-organized cellular computation
- Macaws select toxin-binding clay over adjacent non-binding clay
- Plant cells use calcium signaling mechanisms structurally parallel to animal neuron signaling
- The 20th-century mechanistic consensus about animal behavior was ideologically influenced, not purely empirically derived
- Adult neurogenesis occurs in humans, overturning a century of dogma

**The book's most significant unproven claims:**
- Indigenous shamanic frameworks about plant/animal intelligence converge with scientific findings (parallels exist; equivalence is not established)
- Proteins have a "capacity to know" in any non-metaphorical sense
- The slime mold's maze-solving reflects genuine computation rather than a self-organizing physical-chemical process that produces maze-solving as an emergent property without any cognitive analog

**The book's most significant acknowledged gaps:**
- How does *Physarum* actually solve the maze mechanistically? (Nakagaki explicitly says: unknown)
- How does brain jelly produce subjective experience? (Standard hard problem; author honestly acknowledges no answer)
- What specific testable predictions does the "intelligence in nature" thesis generate that a purely mechanistic account would not? (Never addressed)

# What It Would Take to Prove

The hardest question Jeremy Narby's *Intelligence in Nature* raises is not the one it asks. The book asks: is there intelligence in nature? The answer, assembled across twelve chapters of genuine scientific fieldwork, Amazonian travel, and conversations with researchers from Edinburgh to Yokohama, is yes—or at least, probably, or at minimum, more than we thought. Honeybees master abstract rules. Slime molds solve mazes. Macaws select the precise clay that neutralizes their diet's toxins. Daughter plants correctly estimate their hosts' nutritional value within an hour, adjusting their coil number accordingly. Plants use calcium signaling to respond to touch in milliseconds, through mechanisms structurally parallel to animal neuron learning. These findings are real. They are published. They hold up.

The harder question is: what would it take to prove that these findings demonstrate *intelligence*—and not simply sophisticated, adaptive, selection-optimized behavior that happens to resemble intelligence from the outside?

Narby does not ask this question. He asks a softer one: what would it take for Western scientists to *accept* that nature is intelligent? His answer is cultural and epistemological: Western science is philosophically hobbled by the Cartesian-Baconian prohibition on teleology, a prohibition that is itself a metaphysical stance, not a neutral empirical starting point. This is a legitimate and important observation. Francis Bacon's argument that attributing goals to nature is anthropomorphic projection, and Descartes' subsequent reduction of animals to machines, were not derived from evidence. They were assumptions imported into science from a philosophical tradition that needed to establish human dominion over nature and loosen the Church's grip on knowledge. Narby's intellectual history is accurate and his critique well-placed. The 20th century's dominance of mechanist behaviorism—B.F. Skinner's "pigeon, rat, monkey, which is which, it doesn't matter"—was ideologically inflected, not purely empirically derived.

But correcting a philosophical overcorrection is not the same as reaching a defensible conclusion. The mechanists were wrong to deny minds wholesale to non-human organisms. It does not follow that all organisms have minds.

---

The book's central evidentiary move—at every scale, from proteins to slime molds to bees to plants—is: here is a biological entity that exhibits behavior B; B is the kind of behavior we associate with intelligence in systems we already accept as intelligent; therefore this entity has intelligence (or *chise*, Narby's adopted Japanese term for "capacity to know"). This is an inference to best explanation, and in many cases it is a reasonable one. But it requires a claim that the evidence is best explained by intelligence rather than by other mechanisms—and Narby never systematically makes that case.

Consider *Physarum polycephalum*, the true slime mold. Nakagaki and colleagues' maze experiment is the book's most methodologically rigorous finding. A single-celled organism, with no neurons, no brain, no nervous system, placed in a maze with food at entrance and exit, reliably contracts and reroutes until it spans the shortest path between food sources. Nakagaki himself declares: "this organism is so clever and cunning." He wins a point for intellectual openness. He also acknowledges, with equal frankness, that how the organism does this is "quite unknown"—the mechanism is uncharacterized, though calcium oscillators and self-organized contraction waves are implicated.

Here is the problem: self-organized physical-chemical processes that produce optimal outcomes are well-documented in non-living systems. Soap bubbles form minimal surfaces without intelligence. River networks branch in fractal patterns without computation. Crystals grow with structural regularity without cognition. The question for *Physarum* is not whether it produces maze-solving outcomes—it demonstrably does—but whether the mechanism behind those outcomes requires positing something beyond well-understood self-organizing chemistry. Nakagaki does not know the mechanism. Narby does not ask what evidence would distinguish a "genuine computation" hypothesis from an "emergent self-organization" hypothesis. The gap is not acknowledged.

---

The chapter on plant intelligence is where the book's thesis is most ambitious and most precarious. Anthony Trawavas (FRS, Edinburgh) argues that plants exhibit "adaptively variable behavior within the lifetime of the individual"—David Stenhouse's 1974 definition of intelligence. The daughter plant (*Cuscuta*) makes correct foraging choices. The stilt palm effectively walks toward sunlight. Ground ivy puts down roots preferentially in nutrient-rich patches. These are real observations.

The calcium signaling finding is genuinely fascinating: Trawavas's modified tobacco plants glow in milliseconds when touched, through calcium elevation in their cells—the same ion, the same pathway, implicated in animal neuron learning. This structural parallel is the best empirical case in the book for taking plant cognition seriously.

But "same mechanism" does not mean "same function." Calcium is the most ubiquitous signaling ion in all of biology, present in processes ranging from muscle contraction to fertilization to immune response to neuroplasticity. Its presence in both plant touch-response and animal memory formation establishes a shared molecular component; it does not establish that what plants are doing is equivalent to what neurons are doing. The inference Trawavas wants—that plants compute, decide, and in some sense know—requires characterizing not just the molecular component but the information architecture that component operates within. By Trawavas's own admission, "where computation occurs in a plant" remains unknown: "it's in the whole organism." This is a placeholder, not an explanation.

Stenhouse's definition—adaptively variable behavior within the lifetime of the individual—is doing significant work here, and it should be examined. By this definition, a human immune system adapting to a new pathogen over the course of an infection exhibits intelligence. A developing embryo adapting its gene expression to hormonal gradients exhibits intelligence. These are adaptive, variable, individual-lifetime-specific behaviors. Are they intelligent? Narby would presumably say yes. The question is whether the definition has been pulled so wide that it no longer picks out what we intuitively care about when we ask whether organisms "know."

---

The book's most philosophically careful chapter is its penultimate one, on human cognition. Narby reviews the neuroscience of learning, memory, and consciousness honestly, arriving at the genuine hard problem: we do not understand how subjective experience arises from neural tissue. The brain is malleable, distributed, subject to tape-delays and unconscious processing—and none of this explains how it generates the first-person sense of being someone. Narby uses this honest ignorance to argue for epistemic humility about denying cognitive properties to other organisms.

This is a legitimate argumentative move, but it runs in both directions. Our ignorance about consciousness does not license attributing it to everything—it licenses uncertainty about where to draw boundaries. Narby wants the uncertainty to fall on the side of generosity: when in doubt, grant chise. A more cautious reading of the same epistemic situation would recommend: when in doubt, specify what evidence would settle the question. The book consistently chooses the former and consistently avoids the latter.

The *chise* concept is the book's most useful contribution. The Japanese *chisei* (knowingness, recognizingness) is genuinely a more neutral term than "intelligence," which has accumulated too much definitional baggage. Using it to describe what *Physarum*, bees, daughter plants, and proteins all exhibit—a capacity to sense, integrate, and respond to environmental variables in ways that exceed simple reflexes—is descriptively accurate and philosophically honest. The thermostat distinction Narby draws, with help from neuroscientist Valerie Stone, is also genuinely clarifying: a thermostat appears to know the temperature, but its knowing is borrowed from the human who programmed it; a slime mold's maze-solving is not borrowed from any programmer.

But Narby does not notice that evolution is a programmer. The slime mold's self-organizing contraction dynamics are the product of billions of years of selection pressure on *Physarum* ancestral lineages. The behavior is "programmed" in the same sense a thermostat is programmed—the difference is that natural selection is a distributed, iterative, unintelligent process, while a thermostat engineer is a centralized, intentional agent. This matters: if what we mean by genuine chise is "not externally programmed," then neither slime molds nor thermostats qualify. If what we mean is "locally autonomous"—responding to the immediate environment without consulting an external script—then both qualify. The thermostat distinction dissolves under inspection.

---

None of this makes the book's core project wrong. Narby's fieldwork is genuine, his scientific interlocutors are credible, and his findings are real. The intellectual corrective he offers—that Western science overcorrected toward mechanism in the 20th century, and that organisms across the biological hierarchy exhibit sophisticated, adaptive, flexible behavior that warrants more than dismissal—is well-supported and important.

What the book does not prove—and what it would need to prove to fully vindicate its thesis—is that this sophisticated behavior is better explained by attributing a genuine capacity to know than by characterizing the self-organizing mechanisms that produce it. The gap between "exhibits behavior that resembles knowing" and "has the capacity to know" is precisely the gap that Narby's fieldwork, however earnest, cannot close alone.

The question is not whether there is something it is like to be a *Physarum*. We do not know, and Nakagaki is honest about this. The question is what evidence would tell us. Until that question is formulated, "intelligence in nature" remains a necessary provocation—challenging a mechanist orthodoxy that deserved to be challenged—rather than a demonstrated finding.

That is enough. Provocations that are right about what they are challenging do genuine intellectual work, even when they cannot fully defend where they want to land.
