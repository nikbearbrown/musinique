### SECTION 4: Site and Insight (Chapter 4 — McKesson and Denihan Hotels)
**Core Claim:** The near-term value of big data is less in sophisticated AI and more in the "more mundane realm of counting, monitoring, and seeing things with greater clarity." Decisions fortified by data outperform experience and intuition, but the appropriate division of labor assigns data the role of disciplined assistant to human judgment.

**Supporting Evidence:**
- McKesson: centralized inventory of high-cost cancer/antibiotic drugs improved on-time delivery to 99% (from <90%) while cutting inventory by 50% for that category; overall network inventory reduced by $1 billion
- Denihan Hotels: 300% ROI on a "win-back" program for lapsed customers; general manager Brian Gelick reports "double-digit" productivity and revenue improvement (estimated, not audited)
- Steve Jobs anecdote: "no market research" behind the iPad; decision-making as cultivated intuition, not data extraction
- Kahneman's "thinking fast and slow" framework applied: fast/System 1 thinking navigates daily life efficiently but is error-prone; data systems should act as "over-the-shoulder critics" supplying slow/System 2 perspective
- Philip Tetlock's superforecasters: quantified, collaborative forecasting with continuous accuracy tracking improves human prediction within time horizons of ~1 year

**Logical Method:** Paired case studies (industrial logistics + hospitality) + psychological framing (Kahneman + Tetlock) to establish that data enhances but does not replace human judgment.

**Logical Gaps:**
- The Denihan "double-digit improvement" estimate is the general manager's own assessment, not an audited finding. Lohr explicitly notes that Denihan never conducted a cost-benefit analysis of its data investment. Presenting this as a success case without that caveat being foregrounded is analytically inconsistent with the book's stated rigor.
- The Jobs anecdote is used to illustrate the virtue of cultivated intuition, but Jobs is an extreme outlier in product judgment (and also happened to be deeply wrong about market research in some documented cases). Treating Jobs as a model for how intuition works is a survivorship bias problem.
- The Kahneman framing is used in service of "data as assistant" but Kahneman's own work suggests that the value of data-based nudges is largest precisely when human experts resist them—not when they welcome data as a collaborative tool. The chapter underplays the human resistance to data-informed correction that Kahneman's research consistently documents.

**Methodological Soundness:** The McKesson case is the most rigorously documented case study in the book. The Denihan case is suggestive but unverified. The psychological framing is appropriately hedged.
