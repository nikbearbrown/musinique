### CHAPTER 1: The Importance of Social Statistics

**Core Claim:** Social statistics are not discovered facts but social products—created by people with interests, shaped by organizational practices, and interpreted within ideological frameworks. Their apparent authority as numbers conceals their social construction.

**Supporting Evidence:**

*Historical origin of statistics as persuasion tools:*
- 19th-century New York reformers estimated 10,000 prostitutes (c. 10% of the city's female population); police simultaneously reported 1,223—a tenfold difference explained entirely by the competing interests of each party
- The term "statistics" itself originates in 17th-century political arithmetic, explicitly designed to help governments devise policies, not to neutrally describe reality

*Organizational construction of official statistics:*
- Marriage statistics depend entirely on couples' compliance with licensing procedures; couples who marry outside the system are invisible to the record
- Suicide statistics depend on coroners' judgments in ambiguous cases—judgments that vary systematically by coroner and jurisdiction
- Domestic violence statistics depend on police officers' discretionary decisions at the scene, decisions influenced by shift timing, department policy, and the officer's own interpretation of the situation

*Structural bias toward large numbers:*
- Activists require big numbers to justify concern; officials require small numbers to demonstrate competence; both parties produce statistics that serve their institutional interests rather than approximate reality

**Logical Method:** Sociological constructivism applied to a typically exempted domain (official government statistics). Best's argument is that the social construction of knowledge applies even to the hardest-seeming numbers.

**Logical Gaps:**
- The chapter establishes that organizational practices shape statistics, but does not specify what conditions make official statistics more or less reliable. The argument could be read as implying that all official statistics are equally unreliable—which is not Best's position, but the chapter does not clearly delineate when the bias is severe versus negligible.
- The prostitution example conflates two separate problems: definitional differences (who counts as a prostitute?) and measurement differences (how do you find and count them?). These are distinct sources of error that Best later separates in Chapter 2 but conflates here.

**Methodological Soundness:** The historical and sociological analysis is well-founded and conventionally accepted in sociology of knowledge. The implicit normative claim—that statistics should be more accurate than they are—requires a standard of accuracy that Best does not define in this chapter.
