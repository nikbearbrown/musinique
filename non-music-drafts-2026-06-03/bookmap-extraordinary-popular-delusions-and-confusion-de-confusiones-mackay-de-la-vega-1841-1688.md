# BOOKMAP: Extraordinary Popular Delusions and the Madness of Crowds & Confusión de Confusiones
**Charles Mackay (1841) / Joseph de la Vega (1688) | Compiled Edition with Introduction by Martin Fridson**

---

## PART 1: SECTION-BY-SECTION LOGICAL MAPPING

---

### SECTION 1: Peter Bernstein Foreword
**Core Claim:** The financial behaviors documented in 17th-century markets are functionally identical to behaviors visible in contemporary markets, suggesting that market psychology is historically invariant—not a product of technology, institutional structure, or regulatory sophistication.

**Supporting Evidence:**
- Observational assertion: "most features of market behavior today are little different from market behavior in the 17th century"
- Structural argument: in the 17th century, financial markets barely existed; the instruments, mechanics of settlement, and behavioral patterns all emerged simultaneously, suggesting they are intrinsic to markets rather than artifacts of immaturity
- Historical-causal claim: Columbus's explorations created the first non-zero-sum wealth accumulation in Europe, which necessitated capital markets, which immediately produced speculative behavior

**Logical Method:** Argument by historical analogy, moving from "these behaviors appeared immediately in the earliest markets" to "therefore they are constitutive of market psychology, not contingent features."

**Logical Gaps:**
- The claim that market behavior is invariant rests on selective historical observation. Bernstein acknowledges the question—"are contemporary markets less sophisticated than we think, or were early markets more sophisticated?"—but explicitly declines to answer it. This is not analysis; it is rhetorical deferral.
- The causal chain (Columbus → new wealth → non-zero-sum accumulation → capital markets → speculation) is historically plausible but omits significant mediating variables: institutional development, legal frameworks, the role of the Dutch state in enabling the VOC. Correlation of timing is not causation.
- "Markets still in their historical infancy could display behavior so familiar to contemporary readers" proves that such behavior appeared early. It does not prove the behavior is inevitable, universal, or unstoppable—which is the implicit policy implication.

**Methodological Soundness:** The foreword functions as advocacy for the books' importance, not as analysis. Treat its claims as motivating context, not established propositions.

### SECTION 2: Martin Fridson Introduction — "In the Realm of the Senseless"
**Core Claim:** Both Mackay's *Extraordinary Popular Delusions* and de la Vega's *Confusión de Confusiones* endure because they offer complementary explanatory frameworks for market irrationality—Mackay attributing it to mass hysteria, de la Vega to coordinated manipulation—and investors find comfort in at least one framework for understanding their losses.

**Supporting Evidence:**
- Documented reception: Mackay's book was championed by Baruch (1932 republication), Templeton, and has been continuously cited for 150+ years
- Empirical challenge to Mackay: Peter Garber's research shows Mackay plagiarized Johann Beckmann's tulip account and that the iconic "sailor eating the tulip bulb" anecdote likely postdates the events; Garber argues the tulip price swings were rational given bulb genetics and reproduction economics
- Empirical challenge to the South Sea myth: Larry Neal argues the South Sea Company served legitimate debt-conversion purposes before overreaching
- Modern parallel: the MMM pyramid scheme in Russia (1994), where investors returned to buy shares *after* the collapse, explicitly acknowledging it was a Ponzi scheme

**Logical Method:** Comparative reception analysis + challenge from economic historiography + contemporary parallel.

**Logical Gaps:**
- The Garber demolition of tulipomania is presented and then essentially dismissed: "no amount of scholarly debunking is likely to extinguish the fondness of investors for extraordinary popular delusions." This is an accurate sociological observation but not an epistemological argument. The fact that a false narrative is beloved does not rehabilitate it.
- The MMM example is deployed as proof of "the timelessness of de la Vega's confusions and Mackay's delusions." But the MMM case arguably proves only that Ponzi schemes recur—which requires no historical precedent from 1688 Amsterdam to explain.
- Fridson presents Mackay (mass hysteria) and de la Vega (rational manipulation) as complementary. But these frameworks are in genuine tension: if crashes are caused by coordinated bear syndicates, they are not "popular delusions"; if they are caused by mass irrationality, manipulation is secondary. The introduction does not resolve this tension.

**Methodological Soundness:** The introduction is honest about Mackay's historiographical weaknesses while insufficiently exploring the implication: if the foundational anecdotes are fabricated or misattributed, what survives of the argument?

### SECTION 3: Mackay Preface (1841)
**Core Claim:** The book collects "remarkable instances of those moral epidemics" to demonstrate "how easily the masses had been led astray and how imitative and gregarious men are even in their infatuations and crimes."

**Logical Method:** Explicit statement of thesis as moral taxonomy of collective folly.

**Logical Gaps:**
- "Moral epidemics" is a metaphor that does the analytical work of a mechanism without providing one. Epidemics spread through pathogen transmission with identifiable vectors. Mackay's "epidemics" have no equivalent mechanistic account—they spread by... imitation? desire? proximity? The metaphor forecloses rather than enables analysis.
- The decision to exclude "religious manias" as "incompatible with the limits prescribed" is telling. Financial manias and religious manias share structural features (escalating commitment, social proof, catastrophic collapse). Excluding one class of the phenomenon weakens any claim to general theory.

**Methodological Soundness:** The preface announces an empirical project (collection of instances) but frames it as moral philosophy (demonstration of human gregariousness). These are different goals with different standards of proof.

### SECTION 4: The Mississippi Scheme (Mackay)
**Core Claim:** John Law's paper currency system and Mississippi Company represent a case study in the destruction caused by fiat currency expansion beyond productive asset backing, compounded by government's inability to resist using successful financial instruments for political ends (debt monetization).

**Supporting Evidence:**
- Documented state finances: national debt 3,000 million livres, annual revenue 145 million, expenses 142 million—leaving 3 million to service 3,000 million in debt. This is arithmetic, not assertion.
- Law's bank notes initially outperformed metallic currency because they were not subject to arbitrary debasement: "a note of law's bank retained its original value"
- The system collapsed when control passed from Law to the Regent, who issued 1,000 million livres in new notes immediately—ten times Law's prior maximum of 60 million
- Social effects documented with anecdotal specificity: new aristocracy of coachmen and servants; the Count Dahlm assassination case; 305,000 population increase in Paris from speculative migration

**Logical Method:** Narrative causation—identify the structural condition (fiscal insolvency), the catalyst (Law's credible currency reform), the inflection point (government takeover of the bank), and the collapse mechanics.

**Logical Gaps:**
- Mackay's rehabilitation of Law ("more sinned against than sinning," "thoroughly acquainted with the philosophy and true principles of credit") depends heavily on the claim that Law opposed the 1,000-million-livre issuance. Mackay acknowledges this is unknown: "whether law opposed the inordinate increase is not known." A character verdict built on an unknown variable is conjecture, not proof.
- The tulip anecdote problem applies here too: Mackay's entertaining anecdotes (the hunchback renting his hump as a writing desk, the doctor obsessing about stock prices at a patient's bedside) are individually unverifiable and collectively function to create a atmosphere of mass delusion rather than to prove it.
- The collapse mechanics focus on individual psychological breakdown (panic, flight, popular rage) rather than on the structural impossibility: 2,600 million livres in notes against roughly 1,300 million in coin. The arithmetic was the mechanism; the panic was the symptom. Mackay inverts this priority.

**Methodological Soundness:** The Mississippi chapter is Mackay at his strongest structurally—the fiscal numbers he provides are internally consistent and the timeline of policy decisions is documented. The weakness is the constant slippage from structural analysis into moral theater.

### SECTION 5: The South Sea Bubble (Mackay)
**Core Claim:** The South Sea Bubble represents a case of deliberate fraud by directors who understood they were inflating fictitious values, combined with wholesale corruption of government officials who received bribes in the form of fictitious stock allocations. The bubble was not primarily a case of mass delusion but of organized theft enabled by regulatory capture.

**Supporting Evidence:**
- Documented bribery: the Committee of Secrecy found 1,259,325 pounds of fictitious stock distributed to government officials. Specific figures: Earl of Sunderland £50,000; Duchess of Kendall £10,000; Secretary Crags £30,000; Chancellor Aislabie £794,451 in documented transactions
- The stock progression is documented: 130 → 300 → 550 → 890 → 1,000 → 135 (collapse). The timing of insiders' sales (Sir John Blunt and directors selling before the peak) is documented through parliamentary inquiry
- Quantitative absurdity: 10 million pounds of metallic currency could not circulate 200 million pounds of paper credit—a point made contemporaneously by MP Broderick and cited by Mackay. This is the structural diagnosis.
- Parliamentary punishment: specific amounts confiscated from specific directors are documented (Sir John Blunt: £5,000 retained from £183,000; Edward Gibbon: £10,000 from £106,000)

**Logical Method:** Fraud investigation methodology—document the scheme, document the beneficiaries, document the losses, document the punishment.

**Logical Gaps:**
- Gibbon's account of his grandfather's treatment, cited sympathetically by Mackay, raises a genuine issue: the parliamentary punishment was a retroactive bill of pains and penalties without legal due process. Mackay acknowledges this was "bold oppression" that "can scarcely be shielded by the omnipotence of Parliament." This tension—between the justice of punishing fraud and the injustice of retroactive punishment without trial—is noted but not resolved.
- The "bubble companies" list (77+ schemes) is presented as evidence of mass irrationality. But Fridson's own analysis suggests that some of these projects (coal supply to London, fire insurance, textile manufacturing) were economically sensible proposals that happened to be launched during a period of speculative fever. Calling all of them frauds conflates genuine fraud with unlucky timing.
- The "company for carrying on an undertaking of great advantage but nobody to know what it is" is Mackay's favorite South Sea anecdote. It is uncited. No contemporary primary source is provided. Given Garber's findings about tulip anecdotes, this deserves skepticism.

**Methodological Soundness:** The South Sea chapter is structurally the strongest in Mackay's work because it rests on parliamentary records with specific numbers. The corruption case is proven. The "mass delusion" thesis requires more scrutiny—most individual participants were rationally responding to price signals, not irrationally deluded.

### SECTION 6: Tulipomania (Mackay)
**Core Claim:** Dutch society between 1634–1637 experienced a collective speculative delusion in tulip bulbs that penetrated all social classes, drove prices to 5,500 florins for a single bulb, and collapsed suddenly to near-zero, ruining thousands.

**Supporting Evidence:**
- Mackay provides a specific price table: Admiral Leifken (400 parrots) = 4,400 florins; Admiral van der Eyck (446 parrots) = 1,260 florins; Semper Augustus (200 parrots) = 5,500 florins; a single Viceroy bulb traded for an itemized basket of goods (2 lasts of wheat, 4 fat oxen, 8 fat swine, wine, beer, butter, cheese, clothing, silver cup) totaling 2,500 florins
- The "sailor eating the tulip bulb" anecdote and the "English botanist dissecting an Admiral" anecdote

**Logical Method:** Anecdote accumulation as proof of collective irrationality.

**Logical Gaps:**
- The Garber critique, acknowledged in the Fridson introduction, is devastating: Mackay assembled this account from secondhand sources, plagiarized Beckmann, and the signature anecdotes likely postdate the events. More importantly, Garber shows that comparable price swings in rare bulb varieties occurred in periods before and after 1636–1637, and that the most extreme prices applied to informal futures contracts in common varieties—not the rare varieties Mackay describes.
- The Fridson introduction cites a Columbia University economist's argument that high prices for "Semper Augustus"-type bulbs were rational: a single prized bulb could produce millions of descendants over decades, each carrying the distinctive coloration. The net present value calculation makes the high price defensible, not lunatic.
- Mackay attributes the crash to "the breath of popular mistrust." Garber attributes it to the collapse of informal futures markets in common bulbs—a much more specific and testable mechanism. Mackay's explanation is untestable.

**Methodological Soundness:** Of Mackay's three major case studies, tulipomania is the weakest. The primary sources are secondhand; the signature anecdotes are unverifiable; the mechanism of contagion is asserted not demonstrated; and the most rigorous subsequent scholarship substantially revises the standard account. Read as cultural history, this chapter is vivid. Read as economic analysis, it is unreliable.

### SECTION 7: Kellenbenz Introduction to *Confusión de Confusiones*
**Core Claim:** De la Vega's 1688 text is historically significant as the first extended account of stock market operations anywhere, produced within 86 years of the first organized equity market (the VOC, 1602), demonstrating that financial sophistication—options, forward contracts, short selling, settlement clearing, market manipulation—developed almost instantaneously once transferable shares existed.

**Supporting Evidence:**
- Documented transaction types by 1688: spot purchases, hypothecation (margin), forward contracts, prolongation (rollover), put and call options with premium payments, duck-a-ton (fractional synthetic) shares, clearing (rescounter) mechanisms, monthly settlement dates
- Social-historical context: the Sephardic community in Amsterdam faced guild restrictions on most occupations but had full access to wholesale trade and financial markets; one 1701 English observer estimated they conducted "17 parts in 20" of stock speculation
- Historiographical note: the book was written for Portuguese-community readers (possibly as a manual for London-bound relatives), later elaborated into literary form—explaining both its practical detail and its ornate style

**Logical Method:** Contextual historiography—establish who wrote it, why, for whom, and what institutional conditions made the described market possible.

**Logical Gaps:**
- The "17 parts in 20" attribution to Sephardic traders is a single contemporary English source with obvious potential for ethnic exaggeration. Kellenbenz appropriately hedges ("said to"), but this figure has since circulated as if established.
- The identification of de la Vega's text as "the first book" on stock market operations is accurate but potentially misleading: the pamphlet literature around Dutch short-selling edicts (1610, 1621, 1636) and Nicolaas Moucherons' 1687 anti-speculation pamphlet all preceded it. De la Vega is the most comprehensive, not the first to write.

**Methodological Soundness:** Kellenbenz's introduction is the most academically rigorous section of the compiled volume—careful about evidence, clear about what is documented versus inferred, attentive to social context.

### SECTION 8: *Confusión de Confusiones* — Four Dialogues (De la Vega, 1688)
**Core Claim (across all four dialogues):** The Amsterdam stock exchange is a system in which rational information (India trade results, European political conditions, dividend prospects) is systematically distorted by coordinated manipulation, irrational psychology, and structural features (monthly settlements, short-selling legality uncertainty) such that no analytical framework reliably produces profit—yet the market is also an irreplaceable source of liquidity, employment, and capital formation.

**Supporting Evidence:**

*Dialogue 1:*
- Detailed mechanics of VOC share trading: 500 Flemish pounds nominal value, quoted at ~580% in 1688 = ~17,000 guilder market value per share
- Three investor classes documented: dividends-only capitalists (indifferent to price), trading merchants (short-term price-driven), speculators (pure delta exposure)
- The "appeal to Frederick" mechanism: buyers of time-forward contracts could legally repudiate if sellers had not put shares "on the time account"—creating a one-sided default option that systematically favored buyers in declining markets

*Dialogue 2:*
- Three-factor model for share price: conditions in India, European politics, exchange opinion itself (internal market dynamics). The third factor explicitly introduces reflexivity—prices move because participants believe prices will move
- Four principles of speculation: (1) never advise others; (2) take gains without regret; (3) profits are ephemeral; (4) patience and capital are necessary conditions
- Bulls described as cognitively biased toward optimism; bears as biased toward catastrophizing. De la Vega recommends structurally biasing toward bulls because "experience has shown that usually the bulls are victorious"—an empirical claim about long-run equity drift made in 1688

*Dialogue 3:*
- Mechanics of settlement: spot transfer (at company offices, registered in books), monthly settlement on the 20th paid on the 25th, and forward contracts at longer dates. The rescounters (clearing agents) balance offsetting positions and collect/pay differences
- Duck-a-ton shares: 1/10th-nominal synthetic units, settled by cash difference at monthly meetings, price set by "two impartial men" raising a stick. Explicitly called gambling by courts—legally unenforceable
- Broker typology: sworn brokers (licensed, prohibited from proprietary trading, largely compliant), free brokers (unlicensed, proprietary trading common, dressed as agency)

*Dialogue 4:*
- Twelve documented manipulation tactics of bear syndicates: time sales to create declining signal; secret purchases from bulls to spread false selling rumors; using wealthy capitalists as unwitting instruments to trigger panic selling; cornering loan funds to prevent bulls from hypothecating; selling call options to constrain future buying; buying put options to exhaust bull buying capacity; lending to bulls against their stock then selling that stock; fabricating letters with alarming news; using respected outsiders to sell one or two lots as a "new seller" signal; whispering false secrets loudly; selling government bonds to signal political crisis; trading contrary to known position to generate information about market depth
- The 1688 crash is analyzed specifically: good cargo news + bear manipulation of war rumors + collapse of duck-a-ton market simultaneously. De la Vega identifies the specific mechanism (cargo value understated because only quantity, not quality, was initially disclosed) and the bear exploitation of information asymmetry.

**Logical Gaps:**
- De la Vega's claim that "usually the bulls are victorious" is an empirical assertion made without systematic data. It may reflect survivorship bias: the bulls who lost everything are not present in the coffeehouses providing testimony.
- The twelve bear tactics are described with remarkable specificity—detail that suggests either direct observation or participation. De la Vega acknowledges he made and lost five fortunes through speculation. This raises the question of whether the "shareholder" voice in the dialogues is a neutral observer or a retrospective rationalizer.
- The appeal to Frederick mechanism is described confusingly. Kellenbenz notes the legal uncertainty was genuine and courts issued contradictory rulings. De la Vega's account of when it applied and when it didn't should be read as contemporary confusion documented rather than settled legal analysis.
- The duck-a-ton market collapsed just as de la Vega was writing. He describes this collapse with evident distress ("Sad indeed is a catastrophe that annihilates at one stroke names, dreams, persons, fortunes and reputations"). The emotional proximity affects objectivity without invalidating the descriptive content.

**Methodological Soundness:** De la Vega's dialogues are primary source documentation with the limitations inherent to the form: a literary device (dialogue) deployed to describe a technical system, by an insider with personal losses at stake, in a style (Estilo Culto) that prioritizes elaborate allusion over precision. The tactical descriptions in Dialogue 4 are the most practically specific and analytically valuable content in the entire compiled volume.

## BRIDGE: Synthesizing the Logical Architecture

**The volume's central argumentative problem:** Mackay and de la Vega are bound together by the claim that markets are irrational—but they attribute irrationality to entirely different mechanisms. Mackay blames the crowd; de la Vega blames coordinated professionals. Bernstein and Fridson present these as complementary, but they are not: if markets move primarily because bear syndicates execute twelve-step manipulation programs targeting rational actors, then "madness of crowds" is the wrong diagnosis. The crowd is not mad; it is being systematically deceived.

**Three tensions run through the entire compiled text:**

*Tension 1: Documentation vs. Anecdote.* Mackay's work rests on a mixture of documented parliamentary records (South Sea bribery figures, Mississippi debt calculations) and unverifiable anecdotes (tulip-eating sailor, doctor confusing stock prices with pulse rates). The anecdotes do the rhetorical work; the documents do the evidentiary work. Garber's scholarship shows that when the anecdotes are subjected to primary-source verification, they often dissolve. The valid core of Mackay's argument—that asset price cycles occur, that government officials can be corrupted, that there is no reliable method of distinguishing genuine from fictitious value in real time—survives without the anecdotes and is stronger without them.

*Tension 2: Rationality's scope.* De la Vega insists the market is comprehensible to patient observers ("he who makes it his business to watch these things conscientiously... will hit upon the right thing innumerable times"). He also insists no one can reliably predict it ("no perspicacity will divine the game, and no science is sufficient here"). These cannot both be true as stated. The reconciliation, implicit in the dialogues, is that the market is locally predictable (momentum, manipulation cycles, information asymmetry) but globally unpredictable (the crash of 1688 came from cargo value information that no outsider could have obtained). This is a sophisticated epistemological position that the dialogue form prevents de la Vega from stating directly.

*Tension 3: The rehabilitation problem.* Both Bernstein's foreword and Fridson's introduction deploy Mackay's framework to interpret modern events (1987 crash, Nikkei collapse, MMM scheme). This deployment treats "crowd madness" as explanatorily sufficient. But the Garber/Neal scholarship suggests that even Mackay's paradigm cases—tulipomania and the South Sea Bubble—are partially explained by rational responses to real information (bulb genetics, legitimate debt conversion) that later became distorted by structural incentives (futures market asymmetries, regulatory capture). If the paradigm cases are more complex than claimed, the framework loses its explanatory force precisely when it is most needed: distinguishing genuine delusion from rational actors responding to distorted signals.

**The volume's most proven claims:**
- Speculative cycles occur, have occurred throughout recorded financial history, and involve similar behavioral patterns across different asset classes and eras
- Coordinated manipulation of prices by professional syndicates is both ancient and sophisticated, predating modern financial regulation by centuries
- Information asymmetry between insiders and public investors is structural, not incidental, to early modern equity markets—and de la Vega's twelve bear tactics document this in forensic detail
- Governments reliably exploit successful financial instruments for debt monetization, and this exploitation reliably destroys the instruments

**The volume's most significant unproven claims:**
- That "crowds go mad"—the mass psychology hypothesis requires more than anecdotal evidence of people speculating. The mechanistic account of how individual rational responses aggregate into collective irrationality is absent from Mackay and only partially addressed by de la Vega
- That tulipomania was irrational—Garber's scholarship makes this genuinely contested
- That the behavioral patterns documented are timeless rather than contingent on specific institutional conditions (the appeal to Frederick, duck-a-ton shares, monthly settlement mechanics) that no longer exist

## PART 2: LITERARY REVIEW ESSAY

---

# The Permanent Scandal of the Unverifiable Sailor

Here is the problem with the most famous anecdote in three centuries of financial writing: a Dutch sailor, mistaking a rare Semper Augustus tulip bulb for an onion, eats it for breakfast alongside his red herring, and is subsequently imprisoned for destroying property worth 3,000 florins. Charles Mackay reports this story in 1841 as documentary evidence of Holland's 1636 tulip mania. Peter Garber's 1989 archival research suggests the incident, if it happened at all, probably occurred after the speculative period it is meant to illustrate.

The sailor may be fictional. The lesson may be real. These are not the same claim, and the failure to distinguish them is the intellectual scandal at the heart of *Extraordinary Popular Delusions and the Madness of Crowds*—the most cited, least verified foundational text in behavioral finance.

This matters not as antiquarian pedantry. It matters because the book is regularly invoked to explain real market events—the 1987 crash, the Nikkei collapse, the crypto cycles of the 2020s. When economists and journalists reach for "crowd madness" as an explanatory framework, they are reaching, however distantly, for Mackay's tulip-eating sailor. If the sailor is fiction, the framework built on him needs reconstruction from different materials.

---

What survives the demolition is more interesting than what doesn't.

The three financial episodes Mackay documents—John Law's Mississippi Scheme, the South Sea Bubble, and Dutch tulipomania—share a structural feature that requires no anecdotes to establish: in each case, an asset whose underlying value was genuinely uncertain attracted investment in excess of what any plausible value calculation could justify, and collapsed when the information gap closed. This is a theorem, not a narrative. It does not require a sailor.

The South Sea case is particularly instructive. The parliamentary inquiry that followed the collapse documented bribery with the specificity of a modern forensic audit: £50,000 in fictitious stock to the Earl of Sunderland, £794,451 in documented transactions to Chancellor Aislabie, £30,000 to Secretary Craggs. The committee found that 1,259,325 pounds of stock was distributed to officials to facilitate the legislation. This is not crowd madness. This is organized fraud at the highest levels of government, enabled by the fact that the underlying value of South American trade rights was genuinely unknowable—Spain had no intention of opening her colonies to British merchants, but proving this required time.

The Mississippi Scheme is structurally similar. The national debt stood at 3,000 million livres against annual revenue of 145 million—an arithmetic impossibility that Law's system temporarily obscured rather than resolved. When the Regent issued 1,000 million livres in new notes (ten times Law's previous maximum) in a single decision, the system crossed from viable-if-fragile to mathematically doomed. The crowd didn't go mad. The crowd responded rationally to an insolvent government debasing a currency in real time. The madness was the policy, not the people.

---

De la Vega understood this in 1688, writing in Amsterdam's coffee houses about the Dutch East India Company's shares while Mackay was still 150 years away from being born. *Confusión de Confusiones* is the stranger, richer, and more useful half of this compiled volume—a book written as a practical manual for stock speculators that accidentally became the first systematic analysis of financial market microstructure.

What de la Vega documents in his four dialogues is not crowd madness but something more disturbing: a market in which sophisticated professionals possess twelve documented tactics for manufacturing the appearance of crowd madness in order to move prices against less informed participants. The bear syndicate lends money to bulls against their stock, then sells that stock to force the bulls to buy again at higher prices. The bears fabricate letters with alarming news and drop them where they will be found by credulous investors. The bears spread war rumors while selling government bonds to create a cross-market panic signal. These are not the behaviors of a crowd succumbing to mass hysteria. These are the behaviors of professionals creating mass hysteria as a profit strategy.

The implication is uncomfortable: what Mackay read as evidence of human irrationality was, at least partly, manufactured irrationality—engineered by market insiders who understood psychology precisely enough to exploit it systematically.

De la Vega was neither neutral nor objective about this. He lost and made five fortunes through speculation, by his own account. His literary ambitions—the baroque Estilo Culto style, the mythological digressions, the theatrical dialogue format—sometimes obscure the forensic detail underneath. But when the ornament clears, what remains is extraordinary: an inside account of a sophisticated financial market operating at full capacity within 86 years of the invention of transferable equity shares. By 1688, Amsterdam traders had developed puts, calls, forward contracts, margin lending, synthetic fractional shares, clearing agents, and twelve-step price manipulation playbooks. They had also developed a legal mechanism—the "appeal to Frederick"—that allowed buyers in declining markets to repudiate contracts unilaterally, creating a structural one-sidedness that de la Vega describes with exasperated clarity: the system was designed to be exploited.

---

The compiled volume's greatest weakness is also its most revealing feature: the repeated framing of market cycles as evidence of human irrationality, against textual evidence that consistently points toward structural incentives.

The tulip-onion sailor is fictional or misdated. But the verifiable tulipomania records show something equally interesting: informal futures markets in common tulip bulbs collapsed in 1637 when buyers discovered they could legally repudiate contracts under Dutch law—the same legal asymmetry de la Vega would document 51 years later for VOC shares. The "appeal to Frederick" equivalent for tulip futures meant that contracts were enforceable only if sellers chose to enforce them, and once confidence eroded, rational buyers stopped honoring commitments they had a legal right to repudiate. This is not crowd madness. This is rational actors exploiting structural asymmetry until the market ceases to function.

Mackay's thesis—that humans periodically go insane in herds—is not wrong. It is incomplete in ways that matter. The crowd does sometimes go mad. But the crowd is also sometimes pushed.

---

The book that would supersede both Mackay and de la Vega has not been written yet. It would need to show, with primary-source evidence and mechanical specificity, how information asymmetry, legal asymmetry, and coordinated professional manipulation interact with genuine popular credulity to produce the cycles both authors document. It would need to distinguish the cases where collective irrationality was primary (if such cases exist in the strong form Mackay claims) from the cases where collective irrationality was manufactured or amplified by structural incentives.

What we have instead is Mackay's morality tale—vivid, occasionally fabricated, structurally important—and de la Vega's technical manual written in the voice of a baroque poet who lost everything five times and cannot quite bring himself to stop recommending that you speculate anyway.

That combination, across three and a half centuries, is the actual enduring document: not proof that crowds go mad, but proof that financial markets produce, in every era, both the victims who need a theory of crowd madness to explain their losses and the professionals who benefited from manufacturing the appearance of it. Mackay gave the victims their theory. De la Vega gave the professionals their manual. The same book sells to both.
