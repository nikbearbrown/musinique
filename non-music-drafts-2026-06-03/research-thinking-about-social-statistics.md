### CHAPTER 6: Thinking About Social Statistics

**Core Claim:** The correct response to statistical unreliability is neither naïve acceptance nor cynical rejection but critical analysis: evaluating each statistic's definitions, measurements, and sampling choices, assessing whether comparisons are appropriate, and maintaining proportionate skepticism calibrated to the quality of the underlying methods.

**Supporting Evidence:**
- Three orientations toward statistics: the *awestruck* (numbers are magical, beyond question); the *naïve* (numbers are probably accurate, accept them); the *cynical* (numbers are always manipulated, reject them). Best argues all three are epistemically dysfunctional.
- The critical orientation asks: Who created this statistic and why? How was it measured? What was sampled? Is the comparison appropriate? These questions do not require technical sophistication—only the habit of asking.
- Best frames statistics as fetishes: objects to which we attribute magical properties (accuracy, objectivity, truth) that they do not inherently possess.

**Logical Method:** Epistemological prescription—the chapter argues for an approach to statistical evidence rather than demonstrating an empirical claim.

**Logical Gaps:**
- The three-category typology (awestruck/naïve/cynical/critical) is a useful heuristic but slightly overpopulated. Best's distinction between the awestruck and the naïve is not crisp—both accept numbers uncritically; the difference seems to be degree of mathematical sophistication, not orientation toward skepticism. The critical approach is more carefully delineated.
- The chapter does not address what happens when critical analysis is applied to a statistic and the result is "this statistic is likely somewhat biased, but I cannot determine in which direction or by how much." This is the most common outcome of careful statistical analysis—and Best's prescriptions do not equip readers to act under this kind of residual uncertainty.

**Methodological Soundness:** The chapter is the book's weakest analytically—it is primarily normative prescription rather than empirical argument. The fetish metaphor is evocative but does not advance the analysis beyond what earlier chapters established. The chapter functions as a conclusion rather than a contribution.
