### CHAPTER 3: Mutant Statistics — Methods for Mangling Numbers

**Core Claim:** Even accurate statistics can be corrupted in transmission through four mechanisms: (1) inappropriate generalization from flawed definitions, measurements, or samples; (2) transformation—changing a number's meaning while preserving the number; (3) confusion about complex statistical concepts; and (4) compound error—using a bad statistic as the foundation for calculating a new statistic.

**Supporting Evidence:**

*Transformation (the book's richest case):*
- 150,000 anorexic women became 150,000 deaths from anorexia per year—a figure that would require anorexia to kill every young woman aged 15-44 in the United States. The mutation was so obvious that basic numeracy should have caught it; it survived because advocates found the higher figure useful.
- A psychologist's estimate that 6% of priests in treatment were sexually preoccupied with minors was transformed, through four separate steps, into "6% of all priests have had sex with children"—expanding the population (treatment patients → all priests), the behavior (attraction → contact), and the target (adolescents → children)

*Confusion (compound statistical misinterpretation):*
- Workforce 2000's finding that white males would represent roughly 15% of net additions to the workforce was transformed by the report's own executive summary into "only 15% of new workers will be white males"—conflating net additions (a small marginal group) with all new entrants (a much larger group). The press then extrapolated further: "by 2000, 65% of the total workforce will be women"—a claim with no relationship to the original data.
- Cardiac catheterization study: a finding that blacks and women had odds ratios of 0.6 for referral was reported as "blacks and women are 40% less likely to receive cardiac testing"—conflating odds ratios with risk ratios, a technically distinct but easily conflated measure. The correct statement was approximately "7% less likely."

*Compound error:*
- Claim that one-third of teen suicides involve gay or lesbian adolescents is built from: (1) the Kinsey 10% homosexuality estimate, itself based on a non-random sample; (2) a "2-3x higher suicide rate" estimate for homosexuals, based on research with its own methodological problems; multiplied together and rounded up, these produce 30-33%. If the underlying estimates are revised down even moderately (3% homosexual prevalence, 2x suicide rate), the figure falls below 6%.

**Logical Method:** Process tracing—Best follows the chain of transmission for specific statistics, identifying the precise point at which meaning changes.

**Logical Gaps:**
- The four mechanisms (generalization, transformation, confusion, compound error) are not exhaustive and the boundaries between them are sometimes arbitrary. The anorexia case is called "transformation" but involves both confusion (misunderstanding prevalence versus mortality) and generalization (applying a single-year mortality figure to an annual rate).
- Best is primarily interested in errors that inflate numbers. He acknowledges that downward distortions also occur (officials minimize counts to show competence) but does not provide equivalent examples of compound errors that produce underestimates. The book's systematic focus on overestimates may itself reflect a selection bias in the examples Best found compelling.

**Methodological Soundness:** The process-tracing approach is well-suited to demonstrating how specific statistics mutate. The compound error section is the chapter's most analytically valuable contribution—it shows how a chain of questionable estimates can produce a conclusion that is orders of magnitude from reality while each individual step seems defensible.
