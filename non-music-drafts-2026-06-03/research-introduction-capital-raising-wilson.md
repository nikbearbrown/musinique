### SECTION 1: Introduction
**Core Claim:** Capital raising is a learnable system, not a talent, and the primary failure mode is lack of systematic process rather than inferior product quality or performance.

**Supporting Evidence:**
- Author biography: $250M raised as a third-party marketer; Family Office Club with 1,000+ registered family offices managing over $1 trillion in assets; $4B in assets under advisory
- Survey of 500+ capital raisers: #1 issue is "investor relationships or investor lead generation" — getting meetings, not closing them
- Nine common myths enumerated, including "performance markets itself," "it's a numbers game," and "a 3-month capital raising goal is realistic"

**Logical Method:** Credentialed authority + myth-busting + survey data. The introduction establishes Wilson as practitioner-expert and frames the book as corrective to industry misconceptions.

**Logical Gaps:**
- The $250M raised figure is presented without timeline, context, or the base of assets marketed. $250M raised over 10 years at a large third-party marketing firm is a different credential than raising the same amount as a solo operator in 12 months. The credential is real but incompletely specified.
- The $4B "assets under advisory" claim appears throughout the book and grows across sections (from $4B to "over $4B" to "advising on $4B+ worth of assets for centimillionaires and billionaires"). This is assets under *advisory* — a buy-side deal origination role — not assets under management. The distinction matters for evaluating the credential but is never clarified.
- The 500+ survey respondents are described as "active capital raisers" who attended Wilson's events. This is a self-selected population of people who paid to attend capital raising training, which almost certainly overrepresents frustrated, early-stage capital raisers. The finding that their top challenge is "getting in the door" tells us about conference attendees, not the capital raising population broadly.
- "Performance does NOT market itself" is asserted as universal truth. The evidence cited is that even Blackstone's founders had to pitch. This is true but establishes only that effort is necessary, not that performance is irrelevant. For institutional investors with allocation mandates, track record is frequently the primary (sometimes sufficient) gating criterion. The claim overstates the case.

**Methodological Soundness:** The introduction functions as a combination of credentialing narrative and framing document. The survey data is real but methodologically limited. The myths section is accurate in identifying common errors but presents them as more universal than they are — some (e.g., "performance markets itself") are specific failure modes for early-stage managers with no track record and do not apply uniformly across asset classes or manager tenure.
